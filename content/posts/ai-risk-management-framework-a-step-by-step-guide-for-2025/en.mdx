# AI risk management framework: a step-by-step guide

AI can automate decisions, detect fraud, and cut manual effort in finance, HR, and procurement. When it goes wrong, the consequences are serious: bias in hiring, security breaches, regulatory fines, financial losses. In 2023, an AI-driven trading algorithm lost $440 million in minutes because nobody was monitoring it properly. The system made bad trades, and by the time humans stepped in, it was too late.

AI risk management is how you prevent that. A structured framework identifies risks before deployment, controls them during operation, and defines what happens when something goes wrong.

## What an AI risk management framework covers

A proper framework applies across the full model lifecycle from design through deployment and into daily operation.

| Framework component | What it covers | Key actions |
|---|---|---|
| **Governance structure** | Oversight bodies and decision rights | Set up AI ethics committee; assign risk owners |
| **Risk identification** | Technical, ethical, and regulatory risks | Risk workshops, threat modeling, scenario analysis |
| **Compliance alignment** | Laws: EU AI Act, GDPR, ISO 42001 | Map use cases to regulatory checklists |
| **Bias and fairness** | Discrimination in AI outcomes | Bias testing, fairness audits, demographic impact |
| **Explainability** | AI decisions understandable to users | Explainable AI methods, model documentation |
| **Security and privacy** | Model and data protection | Encrypt training data; adversarial testing |
| **Continuous monitoring** | Model behaviour post-deployment | KPIs, anomaly detection, human review loops |
| **Incident response** | AI malfunctions and ethical breaches | Escalation paths, AI-specific recovery plans |
| **Stakeholder communication** | Internal and external transparency | Model cards, risk disclosures, stakeholder updates |

Where most frameworks fail is isolation. They sit outside the project flow. AI governance needs to be inside the operating model: alongside audit and change control. Otherwise, issues surface late when they're harder and more expensive to fix.

## Step 1: identify your AI risks

Start with a risk inventory. For each AI system, answer: what data does it process, what decisions does it influence, and what's the worst-case outcome if it gets something wrong?

Common risk categories:

- **Bias and fairness.** Models trained on historical data inherit its biases. If five years of hiring decisions skewed toward certain profiles, the model will replicate that. Amazon scrapped its AI hiring tool after discovering it systematically downgraded resumes containing the word "women's." That wasn't a glitch: it was predictable. A basic bias audit before deployment would have caught it.
- **Security.** AI systems handling financial transactions, HR records, or procurement contracts are targets. Adversarial attacks, data poisoning, and model theft are real attack vectors. If sensitive data flows into an AI model without encryption, that's an exposure.
- **Compliance.** GDPR, the EU AI Act, ISO 42001, HIPAA: depending on your industry and geography, multiple frameworks apply. Non-compliance isn't just a legal risk; regulators are increasingly demanding to see the methodology behind AI decisions, not just the outputs.
- **Operational.** Model drift is the quiet one. A model trained in Q1 may behave differently by Q3 as data patterns shift. Nobody notices until a customer complains or an audit surfaces the problem.

## Step 2: assess and categorise by impact

Not all risks are equal. Prioritise by the combination of likelihood and business impact.

A simple heat map approach works for most organisations:

- **Critical (high likelihood, high impact):** AI influencing financial approvals, employment decisions, or access control: these require the most rigorous controls, human oversight requirements, and documentation.
- **Moderate:** AI supporting recommendations with human final approval: lighter controls, but audit logging still required.
- **Low:** Internal tools with limited scope and limited impact on people: basic monitoring, periodic review.

The EU AI Act's risk classification is useful here. Use cases in hiring, credit, and access control are explicitly "high-risk" and have specific legal requirements. Map your AI systems to these tiers.

## Step 3: mitigation strategies

**Bias.** Use diverse training data. Conduct regular bias audits across demographic groups. Apply fairness-aware algorithms. Keep humans in the loop for decisions that affect individuals: hiring, lending, access. Review acceptance rates across groups and investigate anomalies.

**Security.** Encrypt training data and model outputs. Implement access controls so only authorised people can modify models. Conduct adversarial testing to check whether inputs can manipulate model behaviour. Monitor for unusual access patterns.

**Operational continuity.** Define what "normal" model behaviour looks like for each system, then set anomaly detection thresholds. When a metric deviates (fraud detection blocking rate, hiring acceptance ratio, supplier approval pattern), you want an alert, not a month-end surprise.

## Step 4: governance and compliance

A bank was fined $30 million after its AI discriminated against minority applicants in mortgage decisions. Governance wasn't in place. No one had defined who was responsible for the model's behaviour or what review process applied before decisions took effect.

Governance for AI needs:

- **Named ownership.** For each model, one person answers for its outputs. A committee without a chair is no one.
- **Audit trails.** Every AI-influenced decision should have a traceable record: what data was used, what was the output, when was it reviewed.
- **Regulatory alignment.** Check your AI systems against ISO 42001, NIST AI RMF, and GDPR (or relevant regional equivalents). This isn't a one-time exercise: regulations update, and AI systems need to be re-checked when they do.

## Step 5: continuous monitoring and incident response

Monitoring tools that are widely used in enterprise environments:

| Tool | Primary focus |
|---|---|
| **Fiddler AI** | Bias detection, explainability, real-time model monitoring |
| **IBM Watson OpenScale** | Decision explanations, bias detection, compliance |
| **WhyLabs** | Data drift, model health, anomaly detection |
| **Microsoft Responsible AI Dashboard** | Fairness, error analysis, data imbalance |
| **Arthur AI** | Fairness, drift, performance degradation |
| **Amazon SageMaker Clarify** | Bias detection in training and inference |

When something goes wrong, teams that improvise make it worse. Define the escalation path before the incident: who gets the call, what authority do they have to pause or roll back the model, what do you tell customers if their data was affected.

I've watched teams scramble when AI failures caught them off guard. No one knew who to alert, how to stop the damage, or what to tell customers. By the time they figured it out, the problem had escalated. A written incident response plan (tested, not just filed) is the difference.

<details>
<summary>What is an AI risk management framework?</summary>

A structured process for identifying, assessing, and managing the risks that arise when AI systems influence business decisions. It covers the full model lifecycle from design through deployment and into daily operation, and includes controls for bias, security, compliance, and operational continuity.

In SAP environments, it applies specifically to models used in procurement (supplier scoring), HR (candidate screening), finance (invoice approvals), and supply chain (demand forecasting). These aren't just algorithms: they affect people, money, and compliance.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the NIST AI Risk Management Framework?</summary>

The NIST AI RMF is a voluntary framework from the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, structured around four functions:

- **Govern**: policies, risk controls, ethical standards
- **Map**: identify potential risks and their impact on users
- **Measure**: evaluate using bias, explainability, and security metrics
- **Manage**: implement mitigations and continuous monitoring

It's widely used in finance, healthcare, and defence, and is increasingly referenced in enterprise AI procurement requirements even when not legally mandatory.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the biggest risks in enterprise AI?</summary>

Five categories matter most:

1. **Bias and discrimination**: models trained on skewed historical data produce skewed outputs. Amazon's hiring tool is the clearest example.
2. **Security**: adversarial attacks, data poisoning, and model theft are active threats for AI handling financial or personal data.
3. **Compliance**: GDPR, EU AI Act, ISO 42001, HIPAA. Non-compliance can result in fines, operational restrictions, and reputational damage.
4. **Operational drift**: model accuracy degrades over time as data patterns change. Without monitoring, this goes unnoticed.
5. **Lack of explainability**: AI that cannot explain its decisions is indefensible to regulators, customers, and internal audit.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you prevent AI bias before deployment?</summary>

Three steps that actually work:

1. Audit the training data for demographic skew before training. If the data already reflects historical bias, the model will too.
2. Run fairness tests across demographic groups after training: check acceptance rates, rejection rates, and approval patterns by group.
3. Apply fairness-aware algorithms that penalise discriminatory patterns during model training, not just after the fact.

Human oversight remains essential for high-stakes decisions. AI in hiring, lending, or access control should support decisions, not make them unilaterally.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What should an AI incident response plan cover?</summary>

Four things, defined before the incident:

1. **Escalation path**: who gets notified first, who has authority to pause or roll back the model, who communicates to customers.
2. **Containment steps**: how to isolate the affected system or decision flow without disrupting downstream processes.
3. **Customer communication**: what you tell affected users and when, especially if personal data or adverse decisions are involved.
4. **Post-incident review**: root cause analysis, model retraining or rollback decision, updated controls to prevent recurrence.

Plans that exist only in someone's head don't work under pressure. Test the plan with a tabletop exercise annually.

</details>
