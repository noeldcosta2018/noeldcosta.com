# SAP business case template: get approval without delay

Getting an SAP project approved can drag on for weeks or months even when the idea is solid. The problem is usually not the technology. It's the way the case is presented.

The people who sign off on budgets look for clarity, numbers, and business outcomes they can explain to others. Technical depth has its place, but not in the opening argument.

I remember a S/4HANA migration where the first pitch failed. The proposal was packed with system architecture diagrams. It had almost nothing on operational gains. We reworked it: the opening focused on cutting shipping delays, reducing inventory costs, and how the team would deliver. The same decision-makers who passed on version one approved version two.

## The executive summary is the only page most people read

Keep it to one page. Executives might only read this section, so every sentence has to earn its place.

Start with the problem you're solving, using real numbers about what's not working. State the proposed solution in one or two sentences: no technical detail. Then: key benefits with specific numbers, a simple timeline showing major milestones, total investment required, and a brief mention of key risks with mitigations.

A CFO put the document down after the first paragraph once because it opened with "technical objects" and "Embedded HANA." We rewrote it to start with "$2.5 million in annual cost savings through inventory reduction" and "40% faster order processing." Same CFO read the whole page and approved the project the same week.

When you've finished writing the executive summary, ask someone outside the project team to read it. If they can explain the key points back to you, it works.

## Write for three audiences, not one

One version for everyone doesn't work. I watched a strong proposal die because it was written for the wrong audience.

**For the C-suite:** Give them numbers they can repeat in a board meeting without notes. "$2M annual savings with an 18-month payback" sticks. Show competitive impact, not just internal efficiency. Show oversight is in place. Don't hide risks; high-level executives respect candour over optimism.

**For IT teams:** Map the plan against existing system diagrams. Explain integration scope clearly. Specify what post-go-live support will be needed: most business cases skip this. Share past integration failures from similar projects: it proves you know the traps.

**For business users:** Show a "week in the life" comparison, not scenarios, but actual task-by-task differences. Be direct on training time. Avoid terminology without context. A small extra click, repeated thousands of times a week, becomes a real problem.

A CFO rejected a business case mid-meeting because it answered none of her questions. We rebuilt it using three distinct sections, tied each to the right audience, and anchored everything with measurable KPIs. The technical plan never changed. Only the way it was told did.

## What goes into the structure

| Section | What it needs to contain |
|---|---|
| **Executive summary** | Problem statement, proposed solution, key benefits, investment total, timeline overview, key risks |
| **Current situation** | Business challenges, pain points, financial impact of current state, competitive context |
| **Proposed solution** | Solution scope, modules, deployment approach, integration points |
| **Financial case** | ROI, payback period, NPV, cost categories, benefit quantification |
| **Implementation plan** | Timeline by phase, team structure, milestones, dependencies |
| **Risk register** | Specific risks (not "data issues"), mitigations, ownership |
| **Governance model** | Steering committee structure, change control process, escalation paths |

## Financial parameters that get scrutinised

### Cost categories to include

Most business cases fail because costs are incomplete. Include all of these:

- SAP software licensing or subscription costs
- Implementation partner fees
- Infrastructure upgrades (if applicable)
- Internal resource costs, often omitted entirely
- Training and change management
- Data migration and cleansing
- **Annual maintenance fees: typically 20–22% of licence cost, recurring every year**

That last one is what finance directors look for first. If it's missing, credibility drops immediately.

### Metrics executives use to evaluate

**ROI:** Net benefits divided by total investment, expressed as a percentage.

Example: $3.5M in five-year benefits against $2M in costs = 75% ROI, but note that five-year projections require realistic benefit quantification. I've seen initial ROI projections of 30% challenged and revised to 18%. The CFO approved the revised case: "I'd rather have an honest 18% than a fictional 30%."

**Payback period:** Initial investment divided by annual cash flow. For a $1.2M project generating $400K per year, that's a three-year payback.

**NPV:** Discounts future cash flows to present value. If decision-makers are asking for NPV, your finance team needs to be in the room.

### Quantifying benefits

Avoid claiming benefits in general terms. Be specific:

- *Inventory reduction:* if current inventory is $10M, a 15% reduction at 20% carrying cost = $300K annual savings.
- *Process efficiency:* if a 45-minute process runs 200 times per day and drops to 15 minutes, at $30/hour that's $750K annual savings.

Benefits that are impossible to quantify can still be listed, but separate them clearly from the measurable ones. Mixing them together undermines both.

## Common mistakes that kill business cases

**Skipping the cost of doing nothing.** Sometimes that number is higher than the SAP investment. If the current system creates $500K per year in rework, that belongs in the case.

**Omitting disruption costs.** Training time, downtime, and a slower first month after go-live affect the return just as much as the licence price.

**Vague risk statements.** "Data issues" is not a risk statement. "Master data mismatch causing invoice rejections in the first 30 days" is. Specific risks with specific mitigations show the proposal is grounded in reality.

**Treating timeline as decoration.** A timeline that shows go-live as a single date without showing where delays typically start doesn't help executives understand what they're approving. Usually, the delays come in the handoffs: between requirements and configuration, between testing and UAT sign-off, between training completion and go-live readiness.

I worked with a manufacturing team that spent six months getting approval. They revised their case four times, each round, different stakeholders raised concerns that should have been addressed from the start. The problem was not the project. It was that every version was written for a different audience without addressing the others.

## Size-specific considerations

What works for a global enterprise won't work for a 200-person company. The financial thresholds, governance expectations, and risk tolerance are different.

**Small businesses** (under 200 employees): keep the case to 8–10 pages. Decision-makers usually want to see three things: what does it cost, what does it replace, and what does go-live look like for us specifically.

**Mid-market** (200–1,000 employees): more attention to total cost of ownership, integration complexity, and change management. ROI needs to be supported by credible, conservative assumptions.

**Enterprises:** full financial modelling expected. NPV, risk-adjusted returns, detailed governance. The steering committee structure is scrutinised because executives have seen too many enterprise projects where governance was nominal.

<summary>Why do SAP business cases get rejected?</summary>

Most rejections come down to one of four causes: the benefits are vague or unverifiable, the costs are incomplete (especially recurring costs), risks are glossed over, or the document is written for one audience when multiple stakeholders need to be convinced.

A business case that gets approved is not the most technically detailed one. It's the one that speaks the language of each person in the room, honestly.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What financial metrics should an SAP business case include?</summary>

At minimum: ROI (net benefits divided by total investment), payback period, and a breakdown of costs across initial investment and recurring annual costs. For larger projects or enterprise decisions, NPV using a realistic discount rate adds credibility.

The most important thing is conservative assumptions. Benefit projections that look inflated get challenged. A realistic 18% ROI closes faster than an optimistic 35%.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long should an SAP business case be?</summary>

The executive summary: one page. The full document: 15–25 pages for mid-market, up to 40 for enterprise with multiple stakeholder audiences. Anything longer should be appendices, not main text.

The test is whether the executive sponsor can explain the key points to the board without referring back to the document. If the case requires an hour to brief from, it's too long.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the cost of doing nothing in an SAP business case?</summary>

The cost of doing nothing is what the business loses by staying with the current system. That includes the cost of manual workarounds, reporting delays, data reconciliation labour, system maintenance on outdated infrastructure, and the opportunity cost of not having reliable data for decisions.

It's often higher than people expect. A current system generating $500K per year in rework and workarounds changes the conversation around a $1.5M SAP investment.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the biggest mistakes in an SAP business case?</summary>

Omitting annual maintenance fees (typically 20–22% of licence cost). Missing internal resource costs. The time your own team spends on the project is a real cost. Vague risk statements that don't name specific failure modes. Benefits that can't be measured, and writing for one audience when the approvers span finance, IT, and operations.

The manufacturing team that revised their case four times over six months made all of these mistakes, in different combinations, each time.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you show ROI for an SAP implementation?</summary>

Start by quantifying both sides honestly. Costs should be complete: software, implementation, internal time, training, data migration, and recurring maintenance. Benefits should be tied to specific, measurable outcomes with realistic assumptions.

Then calculate ROI as net benefits divided by total investment. If you're showing a five-year model, use conservative year-one benefit estimates: early post-go-live performance is usually lower than steady-state. Showing a ramp-up in the model is more credible than claiming full benefits from month one.

</details>
