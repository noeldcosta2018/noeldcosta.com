Getting an SAP project approved can drag on for weeks or months even when the idea is solid. The problem is usually not the technology. It is the way the case is presented. Across 21 SAP programmes that I have led, rescued, or watched go sideways, the business cases that get approved fast share three properties: they speak the language of each person in the room, they show conservative numbers honestly, and they answer the cost of doing nothing as carefully as the cost of doing the project.

The people who sign off on budgets look for clarity, numbers, and business outcomes they can explain to others. Technical depth has its place, but not in the opening argument.

On a GCC manufacturing programme in 2023, I worked on an S/4HANA migration where the first pitch failed. The proposal was packed with system architecture diagrams. It had almost nothing on operational gains. We reworked it. The opening focused on cutting shipping delays, reducing inventory costs, and how the team would deliver. The same decision-makers who passed on version one approved version two.

## The executive summary is the only page most people read

Keep it to one page. Executives might only read this section, so every sentence has to earn its place.

Start with the problem you are solving, using real numbers about what is not working. State the proposed solution in one or two sentences with no technical detail. Then: key benefits with specific numbers, a simple timeline showing major milestones, and total investment required. Close with a brief mention of key risks with mitigations.

On the same GCC manufacturing programme in 2023, the CFO put the document down after the first paragraph because it opened with "technical objects" and "Embedded HANA". We rewrote it to start with "$2.5 million in annual cost savings through inventory reduction" and "40% faster order processing". Same CFO read the whole page and approved the project the same week.

When you have finished writing the executive summary, ask someone outside the project team to read it. If they can explain the key points back to you, it works.

## Write for three audiences, not one

One version for everyone does not work. I have watched a strong proposal die because it was written for the wrong audience.

### For the C-suite

Give them numbers they can repeat in a board meeting without notes. "$2M annual savings with an 18-month payback" sticks. Show competitive impact, not just internal efficiency. Show oversight is in place. Do not hide risks. High-level executives respect candour over optimism.

### For IT teams

Map the plan against existing system diagrams. Explain integration scope clearly. Specify what post-go-live support will be needed; most business cases skip this. Share past integration failures from similar projects; it proves you know the traps.

### For business users

Show a "week in the life" comparison, not scenarios, but actual task-by-task differences. Be direct on training time. Avoid terminology without context. A small extra click, repeated thousands of times a week, becomes a real problem.

On a European retail programme in 2024, the CFO rejected a business case mid-meeting because it answered none of her questions. We rebuilt it using three distinct sections, tied each to the right audience, and anchored everything with measurable KPIs. The technical plan never changed. Only the way it was told did.

## What goes into the structure

| Section | What it needs to contain |
|---|---|
| **Executive summary** | Problem statement, proposed solution, key benefits, investment total, timeline overview, key risks |
| **Current situation** | Business challenges, pain points, financial impact of current state, competitive context |
| **Proposed solution** | Solution scope, modules, deployment approach, cloud edition decision (RISE / GROW / on-premise), integration points |
| **Financial case** | ROI, payback period, NPV, cost categories, benefit quantification |
| **Implementation plan** | Timeline by phase, team structure, milestones, dependencies |
| **Risk register** | Specific risks (not "data issues"), mitigations, ownership |
| **Governance model** | Steering committee structure, change control process, escalation paths, clean-core review forum for RISE |

## Financial parameters that get scrutinised

### Cost categories to include

Most business cases fail because costs are incomplete. Include all of these:

1. SAP software licensing OR subscription costs (RISE / GROW are subscription; on-premise is licence + maintenance)
2. Implementation partner fees
3. Infrastructure upgrades (relevant for on-premise; SAP-managed under RISE)
4. Internal resource costs, often omitted entirely
5. Training and change management
6. Data migration and cleansing
7. **Annual maintenance fees: typically 20 to 22% of licence cost for on-premise. RISE / GROW replace this with annual subscription, which has a different shape but similar cumulative impact over five years**
8. Hypercare and post-go-live support costs

That seventh item is what finance directors look for first on traditional on-premise cases. If it is missing, credibility drops immediately. For RISE / GROW subscription cases, the equivalent is the multi-year subscription commitment; missing the year 2-5 view is the same kind of credibility miss.

### Metrics executives use to evaluate

**ROI.** Net benefits divided by total investment, expressed as a percentage. Example: $3.5M in five-year benefits against $2M in costs equals 75% ROI. Note that five-year projections require realistic benefit quantification.

On a European retail programme in 2024, I saw an initial ROI projection of 30% challenged and revised to 18%. The CFO approved the revised case: "I would rather have an honest 18% than a fictional 30%."

**Payback period.** Initial investment divided by annual cash flow. For a $1.2M project generating $400K per year, that is a three-year payback.

**NPV.** Discounts future cash flows to present value. If decision-makers are asking for NPV, your finance team needs to be in the room.

### Quantifying benefits

Avoid claiming benefits in general terms. Be specific:

- **Inventory reduction.** If current inventory is $10M, a 15% reduction at 20% carrying cost equals $300K annual savings.
- **Process efficiency.** If a 45-minute process runs 200 times per day and drops to 15 minutes, at $30 per hour that is $750K annual savings.

Benefits that are impossible to quantify can still be listed, but separate them clearly from the measurable ones. Mixing them together undermines both.

## How RISE, GROW, and AI change the business case in 2026

The on-premise business case template still works for the on-premise deployment model. For RISE with SAP and GROW with SAP programmes, three structural changes are material.

### Subscription replaces licence and maintenance

RISE and GROW are subscription bundles. Year-one cost is lower than on-premise because there is no upfront licence purchase. Total five-year cost depends on the subscription tier, the user count, and the SAP-managed infrastructure scope. The business case structure needs a multi-year subscription view rather than a one-time licence plus annual maintenance view.

The CFO conversation also shifts: cloud subscriptions are typically opex rather than capex. This is an advantage in some financial structures and a complication in others. Confirm with finance how the company prefers to treat ERP investment before writing the financial case.

### Clean-core reduces long-term customisation maintenance cost

Clean-core compliance (mandatory across SAP Cloud editions since 2024-2025) constrains customisation to SAP BTP extensions. The short-term cost is higher; BTP extensions are not free. The long-term cost is lower because customisations survive upgrades cleanly rather than requiring rebuild at each major version. A five-year business case should reflect this through reduced upgrade-cycle cost in years three and four.

For on-premise programmes where clean-core is recommended but not enforced, the case should still note that voluntary clean-core discipline produces the same long-term saving with the same short-term investment.

### AI capabilities are quantifiable benefits in 2026

The 2024-2025 AI rollout produced capabilities that have real efficiency value when paired with realistic adoption assumptions.

**Joule on Sales Cloud and other modules.** Productivity gains of 10 to 20% on workflow-heavy roles (sales operations, service case management, finance close) where the AI assistance is used consistently. Conservative business cases assume 5 to 10% gain because adoption is the constraint, not the technology.

**SAP Build Code for ABAP.** Reduces custom development effort by 20 to 40% on customisations that still require code. Affects the customisation cost line, not the licence line.

**AI-driven dashboards (Joule for Signavio, Power BI AI, SAP Analytics Cloud AI).** Reduce time-to-insight on operational data. Quantifiable when the operational decision being accelerated has a defined cost-of-delay.

What these AI capabilities do not justify: a blank-cheque "AI will transform our business" narrative. CFOs see that pitch weekly and discount it heavily. Tie every AI claim to a specific workflow, a specific user count, and a specific assumed adoption rate.

## Common mistakes that kill business cases

**Skipping the cost of doing nothing.** Sometimes that number is higher than the SAP investment. If the current system creates $500K per year in rework, that belongs in the case.

**Omitting disruption costs.** Training time, downtime, and a slower first month after go-live affect the return just as much as the licence price.

**Vague risk statements.** "Data issues" is not a risk statement. "Master data mismatch causing invoice rejections in the first 30 days" is. Specific risks with specific mitigations show the proposal is grounded in reality.

**Treating timeline as decoration.** A timeline that shows go-live as a single date without showing where delays typically start does not help executives understand what they are approving. Usually, the delays come in the handoffs: between requirements and configuration, between testing and UAT sign-off, between training completion and go-live readiness.

**Treating RISE pricing as opaque.** Some teams hide the subscription complexity behind a "we have a quote" reference. CFOs need to see the year-one, year-three, and year-five cost. Hiding the multi-year view damages credibility.

On a European manufacturing programme in 2023, a team spent six months getting approval. They revised their case four times. Each round, different stakeholders raised concerns that should have been addressed from the start. The problem was not the project. It was that every version was written for a different audience without addressing the others.

## Size-specific considerations

What works for a global enterprise will not work for a 200-person company. The financial thresholds, governance expectations, and risk tolerance are different.

**Small businesses (under 200 employees).** Keep the case to 8 to 10 pages. Decision-makers usually want to see three things: what does it cost, what does it replace, and what does go-live look like for us specifically. GROW with SAP is often the right answer at this scale.

**Mid-market (200 to 1,000 employees).** More attention to total cost of ownership, integration complexity, and change management. ROI needs to be supported by credible, conservative assumptions. GROW with SAP is viable for standard scope; RISE with SAP is viable for more complex scope.

**Enterprises.** Full financial modelling expected. NPV, risk-adjusted returns, detailed governance. The steering committee structure is scrutinised because executives have seen too many enterprise projects where governance was nominal. RISE with SAP is typically the cloud-first default.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Why do SAP business cases get rejected?</summary>

Most rejections come down to one of four causes: the benefits are vague or unverifiable, the costs are incomplete (especially recurring costs or multi-year subscription view), risks are glossed over, or the document is written for one audience when multiple stakeholders need to be convinced.

A business case that gets approved is not the most technically detailed one. It is the one that speaks the language of each person in the room, honestly.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What financial metrics should an SAP business case include?</summary>

At minimum: ROI (net benefits divided by total investment), payback period, and a breakdown of costs across initial investment and recurring annual costs. For larger projects or enterprise decisions, NPV using a realistic discount rate adds credibility. For RISE / GROW subscription cases, a multi-year subscription cost view (years 1, 3, 5) is required.

The most important thing is conservative assumptions. Benefit projections that look inflated get challenged. A realistic 18% ROI closes faster than an optimistic 35%.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does RISE with SAP change the business case structure?</summary>

Three structural changes follow from the RISE subscription model.

Subscription replaces licence plus maintenance. The financial case needs a multi-year subscription view rather than a one-time licence plus annual maintenance view. The opex / capex treatment also shifts; confirm with finance which structure the company prefers.

SAP-managed infrastructure removes infrastructure capex from the cost stack. This simplifies the financial case but introduces multi-year SLA commitments that need to be documented.

Clean-core compliance reduces long-term customisation maintenance cost. The short-term cost of BTP extensions is higher; the long-term cost of customisations surviving upgrades cleanly is lower. A five-year model should reflect this.

GROW with SAP follows the same subscription model at smaller scale, with more standardised scope.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long should an SAP business case be?</summary>

The executive summary: one page. The full document: 15 to 25 pages for mid-market, up to 40 for enterprise with multiple stakeholder audiences. Anything longer should be appendices, not main text.

The test is whether the executive sponsor can explain the key points to the board without referring back to the document. If the case requires an hour to brief from, it is too long.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the cost of doing nothing in an SAP business case?</summary>

The cost of doing nothing is what the business loses by staying with the current system. That includes the cost of manual workarounds, reporting delays, data reconciliation labour, system maintenance on outdated infrastructure, and the opportunity cost of not having reliable data for decisions.

It is often higher than people expect. A current system generating $500K per year in rework and workarounds changes the conversation around a $1.5M SAP investment.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the biggest mistakes in an SAP business case?</summary>

Omitting annual maintenance fees (typically 20-22% of licence cost for on-premise) or the multi-year subscription view for RISE / GROW. Missing internal resource costs; the time your own team spends on the project is a real cost. Vague risk statements that do not name specific failure modes. Benefits that cannot be measured. And writing for one audience when the approvers span finance, IT, and operations.

The European manufacturing team that revised their case four times over six months made all of these mistakes, in different combinations, each time.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you show ROI for an SAP implementation in 2026?</summary>

Start by quantifying both sides honestly. Costs should be complete: software (or subscription), implementation, internal time, training, data migration, recurring maintenance (or annual subscription), and hypercare. Benefits should be tied to specific, measurable outcomes with realistic assumptions.

Then calculate ROI as net benefits divided by total investment. If you are showing a five-year model, use conservative year-one benefit estimates; early post-go-live performance is usually lower than steady-state. Showing a ramp-up in the model is more credible than claiming full benefits from month one.

For 2026 cases, AI capabilities (Joule, SAP Build Code, AI dashboards) are legitimate benefit lines when tied to specific workflows and conservative adoption assumptions. Blank-cheque AI claims get discounted heavily; tied-to-workflow AI claims get credited.

</details>

The best SAP business case in 2026 is not the most elaborately modelled one. It is the smallest case that answers honestly for each audience in the room, that includes all the costs (including the multi-year subscription view for RISE / GROW), that quantifies AI value against realistic adoption assumptions rather than vendor pitch slides, and that puts the cost of doing nothing on the same page as the cost of doing the project. The case that gets approved is the one whose numbers survive the finance team's pencil. Build for that test.
