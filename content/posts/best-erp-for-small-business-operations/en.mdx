The best ERP for a small business is the one that fixes today's operational gaps at a cost you can sustain with the support you actually have. For most companies under a few hundred people that means a shortlist from SAP Business One, NetSuite, Microsoft Dynamics 365 Business Central, Acumatica and Odoo; SAP GROW comes in at the mid-sized end, and RISE with SAP only when there is serious legacy complexity to carry over.

This guide is for owners, finance directors and operations leads at growing companies. It covers when it is time for ERP, seven options with real examples, a decision table, and the selection mistakes that cost the most.

I have worked on ERP programmes across aviation, defence, consulting and small business, on Oracle, SAP, Microsoft Dynamics and others, and I have seen the same story repeatedly. A company starts small, everyone knows what is going on, and things run fine. Then the team grows and the processes stretch. People build their own versions of the same report. Inventory numbers depend on who ran the export. Customers wait on updates that need five emails.

Those are process breakdowns more than software problems. When systems don't talk to each other, people fill the gaps by hand, and the cost stays invisible until it isn't.

## When it is time to look at ERP seriously

Four signals I see consistently:

1. **Manual fixes become normal.** Spreadsheets work for a while. Then you hire people just to reconcile mismatched reports.
2. **Customers notice before you do.** Late shipments, wrong invoices, promised stock that isn't there. I remember a retailer that lost a long-term client after repeated delivery mistakes. The client forgave the first few, not the last one.
3. **Growth makes it worse.** Ten orders a day can be tracked by hand. Fifty can't. Stockouts and overstocks in the same month are system failures dressed up as people problems.
4. **Leaders can't trust the numbers.** Every department brings a different report to the same meeting and nobody agrees which is right. If running the system takes more energy than running the business, it is time.

## The options I evaluate with smaller clients

### SAP Business One

SAP's ERP for small companies: finance, sales, purchasing, inventory and light production in one system, sold and run through partners on-premise or partner-hosted cloud. Version 10.0 is in [mainstream maintenance until the end of 2028](https://support.sap.com/en/offerings-programs/support-small-medium-enterprises/business-one/maintenance.html). If you want SAP and you are a small business, this is usually where to start, not S/4HANA. My [SAP Business One price guide](/sap-business-one-price-guide/) covers what it costs.

### SAP GROW (S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition)

SAP [launched GROW with SAP in 2023](https://news.sap.com/africa/2023/04/grow-with-sap-brings-proven-cloud-erp-benefits-to-midsize-customers/) to bring its public cloud ERP to midsize companies, and now markets it as SAP GROW on SAP Cloud ERP. It runs preconfigured processes with two major releases a year, and only allows extensions through released interfaces. Total programme cost typically runs $2M to $6M end to end, with 6 to 9 months to go-live, so it belongs at the top end of this list.

One of my clients, a professional services firm, chose GROW because it needed structure and compliance quickly. Go-live was fast. A few months later, adjusting tax treatment across regions needed more partner help than expected: even what looked like a minor configuration change needed the partner. That is the trade-off with preconfigured systems.

**Good for:** greenfield setups that want standard SAP processes fast, with no legacy complexity to bring across, and a path to the rest of SAP (Ariba, SuccessFactors, Concur) later.

**Watch out for:** limited room for customisation. If your processes don't fit the standard model, you hit walls quickly.

### RISE with SAP (S/4HANA Cloud Private Edition)

SAP's package for companies moving existing SAP or complex legacy systems to the cloud, now marketed as SAP Cloud ERP Private. More configuration flexibility than GROW, with SAP running the infrastructure.

A client of mine in industrial equipment moved from ECC to RISE. It had years of transaction data, complex pricing and custom reporting. RISE gave it flexibility, but the project only worked because it brought in a strong partner early. Without that, the integration work would have taken twice as long. Mid-market RISE programmes typically cost $2M to $10M; enterprise programmes $20M to $80M+.

**Good for:** businesses with existing SAP or complex legacy systems that can't start greenfield.

**Watch out for:** for small teams and simple processes it is overkill. Don't choose it because the brochure sounds comprehensive.

### Oracle NetSuite

Cloud ERP with finance, CRM, inventory and e-commerce in one product. Oracle positions it for organisations of all sizes, and it is common in retail, e-commerce and fast-growing companies with several entities.

A company I advised in high-end consumer electronics used NetSuite to run its retail and wholesale channels. It worked well for visibility, but the company paid extra for several reports it had assumed were standard. That is the pattern: a solid base platform, with add-ons that add up.

**Good for:** multi-location businesses that want multi-entity reporting and integrated CRM without three separate tools.

**Watch out for:** price escalation. Get the total cost including add-ons before signing.

### Microsoft Dynamics 365 Business Central

Finance, inventory, projects and purchasing in the Microsoft ecosystem, with clean connections to Outlook, Excel and Teams. Microsoft ships [two major updates a year](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics365/business-central/dev-itpro/administration/update-rollout-timeline), in spring and autumn. Adoption is easier when your team already lives in Microsoft tools.

A 70-person consulting firm I worked with tried to roll it out in-house. Six months later it brought in an implementation partner to redesign workflows, and only then did it start working. It lost months assuming it would just plug in.

**Good for:** organisations standardised on Microsoft that want ERP without extra middleware.

**Watch out for:** limited built-in industry logic. The wrong partner almost always leads to frustration.

### Odoo

Modular, with an open-source community edition and a paid enterprise edition. Start with a few apps (CRM, invoicing) and add more over time. Popular with e-commerce brands, digital services firms and small manufacturers, and budget-friendly if you have development skills in-house.

A food subscription business I advised in Singapore built its whole customer service and delivery system on Odoo. It worked well until the main developer left. Then even small updates became a project. Odoo's risk is developer dependency: custom code is fragile, and upgrades can break it if they aren't managed carefully.

**Good for:** small businesses with tight budgets and in-house technical capability who value flexibility over stability.

**Watch out for:** significant custom logic with no plan for maintaining it when its author isn't available.

### Acumatica

Cloud ERP priced on resources used rather than per user, which helps when headcount fluctuates or grows fast. Strong in construction, field services, distribution and manufacturing.

A client of mine in construction materials adopted Acumatica for dispatch, inventory and cost tracking. Once configured it worked well, but the client almost abandoned it during onboarding: setup needed detailed planning, and it underestimated how much business input was required.

**Good for:** growing businesses that want unlimited users without re-licensing and real operational depth.

**Watch out for:** more configuration effort than entry-level cloud ERPs. Get experienced implementation support.

## A decision table to shortlist two

| If you are... | Shortlist | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A small company with simple operations that wants SAP | SAP Business One | Built for small companies; partner-delivered |
| A small or mid-sized Microsoft-centric business | Business Central | Fits existing tools; large partner network |
| Multi-entity, e-commerce or fast growth | NetSuite, Acumatica | Cloud, multi-entity reporting, room to grow |
| Tight budget with in-house developers | Odoo | Low entry cost if you can maintain it |
| Construction, field service or distribution | Acumatica, Business Central | Operational depth; Acumatica's pricing suits headcount swings |
| Mid-sized, greenfield, want SAP standard processes | SAP GROW | Preconfigured cloud ERP with SAP's wider portfolio |
| Existing SAP or complex legacy to migrate | RISE with SAP | Flexibility to bring complexity across |

Treat it as a starting point, then run scripted demos with the people who will use the system every day.

## Common mistakes in ERP selection

1. **Picking on brand.** Many businesses choose what a peer company chose and regret it. Enterprise SAP and Oracle products can overshoot a business under 50 people or without IT support.
2. **Specifying for a hypothetical future.** Buying features you might need in three years at the expense of what you need now. Fix today's gaps and scale up.
3. **Underestimating total cost.** Plan beyond the licence: partner fees, customisation, training, support and add-ons. Get the full three-year picture. Under RISE and SAP GROW, model user growth over the contract.
4. **No change plan.** Most ERP failures come from user resistance and unclear accountability, not software. Involve department leads from day one. Don't rush go-live.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What should I look for in an ERP for a small business?</summary>

Fit, not brand or size. The system should map to how you actually work, not force a redesign to suit the software. Start with what your team struggles with day to day, such as inventory mismatches, double entry or slow reporting, and look for platforms that fix those gaps. Put the people who will enter orders every day in the demos.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When is the right time to invest in ERP?</summary>

When spreadsheets multiply, systems don't share data, or you are hiring people just to maintain reports. If internal coordination is slowing operations, you are already late. Waiting for a major failure makes cleanup and redesign more expensive.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Should a small business use SAP or Oracle?</summary>

Not automatically. SAP's small-business product is SAP Business One; S/4HANA through SAP GROW is aimed at mid-sized companies, and Oracle's equivalent is NetSuite. I have seen smaller companies get better outcomes with Acumatica or Odoo because they put fit, simplicity and realistic cost ahead of vendor reputation. Ask whether the system handles your operational complexity, at a cost you can sustain, with the support you actually have.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How much should a small business budget for ERP?</summary>

Plan the full three-year cost, not just the licence: partner fees, customisation, training and support. Cloud ERPs often have add-ons that weren't in the first quote. A reasonable rule of thumb is implementation at one to three times the annual licence cost for a first deployment.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Do small businesses need an implementation partner?</summary>

For most, yes. Even a light-touch adviser helps you avoid mistakes that get expensive later. Ask for references from businesses of similar size and industry, not the vendor's showcase clients.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you measure ROI from an ERP investment?</summary>

Track operational measures six months after go-live, not just at launch: hours of manual data entry saved per week, reporting turnaround, stockouts and invoice errors, and time spent reconciling between departments. The value often shows up in what stops happening.

</details>
