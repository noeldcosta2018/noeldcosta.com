When defence teams ask for Customer Information Systems, they usually mean more than a CRM. They want structure. They want a place where complex decisions can sit together in context, across long sales cycles, compliance-heavy workflows, and shifting stakeholder maps.

In my role as Digital Transformation Director at a Middle East defence manufacturer, I was asked to solve exactly this. The problem was not missing effort. People were doing the work. The issue was that effort had no shared home. No common platform, no visibility across teams, and no reliable alignment between what was being sold and what was being planned and delivered.

## The client

A mid-sized defence manufacturer in the Middle East. Components used in military aviation and secure communications, not a public brand: visibility was not the point, precision was.

Every sale followed a detailed path. Engineering reviews, compliance checks, internal approvals, version-controlled quotes. The cycle was long and the stakes for errors were high.

The problem: Customer Information Systems were disconnected. Sales used one platform. Support relied on a different one. Operations worked from spreadsheets, offline folders, and documents that were already outdated before anyone opened them.

| What was broken | Impact |
|---|---|
| No CRM for opportunity flow or stakeholder visibility | No shared picture of where each deal stood |
| Pricing handled manually with no consistent record | Quotes went out with outdated or conflicting numbers |
| Approvals through email, often lost or delayed | Compliance audit trails were incomplete |
| Customer records duplicated across systems | Nobody could say which version was accurate |

At a glance it looked structured, but if you tried to follow a quote from creation to delivery, things fell apart. Approvals were delayed without clear ownership. Pricing was inconsistent depending on who submitted the quote. Quotes were buried in long email threads. Teams asked each other the same questions repeatedly.

## The approach

The goal was not to replace everything. It was to reduce friction without making anything harder to use.

I spent time with different teams before touching any configuration. I watched how tasks moved from capture to delivery. I saw where the tools got in the way, and where people had stopped trusting the data they were working with.

Three priorities came out of those sessions.

**One shared customer record.** One place where the complete customer profile lived: visible to sales, support, and operations in the same format. No more parallel versions, no more "which one is right."

**Structured quoting.** Quotes that followed consistent logic for product configuration and pricing, not individual habits and old template files.

**Connected order execution.** A clean handoff from approved quote to SAP SD, removing the manual re-entry that was causing delays and introducing errors downstream.

## The tools

| System | What it solved |
|---|---|
| Microsoft Dynamics CRM | Centralised customer record; linked sales activity and opportunity flow |
| Experlogix Configure Price Quote | Product configuration rules, pricing logic, quote versioning |
| SAP Sales and Distribution (SD) | Order execution and fulfilment |
| SAP CPI (Cloud Platform Integration) | Integration layer connecting CPQ to SAP SD |

**Microsoft Dynamics as the foundation.** There needed to be one place where customer information was stored accurately and viewed in full context. Dynamics made that possible without requiring teams to start over. Familiarity with the platform helped. It only needed adjustments to fit how the business actually worked. Once teams saw the same data in the same format across functions, trust in the system started to build.

**Experlogix CPQ for configuration and pricing.** Before the change, quoting was handled in too many different ways. People relied on memory. Manual edits were common. That caused problems with both speed and consistency. We set up Experlogix to handle product combinations with defined rules, pricing logic tied to specific configurations, and approval routing based on deal type and value. Engineering received cleaner quotes. Sales teams no longer made adjustments based on what they remembered from previous deals.

**SAP SD via SAP CPI.** SAP SD was already in use for order execution. The gap was the handoff: a quote approved in CPQ still required manual re-entry into SAP. We integrated the two via SAP CPI, automating the quote-to-order flow and eliminating that re-entry. That removed a class of errors and freed up time that had been going into corrections.

## The deployment

Nothing dramatic. Layered changes with early validation at each step.

We started with the CRM layer: getting the customer record clean and giving sales a reliable view. Once that was stable and teams were using it, we introduced the CPQ layer. Once quoting was consistent, we connected the SAP SD integration.

Security and compliance were designed in from day one, not added at the end. For a defence client, audit trail integrity and role-based access are not optional. Every system change was mapped against compliance requirements before it was built.

User adoption followed the technology by about six weeks. The system was ready before the behaviour shifted. That is normal. Trust takes longer to build than configuration. The turning point came when teams started using the tools in real situations and found that the data they got back was actually correct.

## Results

| Outcome | What changed |
|---|---|
| Quote turnaround time | Down 35% on average; complex deals improved by several days |
| Configuration accuracy | Validation rules prevented errors before they reached engineering |
| Audit readiness | Compliance checks no longer required a last-minute sprint |
| Cross-team alignment | Sales, support, and operations working from the same customer record |

The 35% reduction in quote turnaround did not happen in the first week. Early cycles still involved clarifications and corrections, but once product rules and pricing logic became consistent, quoting sped up. Sales representatives no longer had to chase approvals or fix reused templates. The system handled those steps.

## Lessons

**Alignment drives more than technology.** The technical infrastructure was the easier part. Getting teams to trust a single source of truth, and to stop maintaining their own parallel records, was harder. It required showing people that the data was reliable before asking them to depend on it.

**Integration details matter more than features.** The CPQ-to-SAP SD integration via CPI was what made the system valuable end-to-end. A standalone CRM with standalone quoting and manual order entry would have been an improvement in isolation. The connection between them is where the real reduction in friction happened.

**Support and training make the system stick.** Deployed and abandoned is not delivered. The rollout included embedded support through the first month of live use: people in the room when teams hit questions. That period is where adoption either takes hold or unravels.

## How this engagement would look in 2026

The work above ran 2023-2024 connecting Microsoft Dynamics, Experlogix CPQ, and SAP SD via SAP CPI. Three things would change if the same engagement started in 2026.

**SAP Integration Suite replaces SAP CPI as the integration platform.** CPI is still the underlying capability, but it now sits inside the broader Integration Suite with API Management, Open Connectors, Event Mesh, and pre-built integration content. For a Dynamics-to-Experlogix-to-SAP SD flow, the integration design takes less time because more of the connector content is pre-built.

**SAP Sales Cloud and Joule for CX would be on the table as alternatives to Microsoft Dynamics.** In 2023 the client had existing Dynamics familiarity, which made it the right choice. For a net-new engagement in 2026, the comparison should include SAP Sales Cloud as part of the broader SAP CX product family, particularly when the back-end is SAP SD. Joule provides AI-assisted opportunity management, account intelligence, and pipeline insights inside the SAP customer-facing layer. The right answer still depends on existing capability and integration preferences; the conversation has shifted to include the SAP-native option more credibly than it did two years ago.

**For defence specifically, SAP NS2 (National Security Services) addresses US Federal FedRAMP scope.** The Middle East engagement above did not need NS2, but for a defence client with US Federal operations, NS2 is the SAP variant designed for FedRAMP High and IL5 workloads. Programmes with cross-jurisdiction defence scope should evaluate NS2 hosting for US Federal data while keeping standard SAP for non-Federal data.

The architecture pattern (CRM for customer record, CPQ for structured quoting, ERP integration for order execution) still applies. Defence-specific compliance requirements (audit trails, role-based access, version control on quotes) still apply regardless of platform generation.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Why was Microsoft Dynamics selected over other CRM platforms?</summary>

The team already had some familiarity with Dynamics. Starting from a platform people had experience with reduced the adoption barrier and shortened the configuration timeline.

The technical considerations supported it too. Dynamics integrates well with CPQ tools and with SAP via middleware. For a defence client with compliance requirements, the access control model and audit logging were already well-developed. Other platforms would have required more custom work to reach the same baseline.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What role did Experlogix CPQ play, and why CPQ at all?</summary>

Defence products have complex configuration rules. A standard price list does not capture the interaction between product variants, compliance requirements, and customer-specific terms. Without a CPQ layer, each quote relied on whoever happened to be building it knowing the rules, which meant inconsistency.

Experlogix let us encode those rules into the quoting tool itself. A sales representative building a quote for a complex component would get validation feedback in real time, not a correction from engineering two days later. That shift moved errors upstream, where they are cheap to fix, rather than downstream, where they are expensive.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How was SAP SD integrated with CRM and CPQ?</summary>

SAP CPI (Cloud Platform Integration) served as the middleware. An approved quote in Experlogix triggered an automated flow that created the corresponding order in SAP SD with the correct line items, pricing, and customer data.

Before this integration, the handoff was manual: someone copied the approved quote into SAP. That introduced re-entry errors and depended on people doing the right thing consistently. Automating it removed that dependency and created a clean audit trail from quote approval to order creation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What were the biggest challenges during implementation?</summary>

Data quality in the CRM baseline. Duplicate customer records with slightly different names and missing fields had to be resolved before any integration could work reliably. This took longer than the technical configuration.

Change management was the second challenge. Teams had developed habits around their existing tools, including their workarounds. The question was not whether the new system was technically better: it was whether people would trust it enough to stop maintaining their own shadow records. That took time and required showing consistent results before asking for consistent adoption.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How was security and compliance handled?</summary>

Compliance requirements were mapped before any system configuration started. For a defence manufacturer, this means understanding data classification requirements, access control rules, and audit trail standards, and designing the system to meet them, not patching compliance in afterwards.

Role-based access in Dynamics limited who could view and edit which customer records. Audit logging captured every change to quote versions and order records. The SAP SD integration preserved the audit trail from quote approval through order execution, when compliance reviews happened, the data was already in the right format.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can this model be applied to other defence or manufacturing companies?</summary>

The architecture (CRM for customer record, CPQ for structured quoting, ERP integration for order execution) applies wherever sales cycles are long, products are configurable, and compliance documentation is required. Defence is a natural fit, but the same pattern works in aerospace manufacturing, industrial equipment, and any business where a quote requires engineering validation before it goes out.

The specific tools are less important than the integration logic. What matters is that the approved quote flows into the ERP without manual re-entry. The customer record must be a single source of truth, not a set of disconnected versions maintained by different teams.

</details>
