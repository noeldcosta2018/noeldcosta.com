I once walked into an SAP rollout where IT wanted tight system controls and Finance needed more flexibility. By the time we got there, the teams had stopped talking to each other entirely. Finance was frustrated. IT was defensive. Leadership was asking why no one was communicating.

We established a stakeholder map, ran regular alignment meetings, and created a single source of truth for decisions. Within six weeks the situation was manageable. If that groundwork had been done at the start, we would have saved months of argument.

Stakeholder conflicts do not appear out of nowhere. They build over time when expectations are not managed, communication is inconsistent, and decisions are made without alignment. You can prevent most of them with discipline at the start. The ones you cannot prevent, you can at least resolve quickly if the governance structure is in place.

## Who the stakeholders are and what they actually care about

Not all stakeholders have the same concerns or the same influence. Treating them the same way is a fast route to irrelevant communications and missed risks.

| Stakeholder group | Primary concerns | Engagement priority |
|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | ROI, risk to the business, programme credibility | Direct, regular, concise |
| Programme steering committee | Timeline, budget, scope control | Structured steering reviews |
| Finance leadership | Revenue recognition, reporting integrity, system controls | Early involvement in design; sign-off on FI/CO scope |
| Operations and business leads | Process continuity, training, system usability | Design workshop participation; UAT ownership |
| IT leadership | Architecture, security, integration, support model | Technical design sign-off |
| End users | Learning curve, daily workflow impact, job changes | Training and change management |
| System integrator | Delivery scope, change requests, resource management | Formal governance; scope documents |
| HR and change management | People impact, role changes, communication plan | Parallel workstream to implementation |

The power-interest grid gives a practical view. Executives typically have high influence and high interest: they need direct, concise updates and early warning on any risk that threatens the business case. Subject matter experts from Finance and Operations have high interest but operate at a lower organisational level: they need structured involvement in design decisions, not just sign-off requests. End users often have low organisational influence but the highest exposure to daily system risk: their buy-in at go-live determines whether the system works in practice.

Mapping this before the project starts tells you where to invest engagement effort and what type of communication each group needs.

## Setting expectations before problems develop

The most effective stakeholder management happens before anyone has a complaint. Three things to lock in at project kickoff.

**Roles and decision authority.** Who has the authority to approve scope changes? Who signs off on UAT? Who can escalate a go-live delay decision to the steering committee? Write it down, get it signed, include it in the project charter. When a decision is contested mid-project, you need a documented authority structure to refer to.

**Communication cadence.** How often will stakeholders hear from the project, through what channel, with what content? Define this at the start. Steering committee: biweekly. Business workstream leads: weekly. End user community: milestone-based with training callouts. If stakeholders only hear from the project when there is a problem, they will assume the project is always in trouble.

**Scope baseline.** What is in scope and what is explicitly out of scope. Both. Exclusions are as important as inclusions. Every undefined scope boundary is a future conflict. Take a Finance lead who assumes expense management is in scope. They find out in the Realize phase that it is not. That stakeholder will be difficult for the rest of the project.

## Stakeholder engagement aligned to SAP Activate phases

Engagement needs change as the project progresses. What worked in Explore does not work in Deploy.

**Discover/Prepare.** Stakeholder identification and mapping. Establish governance structure. Brief executive sponsors on the programme approach. Run initial alignment sessions with Finance, Operations, and IT leads. Identify who has influence over the critical decisions and build the relationships before the decisions need to be made.

**Explore.** Business process workshops with business leads and subject matter experts. These sessions surface the fit-gap findings that will drive customisation decisions. The right stakeholders in the room mean fewer surprises. The wrong stakeholders, or absent ones, mean the decisions get revisited in Realize when configuration has already started.

**Realize.** UAT planning and preparation. Business leads need to understand what they are signing off on and have the time allocated to do it. This is the phase where competing priorities between the project and day jobs create the most conflict. UAT owners who are not available or not prepared are a pattern I see on almost every programme. Address it in the project plan during Explore, not two weeks before UAT begins.

**Deploy.** Daily cutover standups. The audience changes. Business operations leads take centre stage. IT and the SI move to support roles. Communication frequency increases. The steering committee needs clear go/no-go criteria before cutover starts, not after.

**Run.** Hypercare communication with end users. Issue escalation paths active. Post-go-live stabilisation review scheduled for days 30, 60, and 90.

## Handling resistance

Resistance to SAP almost always has a rational basis. The stakeholder who pushes back on a new process is usually protecting something. A workaround that compensates for a gap in the old system. A manual step that adds a check not visible in the standard process. Or a concern about their team's capacity to absorb the change.

Find the rational basis before reacting to the resistance. Nine times out of ten, addressing the underlying concern resolves the resistance without confrontation.

Three resistance patterns and how I handle them:

**"We need this customisation."** Usually protecting a process that works now and that the stakeholder is not confident will work in standard SAP. Walk through the standard process. Ask where specifically it fails. Often the concern is about edge cases that can be handled in configuration rather than custom development. Occasionally the concern is legitimate. Knowing the difference requires the conversation.

**"We're not ready to go live."** This one deserves serious attention. When a business lead says they are not ready, they usually have a reason. Data quality? Training not complete? A critical process not tested? Find the specific concern. If it is valid, it should delay go-live. If it is anxiety rather than evidence, you can address it with focused preparation rather than a date change.

**"Nobody told us about this change."** Communication failure. The stakeholder was on the distribution list but not in the design sessions. The change was documented in a workstream document they did not read. Either way, the solution is not to argue about who communicated what. It is to close the gap now and improve communication going forward.

## Documenting decisions

Every significant decision made during the project needs to be documented: what was decided, who decided it, when, and what alternatives were considered.

This is not bureaucracy. It is self-protection for the project team. In six months, when a stakeholder says "we never agreed to that," you need the decision log. When the steering committee asks why a particular configuration was chosen, you need the record. When a new stakeholder joins the programme late and questions a past decision, you need to be able to explain it without reconstructing it from memory.

A shared decision log, updated weekly and reviewed in steering, is one of the highest-value project artefacts. It costs almost nothing to maintain and saves significant time when contested decisions surface.

## Conflict resolution

When conflict between stakeholder groups escalates beyond working-level resolution, three things matter.

First, keep the conflict in the governance structure. A dispute between Finance and IT about system access controls should go to the steering committee for a decision, not be resolved informally by whoever has more persistence. Informal resolution of structural conflicts creates resentment and revisited decisions.

Second, frame the decision in terms of business impact, not technical preference. Finance and IT arguing about access control is a political argument. Finance and IT presenting the data security risk versus the operational efficiency impact is a business decision. The steering committee can make the second one. They cannot resolve the first one.

Third, make the decision and document it. A steering committee that defers contested decisions adds uncertainty to the project. Stakeholders who do not know which way a decision will go cannot plan around it. Make the call. Record it. Move on.

## Measuring whether stakeholder management is working

If your inbox is full of urgent stakeholder emails, the plan is not working. A well-managed project has structured updates and decision-making channels, not daily escalations.

Four signals that engagement is healthy:

1. Steering committee meetings produce decisions, not deferrals
2. Business leads attend design workshops and UAT without repeated chasing
3. Scope change requests go through the formal change process rather than arriving as informal requests
4. Post-go-live stabilisation issues are reported through defined channels rather than escalated directly to leadership

Four signals that engagement is breaking down:

1. Stakeholders contact the project manager individually outside the governance structure
2. Business leads approve deliverables without reading them and then contest the decisions later
3. Executive sponsor is disengaged between steering sessions
4. The change freeze date is challenged by stakeholders who did not engage during design

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is stakeholder management in an SAP implementation?</summary>

Stakeholder management in an SAP implementation is the structured process of identifying who has influence over or interest in the project. It covers understanding their concerns, establishing communication and decision-making processes, and maintaining their engagement throughout the programme lifecycle.

SAP affects multiple functions simultaneously: Finance, HR, Procurement, Operations, IT. Each group has different priorities and different levels of influence. Managing them as a single audience produces communications that are too generic to be useful and misses the specific concerns that drive resistance.

The core activities: stakeholder mapping at project start (who, what influence, what concerns), communication planning (channel, frequency, content per group), and decision governance (who has authority for what, how contested decisions escalate). Then ongoing engagement management: building working relationships with business leads who will sign off on UAT and represent the project to their teams.

SAP Activate builds stakeholder engagement into every phase. The business process workshops in Explore, UAT ownership in Realize, and cutover readiness reviews in Deploy all require active, prepared business stakeholders. The quality of those stakeholders' engagement determines the quality of the project deliverables.

<details>
<summary>How do you build a stakeholder map for an SAP project?</summary>

A stakeholder map plots each stakeholder on two dimensions: influence (their ability to affect the project's outcome) and interest (how much the project affects them).

High influence, high interest: executive sponsor, CFO, CIO. These stakeholders need direct, regular engagement. They have the authority to approve changes, delay go-live, and allocate resources. If they disengage, the project loses its cover.

High interest, lower influence: Finance controllers, business process owners, IT architects. These stakeholders are in the design sessions, the fit-gap workshops, and the UAT cycles. Their detailed knowledge of how the business works makes them critical inputs. Missing them from design produces gaps that surface in testing.

Lower interest, high influence: board members or senior executives not directly in the programme. These stakeholders need briefing at key milestones and clear communication when something affects the business case. They do not need weekly updates.

Lower interest, lower influence: end users. They need targeted communication focused on what changes for them, when training happens, and where to go for help. They do not need programme governance detail.

Document the map and update it as the project progresses. Stakeholders change roles, influence shifts as the project becomes more visible to leadership, and new stakeholders join as the programme expands scope.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How should communication be structured across SAP project phases?</summary>

Communication frequency and content should match the phase.

In Discover and Prepare: focus on governance and alignment. Executive briefs on programme objectives and business case. Kick-off sessions with workstream leads. Establish communication channels and cadence for the duration.

In Explore: weekly working sessions with business process owners. Design decisions documented and circulated for review. Fit-gap findings reviewed with Finance and Operations leads before finalisation. Steering committee update at phase close.

In Realize: biweekly steering reviews tracking UAT preparation, defect status, and data migration progress. Weekly workstream status for business leads. Issue escalation structure active. UAT communication to end user community.

In Deploy: daily cutover standups with operations, IT, and SI leads. Executive go/no-go briefing before cutover begins. Hypercare communication plan ready for day one.

In Run: daily hypercare update for the first two weeks, moving to weekly as stability improves. Post-go-live retrospective at 30, 60, and 90 days.

The most common gap: communication during Realize when the project is in execution mode and stakeholders disengage between status reports. Weekly touchpoints with business workstream leads maintain visibility and surface blockers before they affect the schedule.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you manage resistance to SAP from business stakeholders?</summary>

Resistance has a source. Find it before reacting to it.

The most common sources: concern that the new process will not handle an important edge case, fear that the change will disrupt their team's productivity, and feeling excluded from decisions that affect their area. There is also self-interest in preserving the status quo (a process owner whose expertise is in the legacy system and who worries about relevance in the new one).

Each source needs a different response. Concerns about process gaps should be taken seriously, worked through in a design session, and either resolved in configuration or documented as a known gap with a defined workaround. Fear about productivity disruption is legitimate and should be addressed through realistic training plans and clear hypercare support commitments. Exclusion from decisions is a communication failure that needs to be corrected, not argued with.

Resistance that does not have a rational basis, pure political resistance or personal obstruction, is harder. The lever is usually the executive sponsor. A business lead who is openly blocking the project needs to hear from their sponsor that the programme has leadership commitment. That conversation is the sponsor's job, not the project manager's.

The worst approach is to push through resistance without addressing it. A business lead who goes into UAT with unresolved concerns will find problems in UAT that reflect those concerns rather than the system's actual performance.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does a stakeholder management plan include for SAP projects?</summary>

A stakeholder management plan for an SAP project includes: stakeholder register with name, role, influence level, interest level, and primary concerns; communication plan with channel, frequency, and content per stakeholder group; decision governance structure with defined authority levels for scope changes, design decisions, and go-live readiness. It also includes an engagement plan with specific activities at each project phase, an escalation path for contested decisions, and a feedback mechanism for stakeholders to raise concerns through official channels.

The register should be a living document, updated at each phase gate. Stakeholders change roles. New people join the programme. Influence levels shift as the project becomes more visible.

The communication plan should be documented enough that it can be executed by the project team without requiring the project manager to personally manage every stakeholder interaction. That scale does not work on a programme with thirty or more stakeholders.

The governance structure is the most important element. Without clear decision authority, contested decisions go to whoever shouts loudest rather than whoever has the right mandate. This is where SAP projects produce their most avoidable conflicts.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you handle conflicts between Finance and IT in an SAP programme?</summary>

Finance and IT conflicts in SAP programmes almost always come down to one of three tensions. System access versus segregation of duties. Flexibility in reporting versus data governance. Or integration pace versus security review requirements.

The first step is to name the tension clearly. "Finance wants flexibility and IT wants security" is too abstract to resolve. "Finance wants finance controllers to have read access to production orders for reporting, and IT believes that access violates segregation of duties requirements" is a resolvable question with a documented answer.

The second step is to take it to governance rather than let it become a bilateral dispute. A Finance versus IT argument that escalates through individual channels generates heat. The same question put to the steering committee as "we need a decision on access model for finance reporting. Here are the two options with their risk profiles" gets resolved.

The third step is to document the decision. Finance and IT disputes are often revisited when a new person joins the team or when the original decision creates a downstream problem. The documented decision, with the rationale and the alternatives considered, prevents the argument from starting over.

If the steering committee cannot resolve it, it goes to the executive sponsor. That is not an escalation failure. That is the governance structure working as designed.

</details>
