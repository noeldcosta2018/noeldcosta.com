SAP stakeholder management is the discipline that determines whether your programme finishes on time or spends its final quarter in retrospective argument. Across 21 SAP programmes that I have either led, rescued, or watched go sideways, the pattern holds: the technology rarely fails on its own. The programme fails when decisions do not get made, when expectations were never set, when communication runs on personal relationships rather than structured cadence, and when conflicts that should have been resolved at working level escalate to leadership weeks too late.

On a European manufacturing programme in 2024, I walked into an SAP rollout where IT wanted tight system controls and Finance needed more flexibility. By the time we got there, the teams had stopped talking to each other entirely. Finance was frustrated. IT was defensive. Leadership was asking why nobody was communicating.

We established a role map, ran regular alignment meetings, and created a single source of truth for decisions. Within six weeks the situation was manageable. If that groundwork had been done at the start, we would have saved months of argument. Conflicts on SAP programmes do not appear out of nowhere. They build over time when expectations are not managed, communication is inconsistent, and decisions are made without alignment.

This guide covers who the people on an SAP programme actually are and what they care about, how to set expectations before problems develop, how engagement changes phase by phase, how resistance is handled, how conflicts are resolved, what AI tooling in 2026 actually does for engagement workflows, and the signals that tell you whether the engagement plan is working.

## Who the players are and what they actually care about

Not everyone in an SAP programme has the same concerns or the same influence. Treating them the same way is a fast route to irrelevant communications and missed risks.

### The role taxonomy on SAP programmes

| Role | Primary concerns | Engagement priority |
|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor (CEO, COO, Group CFO) | ROI, business risk, programme credibility | Direct, regular, concise |
| Programme steering committee (CIO, CFO, business unit heads) | Timeline, budget, scope control | Structured steering reviews |
| Finance leadership (CFO, finance controllers) | Revenue recognition, reporting integrity, system controls | Early involvement in design; sign-off on FI/CO scope |
| Operations and business leads | Process continuity, training, system usability | Design workshop participation; UAT ownership |
| IT leadership (CIO, head of architecture) | Architecture, security, integration, support model | Technical design sign-off |
| End users | Learning curve, daily workflow impact, job changes | Training and change management |
| System integrator (SI) | Delivery scope, change requests, resource management | Formal governance; scope documents |
| HR and change management | People impact, role changes, communication plan | Parallel workstream to implementation |
| Business process owners | Process accuracy, exception handling, edge cases | Design workshop facilitation; configuration sign-off |

### Power-interest grid for 2026

The power-interest grid gives a practical view of where to invest engagement effort.

**High influence, high interest.** The CIO, CFO, and executive sponsor. These people have the authority to approve changes, delay go-live, and allocate resources. They need direct, concise updates and early warning on any risk that threatens the business case. If they disengage, the project loses its cover.

**High interest, lower influence.** Business process owners, finance controllers, IT architects. These people are in the design sessions, the fit-gap workshops, and the UAT cycles. Their detailed knowledge of how the business actually works makes them critical inputs. Missing them from design produces gaps that surface in testing.

**Lower interest, high influence.** Board members and senior executives not directly in the programme. They need briefing at key milestones and clear communication when something affects the business case. They do not need weekly updates.

**Lower interest, lower influence but high exposure.** End users. They have no organisational influence but the highest exposure to daily system risk. Their buy-in at go-live determines whether the system works in practice. They need targeted communication focused on what changes for them, when training happens, and where to go for help.

### How RISE with SAP and GROW with SAP shift the engagement model

RISE and GROW changed the governance picture between 2023 and 2026. The traditional model had the client, the system integrator, and the executive sponsors. The RISE model adds SAP itself as a delivery participant. SAP holds infrastructure responsibility, runs a customer success cadence, and enforces clean-core compliance.

In practice, three governance changes follow:

1. **A clean-core review forum** that did not exist in pre-RISE programmes. Customisations on SAP BTP must satisfy clean-core principles. The review forum approves or rejects customisation requests against those principles. Skipping this forum produces technical debt that surfaces eighteen months later at the first major upgrade.
2. **A customer success cadence with SAP** as a regular touchpoint. SAP's customer success team engages with the client on adoption metrics, BTP usage, and roadmap alignment. This is not the same as the implementation governance; it runs in parallel and continues post-go-live.
3. **An escalation path into SAP** when infrastructure or platform issues affect the programme. The CIO needs to know who to call at SAP when something is failing at the platform level, not just at the partner. The escalation contacts and the SLA framework should be confirmed before the contract is signed.

For GROW with SAP programmes (mid-market S/4HANA Cloud public edition), the same three changes apply at a lighter weight. The clean-core review is more constrained because public edition has narrower customisation scope. The customer success cadence is more standardised. The escalation path runs through the GROW partner first, then to SAP.

For on-premise programmes, the traditional client/SI/sponsor model still applies. SAP is a vendor, not a participant.

## Setting expectations before problems develop

The most effective engagement on an SAP programme happens before anyone has a complaint. Three things to lock in at project kick-off.

### Roles and decision authority

Who has the authority to approve scope changes? Who signs off on UAT? Who can escalate a go-live delay decision to the steering committee? Write it down, get it signed, include it in the project charter. When a decision is contested mid-project, the documented authority structure is what you refer to.

On programmes where this is not done, contested decisions go to whoever shouts loudest or whoever has personal access to the executive sponsor. Neither is governance. Both create resentment.

### Communication cadence

How often will the project communicate, through what channel, with what content? Define this at kick-off. Steering committee: biweekly. Business workstream leads: weekly. End user community: milestone-based with training callouts. If people only hear from the project when there is a problem, they will assume the project is always in trouble.

### Scope baseline

What is in scope and what is explicitly out of scope. Both. Exclusions are as important as inclusions. Every undefined scope boundary is a future conflict. Take a Finance lead who assumes expense management is in scope. They find out in the Realize phase that it is not. That person will be difficult for the rest of the project, not because they are difficult by nature but because the project broke an implicit promise.

## Engagement aligned to SAP Activate phases

Engagement needs change as the project progresses. What worked in Explore does not work in Deploy.

### Discover and Prepare

Role identification and mapping. Establish the governance structure. Brief executive sponsors on the programme approach. Run initial alignment sessions with Finance, Operations, and IT leads. Identify who has influence over the critical decisions and build the relationships before the decisions need to be made.

### Explore

Business process workshops with business leads and business process owners. These sessions surface the fit-gap findings that will drive customisation decisions. The right people in the room mean fewer surprises. Absent or wrong people in the room mean the decisions get revisited in Realize when configuration has already started.

### Realize

UAT planning and preparation. Business leads need to understand what they are signing off on and have the time allocated to do it. This is the phase where competing priorities between the project and day jobs create the most conflict. UAT owners who are not available or not prepared are a pattern I see on almost every programme. Address it in the project plan during Explore, not two weeks before UAT begins.

### Deploy

Daily cutover standups. The audience changes. Business operations leads take centre stage. IT and the SI move to support roles. Communication frequency increases. The steering committee needs clear go/no-go criteria before cutover starts, not after.

### Run

Hypercare communication with end users. Issue escalation paths active. Post-go-live stabilisation review scheduled for days 30, 60, and 90.

### Joule, Copilot, and Atlassian Intelligence in 2026 engagement workflows

The 2024-2025 AI rollout reached engagement workflows. The honest assessment after using these tools on programmes in 2024:

**Microsoft Copilot for meeting summarisation** is the most concretely useful capability. Steering committee minutes that took 90 minutes to produce after each session now take 15 minutes of human review on the Copilot-generated draft. The quality is acceptable. The decisions captured are usually correct because Copilot pulls them from the recorded transcript rather than reconstructing them.

**Atlassian Intelligence for decision log automation** in Confluence is the second concretely useful capability. Templates with prompts that turn meeting recordings or unstructured notes into structured decision-log entries. Saves 30 to 60 minutes per major decision once the template is built.

**Joule for SAP Activate** helps with requirements gathering and fit-gap analysis. Useful in Explore phase workshops to draft initial fit-gap candidates from business process descriptions. Still requires human validation, but the draft saves time.

**Sentiment analysis on programme communications** (some vendors are pitching this hard for 2025-2026) is mostly theatre on programmes of fewer than 100 people. The signal is weak, the false positive rate is high, and the political cost of being seen to monitor sentiment is real. On very large programmes (500+ people), the picture changes; you can identify cohorts that are disengaging and intervene before the issue surfaces in retros. For most programmes, this is not where to spend the AI budget.

The investment that returns reliably in 2026 engagement workflows: Copilot in M365 enabled across the project team, Atlassian Intelligence in Confluence for the decision log, Joule for SAP Activate during Explore. Treat sentiment analysis as something to evaluate later.

## Handling resistance

Resistance to SAP almost always has a rational basis. The person who pushes back on a new process is usually protecting something. A workaround that compensates for a gap in the old system. A manual step that adds a check not visible in the standard process. A concern about their team's capacity to absorb the change.

Find the rational basis before reacting to the resistance. Nine times out of ten, addressing the underlying concern resolves the resistance without confrontation.

### "We need this customisation"

Usually protecting a process that works now and that the person is not confident will work in standard SAP. Walk through the standard process. Ask where specifically it fails. Often the concern is about edge cases that can be handled in configuration rather than custom development. Occasionally the concern is legitimate. Knowing the difference requires the conversation.

In the clean-core era, this conversation has higher stakes. Customisation in SAP Cloud editions must move to SAP BTP under clean-core principles. The cost of customisation is real and recoverable on BTP; the cost of unclean customisation in legacy code is permanent. Use the conversation as an opportunity to align on clean-core compliance, not just on the immediate process question.

### "We are not ready to go live"

This one deserves serious attention. When a business lead says they are not ready, they usually have a reason. Data quality? Training not complete? A critical process not tested? Find the specific concern. If it is valid, it should delay go-live. If it is anxiety rather than evidence, address it with focused preparation rather than a date change.

The most common version of this pattern: the business lead is not ready because UAT exposed problems that have not been resolved. Pushing through anyway transfers the problem from UAT to production. The cost of a two-week delay is typically much lower than the cost of a hypercare period dominated by issues that were known before go-live.

### "Nobody told us about this change"

Communication failure. The person was on the distribution list but not in the design sessions. The change was documented in a workstream document they did not read. Either way, the solution is not to argue about who communicated what. It is to close the gap now and improve communication going forward.

Apologise. Walk them through the change. Add them to the design review for similar changes. Then update the engagement plan so the gap does not repeat. Do not litigate the past.

## Conflict resolution and decision documentation

When conflict between groups escalates beyond working-level resolution, three things matter.

### Keep conflicts in the governance structure

A dispute between Finance and IT about system access controls should go to the steering committee for a decision, not be resolved informally by whoever has more persistence. Informal resolution of structural conflicts creates resentment and revisited decisions. The governance structure exists precisely for these moments. Use it.

### Frame decisions in business impact terms

Finance and IT arguing about access control is a political argument. Finance and IT presenting the data security risk versus the operational efficiency impact is a business decision. The steering committee can make the second one. They cannot resolve the first one.

The translation from political to business framing is the job of the project manager or the SI lead, depending on the contract. Done well, it makes the steering committee meeting productive. Done poorly, it makes the steering committee a venue for grievance rather than decision.

### Document every decision and its alternatives

Every significant decision made during the project needs to be documented: what was decided, who decided it, when, and what alternatives were considered. This is not bureaucracy. It is self-protection for the project team.

In six months, when someone says "we never agreed to that", you need the decision log. When the steering committee asks why a particular configuration was chosen, you need the record. When a new person joins the programme late and questions a past decision, you need to be able to explain it without reconstructing it from memory.

A shared decision log, updated weekly and reviewed in steering, is one of the highest-value project artefacts. It costs almost nothing to maintain and saves significant time when contested decisions surface.

## Measuring whether engagement is working

If the project manager's inbox is full of urgent escalations, the engagement plan is not working. A well-managed project has structured updates and decision-making channels, not daily firefighting.

### Healthy signals

1. Steering committee meetings produce decisions, not deferrals
2. Business leads attend design workshops and UAT without repeated chasing
3. Scope change requests go through the formal change process rather than arriving as informal requests
4. Post-go-live stabilisation issues are reported through defined channels rather than escalated directly to leadership
5. The decision log is current and referenced in steering meetings

### Breaking-down signals

1. People contact the project manager individually outside the governance structure
2. Business leads approve deliverables without reading them and then contest the decisions later
3. The executive sponsor is disengaged between steering sessions
4. The change freeze date is challenged by people who did not engage during design
5. The same conflict surfaces in three consecutive steering meetings without resolution

When the breaking-down signals start appearing, the response is not to push harder on the existing engagement plan. It is to diagnose which specific element is failing (cadence, authority, communication, documentation) and address that element. Trying to fix engagement by sending more emails or scheduling more meetings makes the problem worse.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is stakeholder management in an SAP implementation?</summary>

Stakeholder management in an SAP implementation is the structured process of identifying who has influence over or interest in the project, understanding their concerns, establishing communication and decision-making processes, and maintaining engagement throughout the programme lifecycle.

SAP affects multiple functions simultaneously: Finance, HR, Procurement, Operations, IT. Each group has different priorities and different levels of influence. Managing them as a single audience produces communications that are too generic to be useful and misses the specific concerns that drive resistance.

The core activities: role mapping at project start (who, what influence, what concerns), communication planning (channel, frequency, content per group), and decision governance (who has authority for what, how contested decisions escalate). Then ongoing relationship management: building working relationships with business leads who will sign off on UAT and represent the project to their teams.

SAP Activate builds these activities into every phase. Business process workshops in Explore, UAT ownership in Realize, and cutover readiness reviews in Deploy all require active, prepared business participants. The quality of their engagement determines the quality of the project deliverables.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you build a role map for an SAP project?</summary>

A role map plots each participant on two dimensions: influence (their ability to affect the project's outcome) and interest (how much the project affects them).

High influence, high interest: executive sponsor, CFO, CIO. These people need direct, regular engagement. They have the authority to approve changes, delay go-live, and allocate resources. If they disengage, the project loses its cover.

High interest, lower influence: finance controllers, business process owners, IT architects. These people are in the design sessions, the fit-gap workshops, and the UAT cycles. Their detailed knowledge of how the business works makes them critical inputs. Missing them from design produces gaps that surface in testing.

Lower interest, high influence: board members or senior executives not directly in the programme. They need briefing at key milestones and clear communication when something affects the business case. They do not need weekly updates.

Lower interest, lower influence: end users. They need targeted communication focused on what changes for them, when training happens, and where to go for help. They do not need programme governance detail.

Document the map and update it as the project progresses. People change roles. Influence shifts as the project becomes more visible to leadership. New participants join as the programme expands scope.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How should communication be structured across SAP project phases?</summary>

Communication frequency and content should match the phase.

In Discover and Prepare: focus on governance and alignment. Executive briefs on programme objectives and business case. Kick-off sessions with workstream leads. Establish communication channels and cadence for the duration.

In Explore: weekly working sessions with business process owners. Design decisions documented and circulated for review. Fit-gap findings reviewed with Finance and Operations leads before finalisation. Steering committee update at phase close.

In Realize: biweekly steering reviews tracking UAT preparation, defect status, and data migration progress. Weekly workstream status for business leads. Issue escalation structure active. UAT communication to end user community.

In Deploy: daily cutover standups with operations, IT, and SI leads. Executive go/no-go briefing before cutover begins. Hypercare communication plan ready for day one.

In Run: daily hypercare update for the first two weeks, moving to weekly as stability improves. Post-go-live retrospective at 30, 60, and 90 days.

The most common gap: communication during Realize when the project is in execution mode and business leads disengage between status reports. Weekly touchpoints with business workstream leads maintain visibility and surface blockers before they affect the schedule.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you manage resistance to SAP from business leads?</summary>

Resistance has a source. Find it before reacting to it.

The most common sources: concern that the new process will not handle an important edge case, fear that the change will disrupt their team's productivity, and feeling excluded from decisions that affect their area. There is also self-interest in preserving the status quo (a process owner whose expertise is in the legacy system and who worries about relevance in the new one).

Each source needs a different response. Concerns about process gaps should be taken seriously, worked through in a design session, and either resolved in configuration or documented as a known gap with a defined workaround. Fear about productivity disruption is legitimate and should be addressed through realistic training plans and clear hypercare support commitments. Exclusion from decisions is a communication failure that needs to be corrected, not argued with.

Resistance that does not have a rational basis (pure political resistance or personal obstruction) is harder. The lever is usually the executive sponsor. A business lead who is openly blocking the project needs to hear from their sponsor that the programme has leadership commitment. That conversation is the sponsor's job, not the project manager's.

The worst approach is to push through resistance without addressing it. A business lead who goes into UAT with unresolved concerns will find problems in UAT that reflect those concerns rather than the system's actual performance.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does an engagement plan include for SAP projects?</summary>

An engagement plan for an SAP project includes: a role register with name, function, influence level, interest level, and primary concerns; a communication plan with channel, frequency, and content per group; a decision governance structure with defined authority levels for scope changes, design decisions, and go-live readiness. It also includes specific activities at each project phase, an escalation path for contested decisions, and a feedback mechanism for raising concerns through official channels.

The register should be a living document, updated at each phase gate. Roles change. New people join the programme. Influence levels shift as the project becomes more visible.

The communication plan should be documented enough that the project team can execute it without requiring the project manager to personally manage every interaction. That scale does not work on a programme with thirty or more named participants.

The governance structure is the most important element. Without clear decision authority, contested decisions go to whoever shouts loudest rather than whoever has the right mandate. This is where SAP projects produce their most avoidable conflicts.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you handle conflicts between Finance and IT in an SAP programme?</summary>

Finance and IT conflicts in SAP programmes almost always come down to one of three tensions. System access versus segregation of duties. Flexibility in reporting versus data governance. Or integration pace versus security review requirements.

The first step is to name the tension clearly. "Finance wants flexibility and IT wants security" is too abstract to resolve. "Finance wants finance controllers to have read access to production orders for reporting, and IT believes that access violates segregation of duties requirements" is a resolvable question with a documented answer.

The second step is to take it to governance rather than let it become a bilateral dispute. A Finance versus IT argument that escalates through individual channels generates heat. The same question put to the steering committee as "we need a decision on access model for finance reporting; here are the two options with their risk profiles" gets resolved.

The third step is to document the decision. Finance and IT disputes are often revisited when a new person joins the team or when the original decision creates a downstream problem. The documented decision, with the rationale and the alternatives considered, prevents the argument from starting over.

If the steering committee cannot resolve it, it goes to the executive sponsor. That is not an escalation failure. That is the governance structure working as designed.

</details>

The right engagement plan for an SAP programme is not the longest communication matrix. It is the smallest set of relationships, cadences, and decisions that the team will actually maintain through fourteen months of execution. Most engagement plans are written for the ideal organisation and executed against the real one. Build for the real one from the start.
