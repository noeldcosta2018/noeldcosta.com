ERP modernization mistakes are rarely recognised in real time. They surface later, after go-live, when expected efficiencies do not materialise, or when operational workarounds reappear. Most are not technical. They are strategic, and they creep in during planning or early execution.

Across 21 SAP programmes I have led, rescued, or watched go sideways, projects run by experienced teams have fallen short despite strong funding and a full stack of external advisors. The cause is rarely the system. It is gaps in ownership, integration planning, and poor communication between teams making interdependent decisions.

The ten patterns below are the ones that show up most often. The article closes with a section on how the 2026 SAP shifts (RISE with SAP, GROW with SAP, clean-core, Joule, SAP Cloud ALM) change which of these mistakes you can still afford to make.

## The ten mistakes

### Mistake 1: Treating go-live as the finish line

The mindset of treating go-live like a finish line is one of the most costly patterns I have seen. Once the system is live, many teams assume the heavy lifting is done. That is exactly when real pressure begins, when daily operations, shifting requirements, and actual user behaviour start pushing against what was designed.

On a European financial services programme in 2024 the steering committee disbanded immediately after go-live. Six months later, adoption was flatlining and nobody owned the backlog. Leadership had already moved on. There was no process for the inevitable enhancements, corrections, and governance decisions that a live system generates. The recovery cost the programme a six-figure additional spend on a re-stood-up governance forum and a hypercare extension.

**The fix:** plan a 6 to 12 month post-go-live governance period with dedicated funding. Extend the steering committee mandate beyond go-live. Monitor process alignment, not just system uptime. Set post-deployment quality gates with clear ownership.

### Mistake 2: Migrating legacy processes without rethinking them

On a European manufacturing engagement in 2023 we found entire approval chains rebuilt exactly as they were in the old system, even when half the people involved had nothing to do with the current process. Nobody had stopped to ask if those steps were still relevant. The result is a modern ERP running outdated workflows. That is not progress. It is a more expensive version of what you already had.

The most common version in S/4HANA migrations: custom batch jobs moved as-is from ECC, built on table structures that no longer exist. The migration is technically clean. The business logic is broken. Under the 2026 clean-core posture, that custom ABAP also blocks Joule integration and reopens the upgrade conversation on every release.

**The fix:** business process redesign happens before the system build, not after go-live. Walk operations, finance, and delivery teams through the workflow together before a single field is configured. Challenge every step with "does this still add value here?"

| Legacy migration area | What often goes wrong | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Custom code from ECC | Unused Z-code moved to S/4HANA without relevance checks | Use the Custom Code Analyzer. Retire unused code. Align to clean-core. |
| Outdated workflows | Old approval flows rebuilt even when automation is now possible | Revisit with business owners. Map to Fiori capabilities or SAP Build Workflow on BTP. |
| Non-standard master data | Flexible legacy setups break S/4HANA validation rules | Cleanse and harmonise before migration using SAP MDG. |
| Reports on legacy tables | Direct table access does not align with S/4HANA CDS views | Rebuild using CDS views. Avoid table-level access. |
| Manual workarounds | Side processes hidden during migration reappear post-go-live | Run process mining sessions pre-migration. Digitise the gaps. |
| Copy-pasted batch jobs | Background jobs moved as-is without runtime behaviour analysis | Review via SAP Job Catalog. Adjust for HANA performance differences. |

### Mistake 3: Starting data migration too late

Moving bad data into a new ERP is like moving into a new house without throwing anything away. All the clutter comes with you, and it gets harder to clean once it is inside a structured platform.

On a GCC retail programme in 2024 the go-live failed because nobody caught that a core dataset had entries from five different business units, each with their own coding logic. The technical migration was correct. The data was not usable. Reports broke, users lost trust, and cleanup under a live system took months. The recovery ran into a six-figure overspend on contractor extensions and a revised cutover weekend.

**The fix:** treat data as a dedicated workstream with real accountability. Assign process owners, not just technical consultants. Decide what to bring over, what to archive, and what to rebuild before migration begins.

| Migration challenge | What typically fails | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Master data inconsistencies | Different plants use different naming, breaking validation on load | Run master data harmonisation before mapping begins |
| Load sequence dependencies | Material loads fail because vendor master was incomplete | Use a dependency map. Validate prerequisites for each load. |
| Single dry run | Errors surface too late during production cutover | Schedule at least two full mock loads with feedback loops |
| Undocumented transform rules | Rules exist in Excel sheets with no version control or ownership | Document every rule. Assign ownership. Use SAP MDG where available. |
| Mapping mismatches | Technical mapping differs from what business expected | Run field-level mapping reviews with business users before loading |
| No rollback plan | Critical load fails; team scrambles without a backtrack procedure | Define rollback triggers. Build backup points into the cutover plan. |

### Mistake 4: Treating change management as a side task

The most common version of this mistake is assuming change management is "already being handled." Usually that means a few slides, a system demo, and one training session before go-live. That is not a plan.

Teams do not push back because they dislike change. They push back when they do not trust it, when nobody explains why things are changing, or how the change helps the end user. Quiet resistance builds. Users follow the system just enough to satisfy the checklist, then revert to spreadsheets.

On a US healthcare programme in 2023, training was compressed from six weeks to two to recover schedule. Go-live arrived. Users left overwhelmed. Many reverted to spreadsheets. Hypercare ran for an extra eight weeks at senior consultant rates. The recovery cost more than the original training programme would have.

**The fix:** change management needs its own budget, timeline, and senior ownership from the start. Run role mapping early. Build adoption KPIs into project governance alongside technical milestones.

| Observed issue | What usually happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Underestimating user impact | Users blindsided by new screens and steps on go-live day | Walk users through key changes early. Repeat the messaging. |
| Single large training session | People forget everything by go-live with no hands-on practice | Use real system access in small groups. Give job-specific cheat sheets. |
| No local champions | Users have no trusted contact in their own function | Identify power users early. Equip them to support their teams. |
| Poor communication on "why" | Users see extra work, not improvement | Link changes to pain points users already experience. |
| No feedback loop | Concerns raised in UAT are logged but never resolved | Set up fast feedback handling. Show users progress on their input. |
| Support gap post-go-live | Teams go quiet after launch; users panic when issues arise | Plan visible support contacts and help channels for the first 30 days. |

### Mistake 5: Planning around vendor roadmaps

On a European manufacturing programme in 2023 we paused key decisions while waiting for a roadmap item that was pushed out by 12 months. The team had built their integration strategy around something that was not shipping on time. They ended up building temporary workarounds that became permanent.

Vendors build roadmaps for broad customer groups. Your business model is usually not the centre of that design.

**The fix:** use the SAP roadmap as one input among several. Map your process needs independently. Treat roadmap promises as potential upside, not core planning assumptions. Never build your architecture around a feature you cannot control or validate.

| Observed issue | What usually happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Over-reliance on roadmap timelines | Project plans aligned to vendor releases without fit validation | Use the roadmap as input. Adjust based on internal strategy and timing. |
| Assuming features will work as expected | Teams plan on modules assumed to be stable at release | Test new functionality in sandbox early. Wait for proven versions. |
| Gaps accepted because "it is on the roadmap" | Key business processes unsupported at go-live | Start from business need. Validate whether the roadmap actually helps. |
| Budgeting for promises | Expected savings from features not yet released | Base budgets on today's reality. Treat roadmap benefits as optional upside. |
| Architecture coupled to future releases | Structure decisions made around functionality you cannot control | Prioritise stable, interoperable design over planned features. |

### Mistake 6: No decommissioning plan for legacy systems

On a European aviation programme in 2024 the client was paying six figures annually to keep an old system running for six users who needed to pull reports twice a year. It had been running for five years after the ERP go-live. Nobody had ever built a decommissioning plan, and without one, the system just kept going.

Once the new ERP goes live, the old systems stay running "just in case." Compliance. Reference. Familiarity. They stay active indefinitely, draining support budget and creating audit risk.

**The fix:** decommissioning should be in the project charter from the start. It involves legal, compliance, and data governance, not just IT. Define a legally sound data retention policy before go-live, not after.

| Observed issue | What usually happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Legacy systems kept just in case | Old ECC or satellite systems run for years post-go-live | Plan a firm decommissioning timeline from day one. Align archival needs early. |
| Compliance gaps post-migration | Audit trails or tax data missed when systems retired ad hoc | Work with audit and finance. Define legal hold periods. Use certified archive tools. |
| Overlapping data landscapes | New ERP and legacy ERPs run in parallel, creating reporting confusion | Define a single source of truth post-cutover. Communicate the sunset date. |
| Dangling integration connections | Interfaces to legacy systems remain with no active users | Map all inbound and outbound flows. Disconnect middleware as part of post-go-live work. |
| No decom ownership | No team owns the task; systems stay live indefinitely | Assign decom ownership to PMO or architecture. Include it in the project charter. |

### Mistake 7: Underestimating integration complexity

ERP integration is not plumbing. It is the nervous system of the entire landscape. When it fails, the business notices before IT does, because workflows stop working mid-process, not in a test environment.

The most common failure pattern: IT and business users each assume the other has defined the integration requirements. Neither has. By the time the gaps surface in testing, there is no time to redesign properly.

On a European retail programme in 2023, go-live slipped five weeks because the order-to-cash interface had not been tested with real production data volumes. The recovery triggered a six-figure spend on rerun mock loads, contractor extension, and revised cutover sequencing.

**The fix:** integration design starts during blueprinting, not after configuration is done. Define scenarios, middleware, data mapping, and message volume estimates in the design phase. Assign interface owners with SLAs before go-live. In 2026 the default middleware for new SAP programmes is SAP Integration Suite on BTP; PI/PO is in end-of-life transition and is no longer the right answer for greenfield architecture.

| Observed issue | What usually happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Interfaces scoped too late | Design mismatches found in testing, too late to address properly | Start integration design during blueprinting |
| Async behaviour underestimated | Teams assume real-time updates; batch delays break expectations | Clarify sync vs async per interface. Validate tolerances with business users. |
| No dependency map | Integration errors cascade; teams troubleshoot in isolation | Create an integration architecture map with upstream and downstream tracking |
| Prebuilt content assumed to work | Standard flows break on live data due to master data mismatches | Always test prebuilt content with real data. Budget adaptation time. |
| No clear ownership | Errors fall between functional and technical teams with no resolution path | Define interface owners with SLAs and escalation paths before go-live |

### Mistake 8: Assuming ERP can handle everything

On a GCC manufacturing project in 2024 the team forced complex service workflows (IT ticketing, asset requests, escalation routing) into the ERP because they did not want to involve external systems like ServiceNow. What followed was custom fields everywhere, manual workarounds, and frustrated users navigating a process that never fit.

ERP works well for structured, transactional, financially anchored processes. For IT service requests, workflow orchestration, or knowledge management, dedicated platforms do a better job. Under the 2026 clean-core posture on RISE and GROW, the choice is no longer optional: custom logic that does not belong in the digital core has to move to SAP BTP side-by-side extensions or to a dedicated platform.

**The fix:** ERP modernisation should include a deliberate decision about what not to build in ERP. Use side-by-side extensions on SAP BTP for exception handling. Use ServiceNow or similar platforms for process orchestration that sits outside the transactional core.

| Common assumption | What often happens | What to consider instead |
|---|---|---|
| ERP handles every process natively | Gaps discovered mid-project; scope expands for custom builds | Conduct capability fit-gap analysis before design. Validate ERP coverage. |
| All reporting built in ERP | Users demand Excel exports or Power BI; reports feel inflexible | Plan reporting architecture early. Consider embedded analytics or a separate data layer. |
| ERP handles all collaboration and workflow | Approvals and mobile workflows feel clunky or limited | Evaluate workflow tools and platform integrations where ERP falls short. |
| Custom extensions are too risky | Workarounds appear in spreadsheets and shadow systems | Use BTP side-by-side extensions for exceptions. Do not force complexity into the core. |
| ERP can replace all legacy tools on day one | Critical niche systems cannot be replaced cleanly at go-live | Phase out legacy systems. Plan co-existence with defined replacement timelines. |

### Mistake 9: Underestimating long-term licensing cost

A US manufacturing client I advised in 2023 doubled their licensing spend in year two because they needed one feature sitting behind a higher-tier module. The original business case had modelled the go-live cost only. Nobody had run a five-year scenario.

Modern ERP licensing is rarely straightforward. You pay for modules, transaction volumes, API usage, and named users, and costs scale with your business whether or not you planned for it. On SAP, the Digital Access Model means third-party system interactions can trigger licence costs that were not in the original commercial model. RISE and GROW subscriptions add their own dynamics: Full User Equivalent (FUE) counts grow with adoption, and capability tiers (advanced operations, expert tools) carry uplift that does not show in the headline price.

**The fix:** build a three-to-five year licensing model before signing. Map user roles to licence types. Audit inactive licences regularly after go-live. Understand indirect access rules before connecting external systems. For RISE and GROW, model FUE growth across a realistic adoption curve, and price the capability tiers you expect to need.

### Mistake 10: Treating ERP as an IT project

This is the most common and most damaging pattern. Planning starts in IT, is led by IT, and ends up solving IT problems. What gets lost is the strategic alignment.

On a European public sector programme in 2024 the organisation launched a large ERP programme without involving operations, finance, or product leadership in the design phase. The ERP went live. The business asked six months later why nothing felt better. The system was technically sound, but it had been built for yesterday's processes, not tomorrow's plans.

**The fix:** ERP is infrastructure, but it needs a business strategy behind it. Bring commercial, finance, and operations leaders into the steering committee from the start. Document strategic alignment in the implementation charter, not just technical scope. Pressure test ERP goals against board-level outcomes before design begins.

## How RISE, GROW, clean-core, and AI change ERP modernization in 2026

Four 2026 shifts change which of the mistakes above are still avoidable and which are now built into the contract.

**RISE with SAP and GROW with SAP are the default deployment models for new SAP programmes.** RISE bundles S/4HANA Cloud private edition with hyperscaler infrastructure, basic managed services, and an SAP customer success engagement. GROW bundles S/4HANA Cloud public edition for mid-market. Both bring a clean-core posture as a contractual constraint, not a preference. New net-new on-premise deployments are now rare and usually driven by data-residency requirements.

**Clean-core compliance is the most consequential 2026 change for the legacy-process and custom-code mistakes above.** Custom ABAP in the digital core blocks upgrades, breaks Joule integration, and triggers commercial renegotiation when SAP releases enable-now features that overlap your custom build. The right place for custom logic is SAP BTP: event-driven extensions, side-by-side services, and SAP Build apps that survive every S/4HANA release. The fit-gap template now needs an explicit column for whether each gap will be solved in standard configuration, on BTP, or escalated for commercial discussion.

**Joule for SAP Activate compresses fit-gap, configuration, and test-script drafting by 30 to 50 percent on the programmes where it has been deployed end-to-end.** SAP Build Code generates ABAP for BTP extensions 20 to 40 percent faster. The implication for vendor proposals in 2026: ask explicitly how AI tooling is reflected in the rate card. A partner that does not factor in Joule or Build Code adoption is either premium-priced or planning to bank the saving as margin.

**SAP Cloud ALM is the 2026 default for application lifecycle management on RISE and GROW.** SAP Solution Manager mainstream maintenance ended in 2027 with extended support through 2027. New cloud-edition programmes standardise on Cloud ALM for requirements, deployment plans, change tracking, and operations monitoring. The integration mistake above (Mistake 7) plays out differently inside Cloud ALM than inside Solution Manager; design accordingly.

The ten mistakes did not change. The cost of getting them wrong did. On a RISE programme, the clean-core decision, the deployment model choice, and the FUE growth model all happen in the first six weeks of mobilisation. The window to influence them is narrow.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Why do ERP modernization efforts fall short after go-live?</summary>

Most teams plan up to go-live and stop there. The system launches, but no one owns what follows: enhancements, feedback loops, process corrections, backlog cleanup. Without a post-go-live governance plan with dedicated funding, issues stack up quietly while the team moves on.

The steering committee disbanding at go-live is the most consistent signal that a programme is heading for trouble. Governance cannot stop at launch. Plan a 6 to 12 month post-go-live governance period with dedicated funding from day one.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the risk of copying legacy processes into a new ERP?</summary>

When old workflows, approvals, and reports are recreated without challenging their value, the new system carries the same inefficiencies as the old one. The specific risk in S/4HANA migrations: custom code and batch jobs built for ECC table structures often do not work in S/4HANA. The migration can be technically clean while the business logic is broken.

Under the 2026 clean-core posture on RISE and GROW, that custom code also blocks Joule integration and reopens the upgrade conversation on every release. Teams spend significant budget on ERP and end up with a digital version of outdated processes that nobody questioned before migration.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does poor data quality damage an ERP modernization?</summary>

Dirty or bloated data multiplies risk. Duplicate vendors, inconsistent master data, legacy codes, and incomplete records all transfer to the new system unless someone cleans them first.

On a GCC retail programme in 2024 a go-live failed because a core dataset had entries from five different business units, each with different coding logic. The technical migration was fine. The data itself was not usable. Reports broke, users lost trust, and cleanup under a live system took months. The recovery cost the programme a six-figure overspend.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why is change management so often underestimated in ERP projects?</summary>

Because it is not visible. Executives assume a few training sessions are enough, but change management is about preparing people for what will actually change in their daily work, before go-live, not during it.

When that preparation does not happen, users push back quietly. They follow the system just enough to satisfy the audit trail, then revert to spreadsheets. Well-configured systems get sidelined because nobody explained why things were changing or how the change helped the people doing the work.

In 2026 the digital adoption layer also matters: SAP acquired WalkMe in 2024 for around $1.5 billion. The right combination of SAP Enable Now (for structured training content) and WalkMe (for in-application guidance at the moment of use) reduces classroom hours and hypercare load.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What causes ERP integration to fail even when the core system is ready?</summary>

Integration failure usually stems from assumptions rather than technical problems. IT assumes business has defined the requirements. Business assumes integration is already handled. Neither has done the work.

By the time the gaps surface in testing, there is no time to redesign. APIs break, data syncs fail, and systems fall out of step. The business notices first, because workflows stop working mid-process, not in a test environment.

In 2026 the default middleware for new SAP programmes is SAP Integration Suite on BTP. PI/PO is in end-of-life transition and is no longer the right answer for greenfield architecture. Plan integration design during blueprinting, not after configuration.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why do legacy systems keep running years after ERP go-live?</summary>

Because nobody planned to shut them down. Everyone is focused on getting the new ERP live. Legacy systems stay active for compliance, reference, or familiarity, and then they stay indefinitely.

On a European aviation programme in 2024 the client was paying six figures annually to maintain an old system for six users who needed to pull reports twice a year. It had been running for five years post-go-live. Decommissioning was not in the original project charter, so it never happened.

A decommission plan needs to be part of the implementation scope from day one, not a post-launch decision.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do RISE with SAP and clean-core change which of these mistakes are still avoidable?</summary>

RISE with SAP and GROW with SAP make some of the mistakes above contractually impossible and others much more expensive.

Clean-core compliance under both subscriptions blocks deep customisation in the digital core. That is good news for Mistake 2 (legacy process migration) because it forces the conversation. It is harder news for organisations that depended on heavy ABAP customisation in ECC, because the migration path now requires deciding what moves to BTP, what becomes standard configuration, and what gets retired.

The integration mistake (Mistake 7) plays out differently because SAP Integration Suite on BTP is the 2026 default, not PI/PO. Plan accordingly.

The licensing mistake (Mistake 9) takes a new form under FUE-based pricing. Model FUE growth across a realistic adoption curve and price the capability tiers you expect to need before signing.

The decommissioning mistake (Mistake 6) becomes more urgent because RISE and GROW are contract-bound: continuing to run a legacy system in parallel adds both subscription cost and clean-core risk.

</details>

ERP modernization mistakes do not always show up in system logs. Sometimes they show up in how people work around the system, or stop using it altogether. The ten patterns above are the ones I have seen most often across 21 programmes. The 2026 shifts (RISE, GROW, clean-core, Joule, Cloud ALM) do not change the patterns. They change the cost of getting them wrong, and they compress the window in which the right decisions can still be made.
