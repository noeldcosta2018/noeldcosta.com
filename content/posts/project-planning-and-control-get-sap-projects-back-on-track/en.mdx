When I started out, I worked on an SAP programme that looked great on paper. Timelines, risk registers, change logs, everything you would expect. But no one followed it. The steering committee rarely met. Finance was waiting on data migration. IT had not started. No one was tracking dependencies. Everyone assumed someone else was keeping things on track.

By month six, half the project was behind schedule. The worst part was that nobody saw it coming. That is what a lack of control looks like.

It was not a planning problem. It was a control problem. The plan existed. Nobody was actively working it. Across 21 SAP programmes that I have led, rescued, or watched go sideways, the same root cause shows up in different industries and different geographies: the plan was produced once and then abandoned in favour of meetings.

## What project planning and control actually means

Planning sets direction. Control keeps the project moving. They are different disciplines, and SAP projects need both.

Planning covers scope, timeline, budget, resource assignments, risk register, and milestone commitments. This is what most teams produce. The document exists. The question is whether anyone uses it week to week.

Control covers tracking actual progress against the plan, surfacing variances early, managing dependencies, running escalation when things slip, and adjusting the baseline when scope or timeline formally changes. This is what most teams do poorly.

| What breaks without control | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| Deadlines slip silently | Nobody checks in between milestone reviews |
| Assumptions go unverified | Teams expect someone else is handling dependencies |
| Scope expands informally | Changes approved in meetings without impact assessment |
| Risks ignored until they land | Risk log updated quarterly instead of weekly |
| Costs accumulate past budget | Effort is tracked in timesheets but not mapped to work packages |
| Teams stop communicating | Status meetings become status updates with no action items |

## Three SAP failures that illustrate the pattern

These are public cases, not client stories. The root causes are the same as what I see repeatedly in advisory work.

### Lidl: over €500 million, seven years, abandoned

Lidl's pricing was based on purchase prices. SAP's standard model works differently. Instead of adapting their processes, they spent seven years trying to customise SAP to match their legacy approach. [Handelsblatt and Computerworld documented the failure](https://www.handelsblatt.com/unternehmen/handel-konsumgueter/elaprado-lidl-stoppt-millionenprojekt-mit-sap/22829458.html). Half a billion euros. Executives fired. The project scrapped. When new leadership came in and implemented SAP using standard best practices, it worked. The technology was never the problem.

### Hershey: around $150 million in lost sales

Hershey went live right before Halloween, their peak sales period. Warehouses could not ship. Orders failed. Retailers could not stock shelves during the most important season of the year. The schedule decision was the failure; a six-month shift would have prevented the entire incident. The lesson is obvious and still ignored on projects regularly.

### Revlon: stock drop of 6.9% in one day

Revlon's 2018 SAP go-live collapsed production and distribution. They could not make products. They could not ship orders. Testing was light. No backup plan existed. Lawsuits followed. The root cause was inadequate testing, no rollback plan, and risk management treated as a checkbox rather than a discipline.

None of these failed because SAP was the wrong choice. They failed because companies ignored planning and control fundamentals that have existed for decades.

## The core disciplines

### Work Breakdown Structure

WBS breaks the total project scope into traceable deliverables with clear ownership. Without it, work is invisible until it is late. A good WBS for an SAP programme covers process design, configuration, data migration, integration, testing, training, and cutover. Each one broken down to the task level. Each task with an owner and a due date.

The value of WBS is not the document. It is that it forces a conversation about what actually needs to happen, who is doing it, and what it depends on. Dependencies are what kill projects. A delay in data migration blocks integration testing, which blocks UAT, which compresses the cutover window. That chain becomes visible in a WBS and invisible without one.

### Schedule management

Timelines fail for predictable reasons. Resources get pulled. Estimates were wrong. Decisions take longer than planned. Build buffers into the schedule from day one, not as padding, but as explicit contingency that sits next to the task that might need it.

Track schedule weekly, not monthly. A one-week delay in week four is a conversation. A four-week delay in week sixteen is a crisis. The same issue, but the cost of response is different by an order of magnitude.

Go-live timing matters separately from overall schedule. Do not go live during peak business cycles. Hershey's $150 million lesson applies to every company.

### Budget control

Budgets fail because of three things: unmanaged scope changes, underestimated data migration effort, and hypercare costs that exceed early estimates. Start tracking actual spend against budget from week one. By the time variance becomes visible at a steering committee level, it is usually too late to correct without significant disruption.

Change control is the primary budget protection mechanism. Every scope change should trigger an impact assessment: timeline effect, cost effect, resource effect. If that assessment is not completed before approval, the change has effectively bypassed the budget process.

### Risk management

Risk registers maintained quarterly are theatre. Risks need weekly review, owners, and defined response plans. The risks worth naming explicitly on every SAP programme: data quality discovered late, integration delays, resource availability gaps, cutover window compression, and user adoption failure.

On a European manufacturing programme in 2023, the team knew three months before go-live that data migration was behind. The risk was on the register. Nobody escalated. The go-live slipped by six weeks because the decision to add resources was made five weeks too late.

### Stakeholder and communication management

Executives need headlines. Delivery teams need specifics. Project managers need variance data. If everyone gets the same update, it helps nobody.

Escalation paths need to be documented and practised before a crisis arrives. On a GCC retail engagement in 2023, we kept a one-page document with the escalation matrix: who makes decisions at each level, how fast they need to respond. Every blocker was resolved within 48 hours. That is not coincidence. It is design.

On a European aviation programme in 2023, a scope expansion went unchecked. We started with a booking upgrade. Six months in, loyalty changes, crew scheduling, and finance modules had been added. None were urgent. Nobody said no. Timeline doubled. Costs went up 70%. The project survived but barely. Another aviation programme on a similar starting scope kept a strict scope log and sent every request to a change board with a business value assessment. Their project finished on time.

## How RISE, GROW, and AI change planning and control in 2026

The planning and control playbook written for the on-premise era does not survive contact with RISE with SAP unchanged. Three changes are material.

### RISE shifts the control structure

Under RISE with SAP, SAP holds infrastructure responsibility, runs a customer success cadence, and enforces clean-core compliance. The control structure needs to absorb this.

A clean-core review forum sits below the steering committee in the governance stack. Customisations on SAP BTP must satisfy clean-core principles. The forum approves or rejects customisation requests against those principles. Without this layer, scope discipline collapses because every customisation discussion ends up at the steering committee.

The escalation path into SAP itself is new. For platform-level issues (infrastructure performance, hyperscaler region availability, SAP service-level commitments), the project manager needs a documented path to SAP customer success that does not run through the implementation partner.

### Clean-core improves scope discipline by design

The most useful side effect of clean-core compliance is that it constrains the customisation surface area available to scope requests. A request to "just modify the standard order-to-cash process" is no longer a casual configuration discussion. It becomes a BTP extension that requires design, build, and test effort under clean-core principles. The friction is the point.

For on-premise programmes, clean-core is recommended but not enforced. The discipline still has to come from governance.

### AI for planning and control workflows

The 2024-2025 AI rollout reached planning and control tooling. Three capabilities are useful now.

**Joule for SAP Activate** drafts risk register updates from project artefacts (issue logs, status reports, meeting minutes). Saves time on the weekly maintenance work. Still requires human judgement on which risks need active management.

**Microsoft Copilot in M365** drafts variance summaries for steering committee reports from Power BI and SAP Cloud ALM dashboard data. Useful when the dashboard is detailed but the executive summary is short.

**Anomaly detection on dashboards** (Power BI AI, SAP Analytics Cloud) flags KPIs that deviate from established patterns. Worth using on resource utilisation, scope change rate, and support ticket trends. Not worth using on metrics with high natural variance.

What AI does not solve: the political work of acting on what the data shows. The dashboard can flag schedule slippage in red for six weeks; if the steering committee does not act on it, the slippage continues. The metrics are the signal. The action is human.

## Key disciplines summary

| Discipline | What breaks without it | Minimum viable practice |
|---|---|---|
| Work Breakdown Structure | Invisible work, undefined owners, missed dependencies | Decompose to task level; assign an owner and due date to each |
| Schedule tracking | Delays accumulate before anyone notices | Weekly review; flag any task over 3 days behind immediately |
| Budget control | Variance discovered too late to recover | Track actual vs planned weekly; change control before approvals |
| Risk management | Known risks land without response plans | Weekly risk review; named owner on every active risk |
| Communication management | Misalignment, late decisions, steering committee surprises | Tailored updates by audience; escalation path tested before needed |
| Change control | Scope creep, informal additions, budget erosion | Every change gets an impact assessment before approval |
| Clean-core governance (RISE / GROW) | Customisation pressure surfaces at steering | Clean-core review forum below the steering committee |

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between project planning and project control?</summary>

Planning produces the roadmap: scope definition, timeline, budget, resource plan, and risk register. It happens at the start and sets direction.

Control is the ongoing process of tracking actual progress against that plan, surfacing variances, managing dependencies, and adjusting the baseline when formal changes occur. It is what happens every week for the life of the project.

Most SAP projects invest heavily in planning and underinvest in control. The plan is produced, approved, and then not actively managed. By the time variance becomes visible at a steering committee, weeks or months of recovery cost have already accumulated.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why do SAP projects fail despite having a project plan?</summary>

The plan exists but nobody works it. That is the most common cause.

More specifically: dependencies are not tracked, so a delay in one workstream silently blocks another for weeks. Risk logs are updated quarterly instead of weekly. Scope changes are approved informally without impact assessment. The steering committee meets monthly and sees milestone-level summaries that do not reflect what is actually happening on the ground.

The Lidl, Hershey, and Revlon failures all had project plans. What they did not have was active control: real tracking, real escalation, real response when warning signs appeared.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I manage scope creep on a long SAP programme?</summary>

Every scope change needs a written impact assessment before approval: timeline effect, cost effect, resource effect. Without that assessment, approving a change is approving an unknown consequence.

The most effective mechanism: require any scope addition to offset something else. If a new requirement comes in, something leaves. That constraint alone makes business leads prioritise more honestly.

Executives have to support this. Project managers cannot hold scope lines against department heads without leadership backing. When a CFO or COO publicly reinforces the change control process, the volume of informal scope requests drops immediately. For RISE programmes in 2026, the clean-core review forum adds an architectural constraint on top of the governance constraint, which strengthens the discipline further.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is a Work Breakdown Structure and why does it matter for SAP?</summary>

A WBS decomposes the total project scope into trackable deliverables, each with an owner and a due date. For an SAP programme, this means process design, configuration, data migration, integration, testing, training, and cutover, all broken down to the task level.

The practical value is dependency mapping. Data migration completion feeds integration testing. Integration testing feeds UAT. UAT feeds cutover. When any of these dependencies slips, the downstream impact is visible in the WBS and can be assessed immediately. Without a WBS, that chain is invisible until it breaks at a critical moment.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I manage risks on an SAP project effectively?</summary>

Weekly review, not monthly. A risk log that gets updated quarterly is documentation, not management.

Every active risk needs a named owner, a response plan, and a trigger point: the specific condition that would escalate the risk from managed to active. Common risks on SAP programmes that consistently get underweighted: data quality issues discovered late, integration delays caused by third-party dependencies, resource availability gaps in the testing phase, and user adoption failure post-go-live.

On a European manufacturing programme in 2023, the team knew three months before go-live that data migration was behind. The risk was logged. No escalation happened. The decision to add resources came five weeks too late. A six-week slip resulted. That outcome was preventable.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does RISE with SAP change project planning and control?</summary>

RISE adds SAP as a delivery participant. Three control-structure changes follow.

A clean-core review forum sits below the steering committee in the governance stack. Customisations on SAP BTP must satisfy clean-core principles. Without this layer, customisation pressure becomes a steering committee fight on every cycle.

The escalation path into SAP itself is new. For platform-level issues (infrastructure, hyperscaler region, service-level commitments), the project manager needs a documented path to SAP customer success that does not run through the implementation partner.

SAP customer success runs a parallel engagement cadence with the client throughout RISE. This is not implementation governance; it is adoption and value-tracking. The control structure should integrate it rather than ignore it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What should a steering committee do on an SAP programme?</summary>

Make decisions, not just receive updates.

The steering committee's job is to resolve issues that the project team cannot resolve at their level: resource conflicts, scope disputes, budget adjustments, and decisions that require cross-functional authority. If a steering meeting ends without action items, it was a status update, not governance.

Meeting frequency matters. Monthly steering on a large SAP programme means some issues sit unresolved for up to four weeks. Bi-weekly is usually the minimum for a programme in active delivery. Weekly during cutover and hypercare.

The agenda should be structured around decisions needed, not progress summaries. Progress summaries belong in a written report distributed before the meeting. Meeting time is for the decisions the report identified.

</details>

The best SAP planning and control discipline in 2026 is not the most elaborate methodology. It is the smallest set of artefacts (WBS, weekly schedule review, weekly risk review, change control with impact assessment, clean-core review forum on RISE) that the team will actually maintain through fourteen months of execution. Add AI for the work it actually accelerates (Joule for risk drafts, Copilot for variance summaries, anomaly detection on dashboards) and resist the temptation to ceremonialise the rest. Most planning and control fails not because the methodology was wrong but because the discipline was abandoned by month four.
