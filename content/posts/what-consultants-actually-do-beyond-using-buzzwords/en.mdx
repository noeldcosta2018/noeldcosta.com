The question comes up every time someone is evaluating external consulting support. What exactly is a consultant doing that we cannot do ourselves?

The honest answer is not especially flattering to the consulting industry. Most of the value comes from fixing things that should not have broken. From asking questions that should already have been asked. And from providing capacity and clarity at the exact moment the internal team runs out of both.

That is not a criticism of internal teams. It is a description of how SAP programmes tend to run. The internal team is stretched. The SI has its own priorities. Decisions pile up. Scope drifts. And six months into a twelve-month programme, someone looks at the issue log and realises that twenty items marked "to be resolved" have been sitting there since week three.

That is usually when the call comes in.

## When consultants actually get involved

Blueprint engagements exist, but most of my project recovery work has come mid-programme. A few months in. The SteerCo reports look orange. The team is working hard. Progress is slow and nobody can explain exactly why.

The typical entry points:

**Scope has drifted.** What was signed off in the Explore phase no longer matches what the build team is working on. Requirements were added informally in workshops. The change log was not maintained. Nobody has a definitive list of what is in scope.

**A critical workstream is stalled.** Data migration has been running for two months with the same 15% error rate and no clear plan to improve it. UAT has opened more defects than it is closing. The test team is chasing configuration changes that keep reopening closed test cases.

**The go-live date is fixed and the plan does not support it.** The date was set by the board. The project plan has not been reconciled with actual progress. The programme manager knows the current trajectory does not reach the date but has not yet escalated.

In each case, the consultant's job is not to add more resource to the existing plan. It is to look at what is actually happening, name the problem clearly, and produce a path forward.

## What the work looks like day to day

### Scope and process clarification

On one programme I joined six weeks before UAT was supposed to start, the project team had been configured against a fit-to-standard document from Explore. Business users had not seen the configuration since the sign-off meeting. When I ran a walkthrough with the finance team, they had not seen what had been built, they had been told what was being built. Three of the five scenarios we walked through had process gaps the configuration did not cover.

The fix was not a technical one. It was a conversation gap. The consultant's job was to run the walkthrough that should have happened four months earlier and produce a clear list of what needed to change before UAT opened.

That is not glamorous. It is what the work actually looks like.

### Translating between technical and business teams

SAP programmes routinely produce situations where the technical team has done something technically correct that the business cannot use. A pricing determination that works for 90% of cases but breaks on export orders. A goods movement type that posts correctly but generates a financial document the reconciliation team does not recognise.

The functional consultant's job is to close that gap. Not by being the most technically skilled person in the room. By understanding both the system behaviour and the business impact, and translating between them accurately enough that the right people can make the right decisions.

### UAT support

User acceptance testing is where a programme's accumulated decisions either hold or fall apart. Every shortcut taken in Explore, every informal scope addition, every test case written at summary level rather than step level will surface in UAT.

Supporting UAT effectively means triaging defects correctly, and distinguishing configuration gaps from process gaps from training gaps. It also means managing the emotional temperature when business users hit a sequence of failures that damages their confidence in the whole programme.

I have seen UAT sessions where every defect raised became a reason to question whether the go-live should proceed. Usually this happens because the defect triage process is weak. Critical and minor issues are treated equally. Stakeholders do not have a clear picture of what "go-live ready" actually means.

### Post-go-live stabilisation

The least glamorous work in SAP consulting is hypercare. The go-live happened. The celebratory emails were sent. The implementation team has already started rolling off. And then the first month-end close reveals that the financial postings are not matching the reconciliation format. The production orders are completing but not settling. The help desk is overwhelmed with users who were trained but not prepared for the edge cases.

Post-go-live stabilisation is where a lot of the real value gets delivered and where most programmes are under-resourced. The team here has three jobs: work through the issue queue systematically, distinguish systemic problems from one-off errors, and rebuild user confidence in the system. A team that can do that is worth more than the same team would have been worth in month three of the project.

## The types of consulting work

**Functional consultants** specialise in specific SAP modules: FI, CO, SD, MM, PP, EWM, HCM, SRM. Their job is to configure the system to support business processes and close the gap between SAP's standard processes and the client's requirements. Their value comes from module depth and business process knowledge, not just system knowledge.

**Technical consultants** (ABAP developers, SAP BTP specialists, integration architects) build the extensions and integrations that configuration cannot cover. On modern S/4HANA programmes, BTP-based extensions are replacing traditional ABAP customisation for most new development, which requires a different skill set.

**Project and programme managers** provide delivery structure: governance, risk management, schedule management, issue resolution. The programme manager's job is to maintain visibility across the full project and escalate before problems become crises.

**Change management consultants** work on the organisation side: training programmes, communication plans, stakeholder engagement, and the governance that determines whether users adopt the system or work around it.

Most large SAP programmes need all four. Most mid-market programmes try to run with fewer people covering multiple roles, which is where the gaps typically form.

## When to bring in external help

The most expensive consulting mistake is bringing someone in too late. A pre-cutover risk review, four to six weeks before go-live, can identify critical issues while there is still time to address them. A post-go-live recovery engagement after a failed cutover costs five to ten times as much. It is done under operational pressure with an organisation that has lost confidence in the system.

Three signals that warrant external input:

The issue log has items that have been open for more than four weeks with no resolution date. This is a governance problem, not a technical one, and it rarely resolves on its own.

The go-live date is within sixty days and the cutover plan has not been rehearsed. Cutover that has not been tested will produce surprises. Surprises at go-live are not recoverable in real time.

Business stakeholders have stopped attending project meetings. When the people who have to use the system disengage from the project, the UAT will fail. Engagement does not return without deliberate intervention.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What do SAP consultants actually do on a project?</summary>

SAP consultants analyse business requirements, configure the SAP system to support those requirements, and close the gap between what SAP does by default and what the business needs.

In practice, the day-to-day work varies significantly by phase and role. In the Explore phase, functional consultants run fit-to-standard workshops, document gaps, and make configuration decisions. In the Realize phase, they build the configuration and work with developers on extensions. In the Deploy phase, they support UAT, manage defects, and prepare cutover documentation. In hypercare, they handle post-go-live issues.

The work that does not appear in job descriptions: triaging the gaps that accumulate between phases, translating between technical decisions and business impacts, and maintaining delivery discipline when the team is under pressure and shortcuts are being proposed.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When do organisations actually need a consultant?</summary>

Three situations account for most consulting engagements.

Programme recovery. Something is wrong with a running programme: scope has drifted, a workstream is stalled, the go-live date is at risk. External resource provides capacity and an outside perspective on problems that internal teams are too close to diagnose objectively.

Specialist capability. The internal team does not have the specific module or technical expertise the programme requires. A manufacturing implementation that needs PP-PI configuration, or a BTP integration that requires CPI expertise, cannot be staffed internally.

Governance and advisory. The organisation wants independent oversight of a programme being run by an SI, or needs a neutral party to manage the relationship between the client team and the implementation partner.

Waiting for the situation to deteriorate before bringing in external help is the most common pattern. It is also the most expensive.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between a functional and technical SAP consultant?</summary>

Functional consultants configure the SAP system to support business processes. They work in specific modules: FI/CO, SD, MM, PP, EWM, HCM. Their expertise is in understanding how SAP's standard processes work, where they differ from the client's requirements, and how to configure the system to close those gaps. They work directly with business users to confirm requirements and validate that the configured system meets the business need.

Technical consultants develop extensions and integrations that configuration cannot cover. ABAP developers write custom code for complex business logic. SAP BTP and CPI specialists build cloud-based extensions and system integrations. Basis consultants manage the technical landscape: system sizing, transport management, security, and performance.

On modern S/4HANA implementations, the clean-core requirement is shifting the balance. Extensions that would previously have been built in ABAP are now expected to be built on BTP, which requires a different technical skill set and a different architecture approach.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you know if a consultant is actually adding value?</summary>

Three indicators.

Are they asking questions the internal team has not answered? A consultant who arrives and confirms existing opinions is not adding external value. One who surfaces assumptions that were never tested, gaps that were not visible, or decisions that were deferred without being documented, is doing the work.

Are decisions actually getting made? Consultants often step into situations where the same issues have been discussed for weeks with no resolution. If a consultant's involvement accelerates decision-making, that is measurable value.

Is the issue log shrinking? Not because items are being closed without resolution but because root causes are being addressed and problems are not reopening. An issue log that stays the same length or grows despite active remediation is a sign that either the problems are systemic or the solutions are not addressing root causes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the hardest part of consulting work?</summary>

Naming problems that the client already knows about but has not acted on.

Most of the time, when a consultant is brought in to a struggling programme, the people inside the programme already know what the problems are. The scope that drifted. The test cases that were not written. The stakeholder who stopped engaging. The project manager who does not have the authority to escalate.

The consultant's job is to name these problems clearly and produce a path to resolution. That requires building enough trust quickly to be heard, enough credibility to be believed, and enough directness to say things that are uncomfortable.

Consultants who protect the relationship at the expense of the diagnosis are not helping. They are expensive agreement. The ones who ask the uncomfortable questions in the first two weeks and then help resolve what they uncover are the ones who produce outcomes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why do SAP programmes need consultants if the client already has an SI?</summary>

The SI is accountable for implementing the scope. An independent consultant is accountable to the client for the outcome.

Those are different jobs. The SI wants to deliver what was contracted, close the change request process cleanly, and transition to support. A client-side advisor wants the system to actually work for the business, which sometimes means having difficult conversations with the SI, pushing back on design decisions, or escalating issues that the SI is managing internally.

The specific value: challenging SI assumptions, independently validating that the design meets business requirements, maintaining a change control process that protects the client's commercial position, and providing the client's project leadership with an honest view of programme health that is not filtered through the SI's reporting.

This is not a criticism of SIs. It is a description of the structural conflict of interest that comes with being both the implementation partner and the programme health reporter. The two roles have different incentives.

</details>
