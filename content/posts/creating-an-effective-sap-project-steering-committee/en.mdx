I've never seen an SAP project succeed with a weak steering committee. When tough decisions get delayed or avoided, the project fails, regardless of how skilled the technical team is.

On one rollout, the committee met once a month. The project team flagged broken approval workflows, incomplete testing, and missing training. Leadership said they'd "review it." They never did. The project went live. Finance spent the next six months cleaning up the mess.

At another company, the committee met weekly and made real decisions, when testing revealed gaps, they reassigned resources, when a process wasn't working, they fixed it. That project went live on time, fully tested, with trained users.

One committee led. The other sat in meetings. Same project type. Completely different outcome.

## What a steering committee actually does

A steering committee makes the big calls an SAP project needs. Budget approval. Scope changes. Roadblock removal. The final go/no-go. If the committee is just receiving status reports, it's already failing.

| Function | What good looks like | What weak looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Approves major decisions** | Reviews business case, makes a call on the spot | Defers to "discuss offline": issue reappears next meeting |
| **Removes roadblocks** | HR delaying testing? Committee calls the department head directly | Acknowledges the problem, adds it to the log |
| **Controls scope** | Evaluates every change request against the plan | Rubber-stamps everything that gets escalated loudly |
| **Manages risk** | Sees vendor struggling, lines up backup resources before delays hit | Waits to see if it resolves itself |
| **Budget authority** | Approves $500K security fix because the alternative is worse | Pushes it back to next month's meeting |
| **Go/no-go decision** | Delays go-live by six weeks because testing isn't complete, and holds the line | Approves go-live because the date is on the calendar |

That last one is worth saying clearly: I've watched pharmaceutical clients delay go-live by six weeks over committee objection from project teams who wanted to charge ahead. Tough call. It saved them from a serious production failure.

## Membership by project size

Too many members and the committee can't reach a decision. Too few and critical voices are missing.

| Project size | Budget / scope | Committee size | Who must be in the room |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small | Under $0.5M, single department, < 6 months | 3–5 | Department head, IT lead, finance rep |
| Mid-market | $0.5M–$5M, multi-department, 6–18 months | 5–8 | Business leads from impacted departments, IT leadership, finance rep |
| Enterprise | $10M+, enterprise-wide, 18+ months | 8–12 | C-level from finance, HR, operations, IT; programme manager; dedicated change lead |

The rule I apply: include people with actual decision authority. I've seen committees fail because they had senior titles who couldn't approve anything without checking with someone else first. If the CFO can't be in the room, send someone with a genuine mandate to decide, not just to report back.

## How decisions need to work

### Fact-based, not opinion-based

I watched a manufacturing client's IT team claim a process change would add three months to the timeline. Business insisted it was "simple." The committee refused to decide until they saw effort estimates, dependency analysis, and capacity planning. Turned out IT was right. The committee made the right call because they demanded evidence rather than accepting the loudest voice.

### Fast

Committees that defer decisions accumulate problems. I've seen clients whose committee always ended meetings with "we'll discuss this offline." Issues piled up. The project fell six months behind. Another client's committee made decisions in real time: finished early and under budget.

The pattern is consistent enough that I'd call it a rule: slow committees produce late projects.

### Authority must be clear

Effective committees can overrule department heads. They can approve unplanned budget. They can reject scope additions mid-flight. If those authorities aren't explicit and understood, the committee becomes advisory, and advisory committees don't deliver SAP projects.

## The go/no-go decision

The most critical function is the one that happens once: approving go-live. This decision should never be driven by calendar pressure.

A retail client had a department suddenly demand 20 extra reports not in the original plan. The committee didn't rubber-stamp it. They asked: "Do we need this now? Will it break the timeline? Is this critical or just nice-to-have?" They approved five critical reports and pushed the rest to post-go-live. That decision probably saved their date.

The reverse is equally important, when testing isn't complete, when critical users aren't trained, when data migration hasn't been validated: a strong committee says no. I've cleaned up too many go-lives that were approved because the date felt immovable.

## Setting the committee up to work

**Start with the right chair.** The chairperson should be the project sponsor: typically a C-level executive with genuine authority to hold other executives accountable. A middle manager chairing a steering committee can't override a CFO. That authority matters when scope or budget conflicts surface.

**Keep meetings focused.** The best meetings run 60–90 minutes maximum. Structure: brief status on the top risks, specific decisions that need to be made, actions assigned with owners and dates. No meandering discussion. No detailed technical updates that could be read offline.

**Use data, not decks.** One energy client built a dashboard showing test execution, defect resolution, training completion, and budget burndown. Committee meetings stopped being about understanding where things stood and started being about solving problems.

**Bring in independent verification for major gates.** Before approving go-live, a manufacturing client brought in external reviewers to assess readiness. They found several issues the project team had downplayed. That independent check changed the committee's decision.

**Show the committee what the system actually does.** I've seen committees completely reverse a decision when they watched a "day in the life" walkthrough. A pharmaceutical client realised their approved design required five different screens for one common process. They ordered a redesign on the spot.

## Challenges to plan for

The most common failure mode isn't incompetence: it's politics. Department heads protecting territory. Budget owners reluctant to commit. Functional teams wanting to delay testing because they're busy with year-end activities. The committee has to be structured to cut through this.

On one project, HR kept delaying payroll testing. The committee didn't wait. They reprioritised work and assigned backup testers within a week. The project stayed on track.

The second failure mode is calendar pressure, when go-live dates get announced too early, committees start making decisions based on the date rather than the evidence. Build in explicit quality gate reviews so the committee is evaluating readiness, not just confirming dates.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is the role of a steering committee in an SAP project?</summary>

The steering committee makes the decisions the project team can't make: budget approval, scope changes, resource escalation, and the final go/no-go. They resolve cross-functional conflicts, hold department heads accountable for testing and training commitments, and make calls when project risks exceed the PM's authority.

If the committee is only receiving status updates, it's not doing its job. The value is in the decisions made, not the meetings attended.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How many people should be on an SAP steering committee?</summary>

Match the size to the project. Small projects (under $0.5M, single department) need 3–5 members. Mid-market projects need 5–8. Enterprise programmes with global or multi-entity scope typically need 8–12.

The failure mode is usually too many people, not too few. A committee of 20 can't make decisions: it becomes a presentation audience. Every member should have genuine decision authority over something.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between a steering committee and a project management office (PMO)?</summary>

The PMO manages day-to-day execution: tracking tasks, managing risk logs, coordinating workstreams. The steering committee makes strategic decisions the PMO can't escalate past: budget shifts, scope changes, go/no-go.

A PMO steering committee (in larger organisations) sits above individual projects and manages the portfolio: prioritising resources across initiatives. A project-level steering committee governs a single programme.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What should be on a steering committee agenda?</summary>

The agenda should be focused on decisions, not updates. A working structure: brief status on top three to five risks (not comprehensive updates), specific decision points that need a vote or sign-off, issues requiring cross-departmental resolution, and actions with owners and deadlines from the previous meeting.

If the same issue appears on the agenda three sessions in a row without resolution, the committee has a governance problem, not a complexity problem.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What happens when a steering committee is too passive?</summary>

Problems accumulate. Testing deadlines slip because business users aren't made available. Scope requests multiply because there's no one to reject them. Data migration issues surface at go-live because no one escalated the quality risk early enough.

The consistent pattern across failed implementations is a steering committee that preferred to hear about problems rather than resolve them. By the time issues reach go-live, they're expensive to fix and damaging to business confidence in the system.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When should the steering committee consider delaying go-live?</summary>

When testing isn't complete, when critical users haven't been trained, when data migration hasn't been fully validated, or when a key integration is unstable. Calendar pressure is the wrong basis for a go/no-go decision.

The business case for delay is almost always better than the cost of post-go-live cleanup. A six-week delay is recoverable. A failed go-live that disrupts operations, financial reporting, or supply chain can take months to stabilise and costs far more than the delay would have.

</details>
