I have never seen an SAP project succeed with a weak steering committee. Across 21 SAP programmes that I have led, rescued, or watched go sideways, the committee is the single governance forum where the tough decisions either happen in real time or pile up until they explode at cutover.

On a European manufacturing programme in 2023, the committee met once a month. The project team flagged broken approval workflows, incomplete testing, and missing training. Leadership said they would "review it". They never did. The project went live. Finance spent the next six months cleaning up the mess.

On a US retail programme in 2024, the committee met weekly and made real decisions. When testing revealed gaps, they reassigned resources. When a process was not working, they fixed it. That project went live on time, fully tested, with trained users.

One committee led. The other sat in meetings. Same project type. Completely different outcome.

## What a steering committee actually does

A steering committee makes the big calls an SAP project needs. Budget approval. Scope changes. Roadblock removal. The final go/no-go. If the committee is just receiving status reports, it is already failing.

| Function | What good looks like | What weak looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Approves major decisions** | Reviews business case, makes a call on the spot | Defers to "discuss offline"; issue reappears next meeting |
| **Removes roadblocks** | HR delaying testing? Committee calls the department head directly | Acknowledges the problem, adds it to the log |
| **Controls scope** | Evaluates every change request against the plan | Rubber-stamps everything that gets escalated loudly |
| **Manages risk** | Sees vendor struggling, lines up backup resources before delays hit | Waits to see if it resolves itself |
| **Budget authority** | Approves a six-figure security fix because the alternative is worse | Pushes it back to next month's meeting |
| **Go/no-go decision** | Delays go-live by six weeks because testing is not complete, and holds the line | Approves go-live because the date is on the calendar |

That last one is worth saying clearly. On a European pharmaceutical programme in 2023, I watched the steering committee delay go-live by six weeks against the project-team push to charge ahead. Tough call. It saved them from a serious production failure that would have surfaced in the first regulatory inspection.

## Membership by project size

Too many members and the committee cannot reach a decision. Too few and critical voices are missing.

| Project size | Budget / scope | Committee size | Who must be in the room |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small | Under $0.5M, single department, under 6 months | 3 to 5 | Department head, IT lead, finance representative |
| Mid-market | $0.5M to $5M, multi-department, 6 to 18 months | 5 to 8 | Business leads from impacted departments, IT leadership, finance representative |
| Enterprise | $10M+, enterprise-wide, 18+ months | 8 to 12 | C-level from finance, HR, operations, IT; programme manager; dedicated change lead |

The rule I apply: include people with actual decision authority. I have seen committees fail because they had senior titles who could not approve anything without checking with someone else first. If the CFO cannot be in the room, send someone with a genuine mandate to decide, not just to report back.

For RISE with SAP programmes, the composition shifts further; see the 2026 section below.

## How decisions need to work

### Fact-based, not opinion-based

On a European manufacturing programme in 2024, the IT team claimed a process change would add three months to the timeline. Business insisted it was "simple". The committee refused to decide until they saw effort estimates, dependency analysis, and capacity planning. Turned out IT was right. The committee made the right call because they demanded evidence rather than accepting the loudest voice.

### Fast

Committees that defer decisions accumulate problems. On a European manufacturing programme in 2023, the committee always ended meetings with "we will discuss this offline". Issues piled up. The project fell six months behind. The US retail programme in 2024 referenced earlier made decisions in real time and finished early and under budget.

The pattern is consistent enough that I would call it a rule: slow committees produce late projects.

### Authority must be clear

Effective committees can overrule department heads. They can approve unplanned budget. They can reject scope additions mid-flight. If those authorities are not explicit and understood, the committee becomes advisory, and advisory committees do not deliver SAP projects.

### The go/no-go decision

The most critical function is the one that happens once: approving go-live. This decision should never be driven by calendar pressure.

On a GCC retail programme in 2024, a department suddenly demanded 20 extra reports not in the original plan. The committee did not rubber-stamp it. They asked: "do we need this now? Will it break the timeline? Is this critical or just nice-to-have?" They approved five critical reports and pushed the rest to post-go-live. That decision probably saved their date.

The reverse is equally important. When testing is not complete, when critical users are not trained, when data migration has not been validated, a strong committee says no. I have cleaned up too many go-lives that were approved because the date felt immovable.

## Setting the committee up to work

### Start with the right chair

The chairperson should be the project sponsor, typically a C-level executive with genuine authority to hold other executives accountable. A middle manager chairing a steering committee cannot override a CFO. That authority matters when scope or budget conflicts surface.

### Keep meetings focused

The best meetings run 60 to 90 minutes maximum. Structure: brief status on the top risks, specific decisions that need to be made, actions assigned with owners and dates. No meandering discussion. No detailed technical updates that could be read offline.

### Use data, not decks

On a European energy programme in 2024, the team built a dashboard showing test execution, defect resolution, training completion, and budget burndown. Committee meetings stopped being about understanding where things stood and started being about solving problems. The dashboard removed an entire layer of "what is going on" conversation.

### Bring in independent verification for major gates

On a GCC manufacturing programme in 2024, before approving go-live, the client brought in external reviewers to assess readiness. They found several issues the project team had downplayed. That independent check changed the committee's decision and led to a four-week delay that the live operation was grateful for.

### Show the committee what the system actually does

On a UK pharmaceutical programme in 2023, the committee completely reversed a design decision when they watched a "day in the life" walkthrough. The approved design required five different screens for one common process. They ordered a redesign on the spot. The walkthrough was 20 minutes. The redesign saved weeks of post-go-live workaround engineering.

### Plan for the politics, not against it

The most common failure mode is not incompetence. It is politics. Department heads protecting territory. Budget owners reluctant to commit. Functional teams wanting to delay testing because they are busy with year-end activities. The committee has to be structured to cut through this.

On a GCC public sector programme in 2024, HR kept delaying payroll testing. The committee did not wait. They reprioritised work and assigned backup testers within a week. The project stayed on track.

The second failure mode is calendar pressure. When go-live dates get announced too early, committees start making decisions based on the date rather than the evidence. Build in explicit quality gate reviews so the committee is evaluating readiness, not just confirming dates.

## How RISE and AI change the steering committee in 2026

The steering committee model written for the on-premise era does not survive contact with RISE with SAP unchanged. Two material changes for 2026.

### RISE adds SAP as a participant

Under RISE with SAP, SAP is no longer just a vendor. SAP holds infrastructure responsibility, runs a customer success cadence, and enforces clean-core compliance. The steering committee model needs to absorb this.

The practical change: for platform-level issues (infrastructure performance, hyperscaler region availability, SAP service-level commitments), the committee needs a path to SAP customer success that does not run through the implementation partner. This is typically handled by adding the SAP customer success manager (or the equivalent named contact) to the committee as a participant for relevant agenda items, not necessarily as a permanent member.

The second change: a clean-core review forum sits below the steering committee in the governance structure on RISE programmes. The forum approves or rejects customisation requests against clean-core principles. The steering committee receives escalations from the forum when a customisation that the business wants is being blocked. Without this layer, the customisation pressure becomes a committee-level fight on every cycle.

For on-premise programmes, the traditional model still applies. SAP is a vendor, not a participant.

### AI for committee work

The 2024-2025 AI rollout reached governance workflows. Three capabilities are worth using.

**Microsoft Copilot in M365 for meeting summarisation.** Steering committee minutes that took 90 minutes to produce after each session now take 15 minutes of human review on the Copilot-generated draft. The quality is acceptable. Decisions captured are usually correct because Copilot pulls them from the recorded transcript.

**Atlassian Intelligence in Confluence for decision-log automation.** Templates that turn unstructured meeting notes into structured decision-log entries. Saves 30 to 60 minutes per major decision once the template is built.

**Joule for SAP Activate** generates first-draft fit-gap impact assessments for proposed scope changes. Useful when a scope request hits the committee with no prepared impact analysis; the AI draft gives the committee enough to decide on within the meeting rather than deferring.

### Sentiment signals and dashboards

Some AI vendors are pitching sentiment analysis on programme communications as a steering committee input. The honest assessment: this is mostly theatre on programmes of fewer than 100 people. The signal is weak, the false positive rate is high, and the political cost of being seen to monitor sentiment is real. On very large programmes (500+ people), sentiment cohort analysis can identify disengaging groups before issues surface in retros. For most steering committees, sentiment analysis is not where to spend the AI budget.

The investment that returns reliably in 2026 steering committee workflows: Copilot for minutes, Atlassian Intelligence for decision logs, Joule for fit-gap drafts. Treat sentiment AI as something to evaluate later, not deploy now.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is the role of a steering committee in an SAP project?</summary>

The steering committee makes the decisions the project team cannot make: budget approval, scope changes, resource escalation, and the final go/no-go. They resolve cross-functional conflicts, hold department heads accountable for testing and training commitments, and make calls when project risks exceed the project manager's authority.

If the committee is only receiving status updates, it is not doing its job. The value is in the decisions made, not the meetings attended.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How many people should be on an SAP steering committee?</summary>

Match the size to the project. Small projects (under $0.5M, single department) need 3 to 5 members. Mid-market projects need 5 to 8. Enterprise programmes with global or multi-entity scope typically need 8 to 12.

The failure mode is usually too many people, not too few. A committee of 20 cannot make decisions. It becomes a presentation audience. Every member should have genuine decision authority over something.

For RISE with SAP programmes, the SAP customer success contact should be available for relevant agenda items even if not a permanent member.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does RISE with SAP change the steering committee?</summary>

RISE adds SAP as a delivery participant. The committee needs an explicit path to SAP customer success for platform-level issues (infrastructure, hyperscaler region, service-level commitments) that does not run through the implementation partner.

It also requires a clean-core review forum in the governance structure below the committee. Customisations on SAP BTP must satisfy clean-core principles. The forum approves or rejects requests against those principles, with escalations to the steering committee only when a business-critical customisation is being blocked. Without this layer, customisation discussions consume committee time that should go to scope, risk, and go/no-go decisions.

For on-premise programmes, the traditional vendor-not-participant model still applies.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between a steering committee and a project management office?</summary>

The PMO manages day-to-day execution: tracking tasks, managing risk logs, coordinating workstreams. The steering committee makes strategic decisions the PMO cannot escalate past: budget shifts, scope changes, go/no-go.

A PMO steering committee (in larger organisations) sits above individual projects and manages the portfolio: prioritising resources across initiatives. A project-level steering committee governs a single programme.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What should be on a steering committee agenda?</summary>

The agenda should be focused on decisions, not updates. A working structure: brief status on top three to five risks (not comprehensive updates), specific decision points that need a vote or sign-off, issues requiring cross-departmental resolution, and actions with owners and deadlines from the previous meeting.

If the same issue appears on the agenda three sessions in a row without resolution, the committee has a governance problem, not a complexity problem.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What happens when a steering committee is too passive?</summary>

Problems accumulate. Testing deadlines slip because business users are not made available. Scope requests multiply because there is no one to reject them. Data migration issues surface at go-live because no one escalated the quality risk early enough.

The consistent pattern across failed implementations is a steering committee that preferred to hear about problems rather than resolve them. By the time issues reach go-live, they are expensive to fix and damaging to business confidence in the system.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When should the steering committee consider delaying go-live?</summary>

When testing is not complete, when critical users have not been trained, when data migration has not been fully validated, or when a key integration is unstable. Calendar pressure is the wrong basis for a go/no-go decision.

The business case for delay is almost always better than the cost of post-go-live cleanup. A six-week delay is recoverable. A failed go-live that disrupts operations, financial reporting, or supply chain can take months to stabilise and costs far more than the delay would have.

</details>

The best SAP steering committee in 2026 is not the largest one or the most senior one. It is the one that makes decisions inside the meeting, holds department heads accountable between meetings, and refuses to let calendar pressure override evidence at the go/no-go gate. Add the AI tooling for the work it actually accelerates (Copilot for minutes, Joule for impact drafts, Atlassian Intelligence for the decision log) and skip the sentiment-analysis pitch. The committee that lasts is the one whose discipline survives the third missed deadline. Most do not.
