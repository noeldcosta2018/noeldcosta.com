This is one of my favourite engagements to write about. A well-known Middle Eastern retailer: fashion and consumer goods, 18,000 employees, 1,200+ outlets across 7 countries, plus a growing e-commerce arm. They had been running SAP ECC 6.0 for a decade. The system had worked. Then it became a liability.

The number that sticks: 44% of the ECC objects were customised. Nearly half. That accumulation had come in slowly (one fix here, one workaround there) over years. By the time we started the readiness work, small updates were genuinely risky. Finance month-end close had started pushing into weekends. Store promotions were slow to activate. Leadership had admitted the platform was slowing them down rather than helping.

I remember a supply chain team session early in the programme. We were reviewing which custom reports were actually in use. A few of the team started laughing when they realised several of their "critical" reports had barely been touched in two years. Cutting them away felt practical and oddly freeing at the same time.

## Why they moved

ECC end of mainstream support was the trigger, but the drivers were deeper than the support timeline.

Real-time reporting was the business case. Finance teams were running manual extracts for month-end. Retail operations needed stock visibility that ECC batch processing couldn't deliver. The IT team was spending most of its energy maintaining custom code rather than supporting new capability.

The digital transformation agenda mattered too. The business had been investing in e-commerce and omnichannel retail. ECC's integration model didn't support the pace of change the business needed.

SAP Readiness Check confirmed what we already suspected. Heavily customised. Significant object remediation required. The decision to go brownfield with selective redesign gave us the right balance: protect existing investment, clean what needed cleaning, rebuild only what was genuinely broken.

## The starting point

Three things set the context for everything that followed.

**Scale of customisation.** 44% of objects customised over 10 years of production use. The remediations ranged from straightforward to complex. SAP Signavio was used for process mapping and to identify where standard SAP could replace custom logic: smartShift reduced the manual effort on code conversion.

**Integration complexity.** ECC connected to POS systems, a warehouse management system, finance, HR, and vendor portals. Point-to-point integrations. Fragile. One change in finance could knock over retail operations. Replacing those connections with SAP CPI-based API patterns was a non-negotiable part of the migration design.

**Organisational spread.** Seven countries. 18,000 employees at go-live, 26,000 trained by the end of the programme. Change management at that scale doesn't happen through slide decks.

## The four challenges that shaped the programme

### 1. Custom code at scale

44% customisation isn't a number you can ignore. The work was to group custom objects by business value and technical risk: retire, replace, or refit. Developers worked alongside process owners on every object that had active business use.

Quality gates with measurable exit criteria at each phase kept the team honest. Without them, pressure near go-live would have pushed unresolved objects forward.

### 2. Regression with downstream systems

POS, WMS, Finance, HR, vendor portals: all had to be validated. Standardised mappings and error handling through CPI patterns reduced the surface area for problems. End-to-end testing ran after every significant configuration change, not just at the end.

Throughput and latency were validated under realistic load. Batch jobs that finished slowly in ECC had to finish faster in S/4HANA or the overnight warehouse window wouldn't hold.

### 3. Data quality

This is the one that slows every programme down when it's left too late. Duplicate vendor records and outdated master data dragged out the testing phase. We assigned data stewards in each function with clear SLAs for resolution. The rule was simple: if the data wasn't clean by the start of QA, the problem came back to the steward.

Cutover rehearsals ran loads end-to-end and tracked defects daily. The multiple rehearsal cycles were what made the cutover schedule reliable.

### 4. Business change and adoption

26,000 employees across 6 countries. Different languages, different reporting needs, different relationships with the technology. Finance and retail operations had different priorities, which surfaced in a workshop early in the programme and forced a useful adjustment to the training design.

Role-based job guides, floor-walks near go-live, and stakeholder forums throughout kept the human side of the programme moving. A store lead told me afterwards that the two-page role guide mattered more than any town hall. I believed her.

| Challenge | What we did |
|---|---|
| 44% customised objects | Grouped by business value and risk; retire / replace / refit decision with process owners; quality gates per phase |
| Downstream system regression | End-to-end test after each config change; CPI-based API patterns to replace point-to-point; load and latency validation |
| Data quality | Data stewards per function with SLAs; daily defect tracking; cleansing completed before QA start |
| Business change at scale | Role-based job guides; stakeholder forums throughout; training tied to actual job tasks not just modules |

## How we executed it

### Phased delivery on SAP Activate

Sandbox → Dev → QA → Pre-production → Go-live, each environment had a specific purpose.

Sandbox validated the path and locked scope. Dev hardened transports and applied quality gates by workstream. QA ran with real business volumes: job tuning happened here, not at go-live. Pre-production became a dress rehearsal, not theory. The go-live playbook was aligned per store and per warehouse.

### Parallel testing with business leads

Finance and supply chain leads tested against actual period-close and promotions cycles. Test scripts were written to reflect business reality, not system logic. Defects were logged against business priority, not just technical severity.

Night runs exposed timing issues. I remember a warehouse lead's visible relief when the second overnight run came through clean after two weeks of failures. That moment mattered more than any status report.

### Cutover rehearsals

Every task in the cutover sequence was timed, trimmed, or merged. The dry run alone saved hours that a spreadsheet wouldn't have caught. Fallback checkpoints were simple: one-page handouts, not dashboards. People said the simple list reduced stress more than any status tool.

A store pilot confirmed POS and WMS stability before the wider rollout. What the pilot surfaced shaped the cutover plan for every subsequent country.

### Hypercare

The war room was joint. IT and business, same room, shared ownership. Issues were closed in sprints. Business leaders saw incident burn-downs alongside savings updates. Role-based guides and floor-walks reduced support calls in the first weeks. By week three, the volume of hypercare tickets had dropped significantly.

## Results

Numbers told part of the story. The changes to how work got done told the rest.

**Finance close.** Month-end went from stretching into weekends to finishing within a normal business day. The finance lead used the phrase "faster than the coffee machine." The CFO's response was satisfaction, not surprise; the readiness work had set that expectation early.

**Custom code.** Nearly half removed. The system became lighter. Cost to maintain dropped. Budget that had been going into keeping ECC stable moved to upgrades and training.

**Real-time reporting.** Store managers started adjusting promotions on the same day instead of waiting for batch reports. Retail analytics moved from a backward-looking exercise to a live one.

**Fiori experience.** Store managers replaced old ECC transaction screens with Fiori apps. One manager called it "refreshing." Training time dropped because the apps worked in ways people already understood.

**Integrations.** POS, WMS, and Finance connections became more stable. Batch jobs finished faster overnight. Warehouse teams had reports waiting at the start of their shift rather than waiting for them to run.

Some teams held onto old reports even when better ones were available. Change didn't land evenly everywhere. That's normal. The overall shift gave the business a simpler backbone and IT a system they could maintain rather than firefight.

## What I'd do differently

Four things came out of this programme as lessons I now carry into every migration.

**Align stakeholders before the design phase starts.** The workshop where store managers flagged their reporting needs were different from finance; that happened early enough to be useful. If it had happened in QA, it would have cost weeks. Create the space for those conversations deliberately, not reactively.

**Start the custom code review on day one.** We started it later than we should have. Several objects had to be rewritten under pressure near go-live. If clean core thinking (which objects genuinely differentiate the business versus which ones just accumulated over years) had been applied from the programme kick-off, the scramble wouldn't have happened.

**Data cleansing is not an IT task.** Duplicate vendor records and outdated master data aren't fixed by IT. They're fixed by the functions that own them. Assign stewards early. Set SLAs. Track completion as a programme health indicator, not a testing dependency.

**Run more rehearsals than feel necessary.** The one that caught the POS-to-WMS sequencing conflict was the second dry run. Nobody had predicted that conflict from the design documentation. The rehearsal found it. Every round also built confidence in the teams doing night operations, and that confidence showed at go-live.

| Lesson | What happened | What I'd do differently |
|---|---|---|
| Early stakeholder alignment | Workshop exposed reporting differences late enough to adjust; if later, it would have blown up at cutover | Schedule structured alignment sessions before design starts |
| Custom code review timing | Started late; rework under pressure near go-live | Apply retire/replace/refit decisions from programme kick-off |
| Data quality ownership | Duplicate vendor records dragged out testing | Assign function-level data stewards with SLAs from week one |
| Rehearsal frequency | Second dry run caught a POS-WMS conflict nobody had predicted | Run more rehearsals than the plan calls for; the last one before go-live should feel boring |

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Why do companies move from SAP ECC to S/4HANA?</summary>

ECC mainstream support ends. That's the forcing function, but the real drivers run deeper.

ECC was built for batch processing. Real-time reporting, modern integrations, and cloud-aligned architecture are S/4HANA capabilities. Companies that have been running ECC for a decade are usually spending more energy maintaining custom code and keeping integrations stable than they are getting value from the system.

On the programme in this case study, the business case was real-time retail analytics and a faster finance close. Those weren't possible in ECC without significant workarounds. The migration delivered both within the first few months of go-live.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does a SAP Readiness Check actually tell you?</summary>

It tells you what you suspect but haven't measured. The object count, the customisation profile, the adapter changes required, the simplification items that affect your existing processes.

On this engagement, the readiness check confirmed that 44% of the objects were customised. It also showed that a meaningful share of those hadn't been touched in years. That finding changed how we structured the custom code workstream: retire first, then replace, then refit only what had genuine business value.

The readiness check is not a project plan. It's a map of what you're dealing with. Teams that skip it tend to underestimate the custom code problem and discover the real scope mid-delivery.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Greenfield or brownfield: which approach is right?</summary>

It depends on how much of your existing investment is worth keeping and how much change the business can absorb at once.

Greenfield means rebuilding from scratch on S/4HANA: clean, no baggage, but expensive and disruptive. It works when the existing system is so heavily modified that the cost of carrying it forward exceeds the cost of a clean rebuild.

Brownfield carries your existing ECC configuration into S/4HANA and migrates rather than rebuilds. You keep existing investment but also bring along technical debt if you're not deliberate about what you clean.

On this programme, the choice was brownfield with selective redesign. We carried forward what was solid, used SAP Signavio to identify where standard S/4HANA could replace custom logic, and rebuilt only what needed rebuilding. That balance kept the timeline realistic and gave the business a system it recognised while removing the parts that had been slowing it down.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What happens if you skip data cleansing?</summary>

Testing drags out. Cutover rehearsals fail. Go-live gets delayed.

On this programme, duplicate vendor records and outdated master data were the specific culprits. They didn't cause a catastrophic failure; they caused weeks of friction in the testing phase that could have been avoided if cleansing had started earlier.

The fix was structural: data stewards assigned per function with SLAs, accountable for their data domains rather than leaving it to IT. Cleansing was tracked as a programme health indicator. By the time cutover rehearsals ran, the data was in shape.

The pattern I've seen across multiple programmes: teams that treat data cleansing as a late-stage dependency always regret it. Teams that treat it as a day-one workstream don't.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you manage integrations during a migration?</summary>

The first step is getting visibility into what exists. On this programme, ECC had point-to-point connections to POS, WMS, Finance, HR, and vendor portals. Some were well-documented. Some weren't.

The migration replaced point-to-point integrations with CPI-based API patterns. That meant rebuilding the connections rather than just migrating them. The work added to the programme scope, but the result was a more stable integration landscape that caused fewer downstream incidents.

End-to-end testing ran after every significant configuration change, not just at the end. Throughput and latency were validated under realistic load. The store pilot before the full rollout proved POS and WMS stability before we committed to a wider go-live schedule.

One change in Finance had previously been able to take down retail operations. After the migration, the integration architecture was resilient enough that that couldn't happen.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does post-go-live hypercare actually look like?</summary>

The war room model works better than a helpdesk ticket queue. IT and business leads in the same room with shared ownership of issues and resolution.

On this programme, issues were closed in sprints, not tracked in a backlog and reviewed weekly. Business leaders saw incident burn-downs paired with savings updates so they had visibility into both the problems and the progress. That transparency kept confidence steady in the first weeks when call volumes were high.

Role-based job guides and floor-walks reduced support calls faster than formal training would have. A store lead told me the two-page guide mattered more than any town hall. By week three, ticket volume had dropped significantly.

The hypercare period is where adoption either takes hold or doesn't. Treating it as a continuation of the project rather than a handover creates a very different outcome.

</details>
