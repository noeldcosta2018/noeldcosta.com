An AI roadmap for manufacturing has four stages: join your plant data to your ERP data, put a governed intelligence layer on top, scale AI agents across plants, then embed AI in how the factory plans and runs. Most manufacturers try to start at stage three. The pilots work in one plant and stall everywhere else.

I have implemented ERP in plastics, steel and defence manufacturing. The pattern repeats.

The model is rarely the hard part. The hard part is that the machine data, the MES and the ERP describe the same order in three different ways.

This roadmap is technology neutral. It works whether your core is SAP, Oracle, Microsoft, Infor or something built in-house, and whichever data platform and AI models you choose.

Each stage pays for the next. The data foundation makes reporting faster on its own.

The intelligence layer delivers the first AI value. Agents deliver value at scale. Embedded AI is where it becomes a lasting advantage.

## Now: build the data foundation

Factory data lives in layers. The ISA-95 model describes them well: sensors and controllers at the bottom, SCADA and historians above, the MES running production, and the ERP planning and costing it all. AI needs all of these layers to talk, and in most plants they don't.

Start with three things.

1. **A modern data platform.** A cloud lakehouse or warehouse that takes ERP data, MES records and time-series data from historians, without copying the whole historian.
2. **Governance from day one.** A catalogue, lineage and data quality rules. If nobody can say where a scrap figure came from, nobody will trust the model that predicts it.
3. **Self-service reporting.** Plant managers should see OEE, scrap and schedule adherence from one source, before any AI is involved.

The hardest work is master data. Material numbers that differ between plants, units of measure that don't reconcile, machine timestamps in local time against ERP postings in UTC. Fix those here, or every AI use case inherits them.

## Year 1: a governed intelligence layer

Year one turns data into something AI can use safely. The core of it is a semantic layer: one business language for plants, lines, work centres, materials, orders and defects. An AI agent asked about "line 3 downtime" needs to know which line, which plant and which shift you mean.

Put AI governance in place in the same year. Keep a register of every AI use case with a named owner, a risk rating and a human review point. If an AI system becomes a safety component of a machine, the EU AI Act treats it as high-risk, so involve your safety engineers early.

Then deliver two or three quick wins that people on the floor will notice.

1. **A maintenance copilot.** Technicians ask questions of manuals, work orders and failure history in plain language.
2. **Shift handover summaries.** Generated from MES events, downtime logs and quality holds, so the next shift starts informed.
3. **Supplier document checks.** Certificates of conformance and delivery notes read and matched against purchase orders.

## Year 2: scale AI agents across plants

Year two moves from assistants to agents that do work. An assistant answers a question. An agent watches a signal, decides what to do within limits you set, and acts in your systems: it raises a work order, holds a batch or proposes a schedule change.

These are the agents I see paying back first in manufacturing.

1. **Predictive maintenance.** Vibration, temperature and current readings trigger a work order in the ERP before the failure, with the spare parts reserved.
2. **Quality.** Vision inspection flags a defect, the agent holds the batch, links it to the process parameters and opens a deviation.
3. **Planning.** The agent rebalances the schedule when a machine goes down or a material is late, and asks a planner to approve.
4. **Procurement.** It chases late deliveries, compares supplier lead times and drafts the expedite request.

The shift in year two is the operating model. Someone has to own each agent, its limits and its results. Treat agents like new team members: defined role, defined authority, regular review.

## Year 3: AI embedded at scale

By year three AI is no longer a project. It sits inside the key processes: sales and operations planning, production, quality, maintenance and costing. People and agents work together, and the whole chain is governed end to end.

This is also when data sharing pays off. Suppliers share capacity and quality data, customers share forecasts, and the agents plan across that wider picture. The value is a factory that adapts faster than its competitors, which is hard to copy.

## Across every stage

Four things don't belong to one year. They run through all of them.

1. **OT security.** Connecting plant systems to cloud AI widens the attack surface. Design the connection to IEC 62443 and keep control networks segmented.
2. **Sovereignty.** Defence, government and some export-controlled work needs in-country or on-premise AI options. Decide which data may leave the plant before you choose a model.
3. **An open, multi-vendor stack.** Use open formats and standard interfaces such as OPC UA, so you can change models and platforms without rebuilding.
4. **Your people.** Upskill planners, engineers and technicians to work with agents. Adoption decides the result more than the model does.

## Where manufacturing AI roadmaps go wrong

The most common failure is starting with a pilot that cannot scale. A vision model trained on one line, with data pulled by hand, proves the idea and nothing else. The second plant has different cameras, different part numbers and nobody to maintain it.

The second is treating AI as an IT project. If production, quality and maintenance leaders don't own the use cases, the agents get built and then ignored. My advice is to start with processes, not systems, and give each use case a business owner before anyone writes code.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is an AI roadmap for manufacturing?</summary>

It is a staged plan for using AI across a manufacturing business. It runs from a data foundation that joins plant and ERP data, through a governed intelligence layer and AI agents, to AI embedded in how the factory plans and runs.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Where should a manufacturer start with AI?</summary>

Start with data, not a model. Join ERP, MES and machine data in one governed platform, fix the master data, and give plant managers self-service reporting. The first AI use cases come in year one, on top of that foundation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Which AI use cases pay back first in manufacturing?</summary>

Maintenance copilots, shift handover summaries and supplier document checks are quick to deliver. Predictive maintenance, vision quality inspection and schedule rebalancing deliver more, but need better data, so they usually come in year two.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long does an AI roadmap for manufacturing take?</summary>

I plan it over three years after the data foundation is in progress: a governed intelligence layer in year one, agents across plants in year two and AI embedded in key processes in year three. Quick wins should show value within the first year.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Do we need to replace our ERP or MES before using AI?</summary>

No. AI sits on top of your existing systems through the data foundation. What matters is that the ERP and MES data is clean, consistent and connected, whichever vendors you use.

</details>
