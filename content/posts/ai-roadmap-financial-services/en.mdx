An AI roadmap for insurers and wealth managers has four stages: a governed data foundation, an intelligence layer that turns documents and data into one business language, AI agents for claims, underwriting and advice, then AI embedded in the key functions. The same structure works for a general insurer, a life company or an asset manager. What changes is where the agents go.

Financial services firms outside banking have a particular problem. Much of their knowledge sits in documents: policy wordings, claim files, medical reports, broker submissions, fund prospectuses and suitability letters. That makes generative AI unusually valuable, and unusually easy to get wrong.

This roadmap is technology neutral. It works with any policy administration, claims or portfolio platform, and any model provider your regulator accepts. I cover banks separately in my [AI strategy for banks](/ai-roadmap-banking/).

## Now: build the data foundation

Insurers and wealth managers usually run several generations of systems. A legacy policy platform for closed books, a newer one for current products, a claims system, a portfolio management system, a CRM and a finance and actuarial stack producing IFRS 17 and solvency figures.

1. **A modern data platform.** A lakehouse that holds structured data from those systems and the documents themselves, indexed and searchable.
2. **Governance, catalogue and lineage.** Actuarial, finance and regulatory figures must trace back to source. So must every data point an AI model uses.
3. **Self-service reporting.** Underwriting, claims and distribution teams work from the same figures as finance.

Treat documents as data from the start. Classify them, extract the key fields and keep the link to the original. Every later use case depends on it.

## Year 1: a governed intelligence layer

Year one builds a semantic layer: one business language for policy, cover, claim, reserve, client, holding, mandate and risk profile. An agent asked about "open claims over the reserve" must use the same definitions as the claims director.

AI governance belongs in the same year.

The EU AI Act treats AI used for risk assessment and pricing of individuals in life and health insurance as high-risk. In the UK, the FCA's Consumer Duty asks firms to show good outcomes for customers, which applies to AI-driven advice and pricing too.

Keep one register of AI use cases with owners, risk ratings, testing and human review.

Then deliver quick wins.

1. **Document copilots.** Underwriters, claims handlers and advisers ask questions of policy wordings, claim files and product terms.
2. **Submission and claim intake.** Broker submissions and first notifications of loss are read, classified and routed.
3. **Client meeting preparation.** Advisers get a summary of holdings, recent activity and open service requests before each meeting.

## Year 2: scale AI agents across the business

Year two moves from assistants to agents that do work within limits. These are the areas where I would put them first.

1. **Claims.** The agent gathers documents, checks cover, proposes a reserve and settles simple claims within an approved limit. Complex claims go to a handler with the file prepared.
2. **Underwriting.** For commercial lines, it extracts the submission, checks appetite and prepares the risk for an underwriter to price.
3. **Fraud.** It scores claims and flags patterns across policies, with an explanation an investigator can follow.
4. **Advice and servicing.** It drafts suitability reports and portfolio reviews for an adviser to check and sign.

Accountability does not move to the agent. Each agent needs an owner, defined authority, monitoring and a route for customers to reach a person.

## Year 3: AI embedded at scale

By year three AI sits inside the key functions: underwriting, claims, distribution, investment operations and finance. People and agents work together, and the chain from data to decision is governed end to end.

Partners join in. Brokers, bancassurance partners, repairers and platforms connect through the same governed layer. The value is sustained: lower cost to serve, faster claims and a client experience that is harder to copy.

## Across every stage

1. **In-country and sovereign options.** Client and health data often has to stay in the country. Choose platforms and models that can run there.
2. **An open, multi-platform ecosystem.** Keep the freedom to change models and platforms as they improve.
3. **Governance aligned to your regulators.** One AI register and one model risk process, mapped to insurance, conduct and data protection rules.
4. **Your people upskilled.** Underwriters, claims handlers and advisers should know what the agents do and when to overrule them.

## Where these roadmaps go wrong

The common failure is a document AI pilot without governance. It reads claim files well in a demo, then nobody can explain why it suggested a reserve, and it cannot go live. Build the register, the testing and the human review into the first use case.

The second is leaving finance and actuarial out. If AI changes how claims are reserved or how products are priced, it touches IFRS 17 and solvency figures. Bring the CFO and the chief actuary in at the start.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>How is AI used in insurance?</summary>

Mainly for document-heavy work: reading claims and submissions, triaging claims, flagging fraud, supporting underwriters and helping servicing teams. Pricing and risk assessment for individuals is also possible, but it carries the highest regulatory risk.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Where should an insurer or wealth manager start with AI?</summary>

Start with the data foundation and treat documents as data. Then deliver internal use cases first: document copilots, claim intake and adviser meeting preparation. Decisions that affect individual customers come later, with stronger controls.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is AI in insurance high-risk under the EU AI Act?</summary>

AI used for risk assessment and pricing of individuals in life and health insurance is listed as high-risk. It needs risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight and monitoring before it goes live.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can AI give financial advice?</summary>

AI can draft suitability reports and portfolio reviews, but a qualified adviser should check and sign them. The firm stays accountable for the advice and for showing good outcomes for the client.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long does an AI roadmap for financial services take?</summary>

I plan it over three years once the data foundation is under way: a governed intelligence layer in year one, agents in year two and AI embedded in key functions in year three. Quick wins should show value in the first year.

</details>
