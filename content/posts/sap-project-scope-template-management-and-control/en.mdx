Teams fill out a scope template and move on. Weeks later, during design or build, someone flags a process that was "assumed to be in scope". The conversation gets uncomfortable. Nobody wrote it down. Nobody meant to leave it out. The gap was invisible until it was not.

Across 21 SAP programmes that I have led, rescued, or watched go sideways, the pattern is consistent: a few unchecked assumptions at the start quietly shift the project weeks off course.

The scope template's job is not to record what people agreed in the room. It is to force clarity before configuration locks in assumptions that become expensive to reverse. That includes what is in, what is explicitly out, who owns what, what non-functional requirements the project is responsible for, and, in 2026, which cloud edition is being deployed and how clean-core compliance will be governed.

## What belongs in a scope template

### 1. Project objectives

This section gives the project purpose. Why is this work being done? What does the business expect to see when it is finished? Objectives that are not written down get interpreted differently by different people. Different interpretations surface during UAT, not before.

Tie objectives to measurable outcomes: reduce month-end close by three days, eliminate manual reconciliations across three entities, achieve a single view of inventory across all plants. Vague objectives create vague success criteria.

### 2. Scope definition

Which modules are included? Which plants and legal entities? Which integrations? Which countries? Which languages? Which cloud edition (S/4HANA Cloud public via GROW, S/4HANA Cloud private via RISE, or on-premise)?

Be specific. "Finance" is not scope. "SAP FI/CO covering accounts payable, accounts receivable, general ledger, and cost centre accounting for the UAE legal entity on S/4HANA Cloud private edition" is scope.

### 3. Exclusions

This section is where most scope templates fail. Exclusions are as important as inclusions. If something is not listed as explicitly excluded, someone will assume it is in scope.

The most common things that need to be explicitly excluded:

1. Countries or entities deferred to a later phase
2. Legacy system integrations being retained temporarily as-is
3. Historical data beyond a defined cutoff date
4. Reports that will be handled in a post-go-live enhancement
5. Specific regulatory requirements deferred pending legal confirmation

Write them down. Name them. On a European manufacturing programme in 2024, the client's scope document explicitly listed the exclusions section. When a stakeholder later asked why a deferred entity was not in the rollout, the team pointed to the signed document. The conversation lasted two minutes instead of two weeks.

### 4. Data migration scope

Data migration is a consistent blind spot. The scope template needs to answer three questions explicitly:

1. **What data gets migrated?** Open items only, or full transactional history? Customer master and vendor master, or just the active subset? Material master for all plants, or only the go-live entities?
2. **What are the cutoff rules?** As-of date for open purchase orders, open sales orders, open work orders. What happens to items in progress at cutover?
3. **What gets archived, not migrated?** Regulatory requirements for historical data retention. Legacy system read-only access period.

Assumptions about data migration scope left undocumented become disputes during build. The cost of resolving them after migration mapping has started is higher than calling them out before it begins.

### 5. Non-functional scope

Non-functional requirements consistently get dropped during planning and surface as blockers late in testing. The scope template should explicitly include:

1. System availability SLA (24x7, business hours only, maintenance window schedule). For RISE programmes, the SAP-managed infrastructure SLA needs to be referenced explicitly.
2. Performance expectations under peak load (response time, transaction throughput)
3. Audit logging requirements (which transactions must be logged, retention period)
4. Data security and access control requirements
5. Reporting latency (real-time vs near-real-time vs daily batch)

These are not features. They are constraints the system must meet. If they are not in scope, they are not being designed for.

### 6. Roles and responsibilities

Every module has a workstream lead on the consultant side. Every workstream also needs a business counterpart with decision authority. Both must be named in the scope document.

If a workstream has no assigned lead, it will not get delivered on time. If the business counterpart does not have decision authority, every design decision escalates.

The most common gap: UAT ownership. Who owns UAT for each business area? Who has the authority to sign off that a process is tested and accepted? This needs to be defined before build starts, not two weeks before go-live.

For RISE with SAP programmes, add the named SAP customer success contact and a clean-core architect (or BTP extension lead) to the roles section.

### 7. Change control process

The scope document must include the process for changing scope. Not a vague statement about formal approvals. A specific process: what triggers a change request, who reviews it, who approves it, how the impact on timeline and budget is assessed, and what documentation is required.

Without this, change requests happen informally. "Can we add this?" becomes "we thought that was included" becomes a three-week timeline extension nobody planned for.

For RISE programmes, the change control process must include the clean-core review forum decision path. Every customisation request needs a documented decision on whether it can be configured (no extension), built as a BTP extension (clean-core compliant), or rejected.

### 8. Assumptions and constraints

List the assumptions the scope is based on. If an assumption is wrong, the scope needs to be revisited. Making assumptions explicit forces the team to validate them rather than carry them silently.

Constraints the scope should call out: regulatory deadlines that create hard go-live dates, budget ceilings, resource availability restrictions, legacy system decommission timelines that create dependency pressure.

## How RISE, GROW, and clean-core add to the scope template in 2026

The eight-element scope template still works. RISE with SAP and the 2024-2025 platform changes added three considerations that need to be wired into the template explicitly.

### Cloud edition decision

For 2026 programmes, the cloud edition decision (S/4HANA Cloud public edition via GROW, S/4HANA Cloud private edition via RISE, or on-premise) is the gravitational centre of the scope template. The rollout strategy, migration strategy, and customisation scope all sit within this decision rather than above it.

The cloud edition decision should be the first item under scope definition. It is not a technology choice; it is a scope-shaping choice. Public edition (GROW) constrains customisation to BTP extensions on a more standardised pattern; private edition (RISE) allows more flexibility within clean-core; on-premise allows in-system modification under voluntary clean-core discipline.

### Clean-core compliance plan

Clean-core compliance is mandatory across SAP Cloud editions. The scope template needs to call out, explicitly:

- Every identified customisation has a documented decision on whether to configure, build as a BTP extension, or reject
- The clean-core review forum is operational by end of Explore
- The partner's BTP extension experience is verified and documented
- The customisation maintenance plan (post-go-live BTP extension governance) is in scope

Without these elements, partners without clean-core experience create in-system modifications anyway, producing technical debt that surfaces at the first major upgrade.

### SAP customer success engagement terms

Under RISE with SAP, SAP itself runs a customer success engagement with the client throughout implementation and post-go-live. The scope template should document:

- Named SAP customer success contact
- Escalation path into SAP for platform issues
- SAP customer success engagement cadence
- Adoption metrics that SAP customer success will track and review with the client

For GROW programmes, a lighter version of the same scope addition applies. For on-premise programmes, this dimension does not apply.

## Customisation control

Customisations are the scope creep mechanism that takes longest to surface. One approved custom report becomes five. One approved workflow deviation becomes a precedent for every exception request that follows. By the time the impact is visible, the team is three months into build.

### Essential vs important vs unnecessary

Classify every customisation request into one of three buckets. Essential: the business process legally or operationally cannot work without it. Important but not critical: improves efficiency but is not a showstopper. Unnecessary: a preference or a replication of legacy behaviour that should be abandoned.

Challenge everything in the third category. Most unnecessary customisations exist because someone did not want to change how they work, not because SAP could not support the process another way.

On a GCC retail programme in 2024, the client classified 47 customisation requests using this framework. 12 were essential. 18 were important but not critical. 17 were unnecessary. The 17 were rejected at scope-lock; the saving was material both in implementation budget and in five-year maintenance cost.

### Cost of customisation is not just the development

Every custom development adds testing scope, training complexity, documentation overhead, and upgrade risk. A customisation that costs $10K to build can cost $50K across its lifetime in maintenance and upgrade rework. Include this in the business case for any non-trivial customisation request.

Under clean-core compliance, the customisation lives on SAP BTP rather than as an in-system modification. The build cost is similar; the maintenance cost is substantially lower because the customisation survives upgrades cleanly. This is the architectural argument for clean-core enforcement.

### Change freeze before go-live

Set a date, typically four to six weeks before go-live, after which no new customisation requests are approved for the current release. Everything after that date goes into the post-go-live enhancement backlog.

On a European retail programme in 2024, the change freeze was set at six weeks before go-live and signed off by the steering committee. When a department head tried to add a custom report at week four, the project manager pointed to the signed freeze date. The request went to post-go-live enhancement. Without the freeze, last-minute requests compress testing and put cutover at risk.

## Analytics scope

Analytics is where scope conversations get heated. Everyone wants reports. Nobody defines how many or what kind before development starts.

Define analytics requirements in the blueprint phase. Ask stakeholders what reports they actually need, not what reports they might possibly want. Get a fixed list. Agree on which reports are standard SAP output and which require custom development. Get that list signed off as part of the scope document.

Standard reports cost a fraction of custom reports. If a stakeholder's requirement can be met with a standard SAP report, that is the decision. Custom report requests that arrive after design is locked should follow the change control process.

Reporting data sources need to be identified at scope stage. A report that pulls from three systems has integration requirements that need to be designed. Discovering late in build that a key report requires a legacy system integration that was not in scope is avoidable with upfront analysis.

For 2026 programmes, Joule for SAP Activate can draft an initial fit-gap from process descriptions, including identifying which standard SAP analytics outputs cover stated reporting needs. This accelerates the scope definition for analytics specifically, since the trade-off between standard and custom is the most common analytics scope dispute.

## Common scope mistakes

| Mistake | What it causes | How to avoid it |
|---|---|---|
| Objectives not measurable | No way to define done; UAT disputes over what "working" means | Set specific measurable outcomes at the start |
| Exclusions not documented | Scope disputes mid-project; additional work absorbed without approval | Explicitly list what is out of scope |
| Data migration scope left vague | Volume estimates wrong; cutover date slips; unexpected rework | Define cutoff rules, what migrates, what archives |
| Non-functional requirements absent | Audit failures; performance issues discovered at go-live | Include availability, performance, and audit requirements |
| No named UAT owners | Testing drags; nobody has authority to sign off | Assign named individuals with decision authority |
| Change control not defined | Informal scope additions; compressed timelines | Document the change request process in the scope template |
| No sign-off | Stakeholders challenge scope later without accountability | Require formal signatures from sponsors and module leads |
| Analytics left for later | Last-minute report requests at go-live minus two weeks | Define and agree on the analytics list in blueprint |
| Cloud edition decision not in scope | RISE / GROW / on-premise debate continues into Realize | Make the cloud edition the first item under scope definition |
| Clean-core compliance plan absent | Partners create in-system modifications; technical debt accumulates | Require clean-core decision per customisation, partner BTP experience verified |

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What should a project scope template include for an SAP implementation?</summary>

Eight core elements plus three RISE / cloud-era additions for a complete scope template on an SAP project in 2026.

Project objectives tied to measurable business outcomes. Scope definition specifying modules, legal entities, countries, integrations, data domains, and cloud edition decision. Explicit exclusions listing what is deliberately out of scope. Data migration scope defining what data transfers, what stays in legacy, and what the cutoff rules are. Non-functional requirements covering system availability, performance, audit logging, and security. Roles and responsibilities with named leads for each workstream and named business counterparts with decision authority (plus SAP customer success contact for RISE programmes). A formal change control process. Assumptions and constraints that the scope is built on.

For RISE / GROW programmes, add: cloud edition decision as first item under scope definition; clean-core compliance plan covering every customisation; SAP customer success engagement terms.

The most skipped element is exclusions. A scope document that does not explicitly call out what is excluded is incomplete. Every undocumented exclusion becomes a potential dispute mid-project.

<details>
<summary>How do you prevent scope creep in SAP projects?</summary>

Scope creep prevention starts in the scope document and is maintained through change control discipline.

The scope document must explicitly list exclusions. If something is not in scope, it needs to be written as out of scope, not just omitted. Omissions get interpreted as inclusions.

Every change request must go through a formal process: written request, impact assessment (timeline, budget, resource), decision by an authorised approver, documentation of the decision. Informal requests accepted informally create undocumented scope additions that compound.

Set a change freeze date before go-live. After that date, new requests go into the post-go-live enhancement backlog. The freeze date needs authority behind it, which means it needs to be agreed and signed off by programme sponsors, not just communicated by the project manager.

For RISE programmes, the clean-core review forum adds an architectural constraint on top of the governance constraint. Every customisation request must satisfy clean-core principles before approval. This strengthens scope discipline by design rather than by willpower alone.

The discipline is not refusing all change. It is making change visible, assessed, and authorised before it is absorbed into delivery.

</details>

<summary>How does clean-core change the SAP scope template in 2026?</summary>

Clean-core compliance is mandatory across SAP Cloud editions since 2024-2025. The scope template needs to call out, explicitly:

- Every identified customisation has a documented decision on whether to configure (no extension), build as a BTP extension (clean-core compliant), or reject

Without these elements, partners without clean-core experience create in-system modifications anyway, producing technical debt that surfaces at the first major upgrade.

For on-premise programmes, clean-core is recommended but not enforced. The scope template can still include the discipline as a voluntary control; the technical debt that builds when clean-core is bypassed surfaces at the first major upgrade regardless of whether the platform enforces it or not.

<details>
<summary>What is data migration scope and why does it need to be in the scope template?</summary>

Data migration scope defines what data gets moved into SAP, from where, and under what rules.

The questions that need explicit answers: which data objects are in scope (materials, customers, vendors, open purchase orders, open sales orders, historical transactions)? What is the cutoff date for transactional data? Does migration include full history or open items only? What are the archiving rules for data that does not migrate?

Data migration scope drives project cost and timeline in two ways. Volume determines the technical migration effort: extraction scripts, transformation logic, validation programmes, load execution. Completeness decisions determine what legacy systems need to stay accessible post-go-live for historical reference, which has cost and governance implications.

Leaving data migration scope vague in the initial scope template creates disputes during build when actual volumes and complexity are being mapped. The conversation is significantly easier before transformation design begins than after.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How should customisations be documented in the project scope template?</summary>

Customisations should be classified, not just listed.

Three categories: essential (the process cannot legally or operationally function without this customisation), important but not critical (efficiency improvement, not a showstopper), unnecessary (a preference or legacy replication that should be challenged).

For each approved customisation, document: what business requirement it addresses, why standard SAP functionality does not meet that requirement, the estimated development effort, the testing impact, the ongoing maintenance implication, and, for cloud editions, the clean-core compliance plan (built as a BTP extension, not as in-system modification).

The scope template should also include the customisation approval process and the change freeze date, after which no new customisation requests are accepted for the current release wave. Without this, late customisation requests compress testing and put go-live at risk.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is non-functional scope and why does it matter in SAP projects?</summary>

Non-functional scope covers the performance, availability, security, and compliance requirements the system must meet, as distinct from the functional processes it must support.

In SAP projects, the non-functional requirements that consistently get dropped from scope and surface as testing blockers include: system availability SLA (what downtime is acceptable and when), response time expectations under peak load, audit trail requirements (which transactions must be logged and for how long), data security and access control requirements by role, and reporting latency expectations.

These are not optional. Audit logging requirements in regulated industries are legal obligations. Performance under peak load affects whether the system is usable at month-end close. Access control requirements affect role design, which affects the user licence structure.

For RISE programmes, the SAP-managed infrastructure SLA needs to be referenced explicitly in the non-functional scope. The client is no longer responsible for infrastructure availability, but the SLA terms govern what SAP commits to and what the escalation path is when it is breached.

Including non-functional requirements in the scope template forces them to be designed for from the start rather than retrofitted after user acceptance testing identifies them as gaps.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When should the scope be formally reviewed and updated?</summary>

Scope should be reviewed at three formal points and updated continuously with version control.

At each SAP Activate phase gate. Scope documented at the start of Explore may not reflect what was discovered during workshops. Before Realize begins, the scope should be revalidated against what was learned and updated where it changed. The same applies at the Realize-to-Deploy transition.

When any material change is approved. Each approved change request should trigger a scope update. The scope document should always reflect the current agreed state of the project, not the state at project kickoff.

When resource, budget, or timeline changes are approved. These changes often have scope implications. If a workstream is cut from the budget, the related scope must be formally removed or deferred.

Version control matters. Every version of the scope document should carry a date, a version number, and a list of what changed from the previous version. This creates the audit trail that protects the project team when stakeholders challenge scope later.

</details>

The best SAP scope template in 2026 is not the longest or the most comprehensively populated. It is the document whose exclusions are as explicit as its inclusions, whose customisation decisions are classified rather than just listed, whose change control process is signed off before configuration starts, and whose cloud edition decision and clean-core compliance plan sit at the centre of the scope rather than as an afterthought. Most scope failures happen because the template was treated as a form to fill rather than a discipline to enforce. Build for the discipline. The format takes care of itself.
