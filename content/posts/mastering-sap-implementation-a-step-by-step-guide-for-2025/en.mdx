SAP implementation is not an IT project. It's a shift in how a business operates. Finance, procurement, HR, supply chain, sales: all of it runs through one system, and every configuration decision has downstream consequences that aren't obvious until you're in UAT.

I've delivered SAP programmes across manufacturing, professional services, aviation, and government. The pattern is consistent: companies that treat implementation as a software installation struggle. Companies that treat it as a business transformation (where the system is the last step, not the first) deliver on time and get the outcomes they promised the board.

SAP S/4HANA is the current flagship. The older ECC platform is still running at many companies but has a known end-of-mainstream-maintenance date. S/4HANA runs in-memory on HANA Cloud, which changes query speed and reporting significantly. Most of what's covered here applies to both.

## What SAP actually covers

The core modules most implementations touch first:

| Module | What it manages |
|---|---|
| FI (Financial Accounting) | General ledger, accounts payable, accounts receivable, asset accounting |
| CO (Controlling) | Cost centers, profit centers, internal orders, management reporting |
| MM (Materials Management) | Procurement, inventory, goods movements, vendor management |
| SD (Sales & Distribution) | Order-to-cash, pricing, shipping, billing |
| PP (Production Planning) | Manufacturing orders, capacity planning, MRP |
| HCM (Human Capital Management) | HR master data, payroll, time management |

Most businesses start with FI/CO and one or two operational modules. The full landscape gets built out over phases.

## The SAP Activate methodology

SAP replaced the old ASAP methodology with SAP Activate. Five phases, each with a defined gate before you move to the next.

| Phase | Focus | Key outputs |
|---|---|---|
| Discover | Understand the business case and fit-gap | System demo, solution scope confirmed |
| Explore | Fit-gap workshops, process design, backlog | Confirmed configuration scope, integration map |
| Realize | Configuration, development, testing | Tested system, data migration validated |
| Deploy | Training, cutover rehearsal, go-live | System live, hypercare team in place |
| Run | Optimization, support, continuous improvement | CoE active, backlog managed |

The gate between Realize and Deploy is the one teams most often skip under schedule pressure. I use a three-day close rehearsal as the readiness gate on every programme. If the finance team can't run a close on the new system in three days, the system isn't ready for go-live. Skipping that gate costs more than the delay.

## Planning before configuration starts

The order matters. Four things need to be done before a single configuration decision is made.

1. **Map current processes end-to-end.** Not what the process is supposed to be: what it actually is. Include the workarounds. I've walked into blueprinting sessions where finance was reconciling in spreadsheets because the legacy ERP couldn't handle intercompany transactions. That's not in any process documentation. It surfaces only when you ask.
2. **Decide standard versus custom for each process.** Identify which processes SAP's standard functionality covers and which require customisation. Standard is always faster. Custom code adds testing cycles, upgrade risk, and maintenance overhead.
3. **Audit data quality before data migration starts.** This is consistently underestimated. A global media company I worked with had 40% of customer records with duplicate or incomplete data. Nobody had run the assessment before the migration workstream started. Three months of cleanup under live system pressure followed.
4. **Assemble the team first, then finalise scope.** The scope you can deliver depends on who's available to configure, test, and own each workstream. Teams that scope first and staff second spend months rebuilding what they overcommitted to.

## Common challenges

| Challenge | What it looks like | What to do about it |
|---|---|---|
| Scope creep | "While we're in there, just add..." requests accumulate | Formal change control from day one; every request gets an impact assessment |
| Data quality | Migration reveals inconsistencies nobody knew existed | Run data profiling 6 months before go-live; clean in source system before migration |
| User resistance | Users revert to Excel within 2 weeks of go-live | Involve end users in design from Explore phase; don't just train, involve |
| Integration failures | Third-party system connections break in UAT | Map interfaces in Explore; test with realistic data volumes early |
| Schedule pressure → skipped testing | Regression cycles cut to meet deadlines | Protect test phases in the schedule; slippage in build must not compress test |
| Team fatigue | Sentiment drops, quality suffers in final stretch | Track morale weekly; a sentiment score above 25% on "feeling overwhelmed" correlates with test defect spikes |

On one programme, the team skipped regression testing to recover three weeks of schedule. Finance couldn't reconcile the books after go-live. Months of cleanup. The three weeks saved cost far more than they were worth.

## What to do before, during, and after

| When | Actions that matter |
|---|---|
| Before go-live | Run a close rehearsal. Validate data migration with reconciliation reports. Train on real processes, not demo scenarios. Confirm rollback plan exists and is tested. |
| During go-live | Increase monitoring. Keep cutover team available 24/7 for the first 72 hours. Capital is at risk during the window between old system shutdown and new system validation. |
| After go-live | Run hypercare for at least 4 weeks. Track support ticket volume by category: it tells you where training failed and where configuration needs adjustment. Plan phase 2 from stabilised baseline, not from go-live enthusiasm. |

## Real examples

A mid-sized manufacturer was dealing with raw material shortages. Production planning was done manually. They had no real-time visibility into what was on hand or what was on order. After implementing SAP PP with proper MRP configuration, raw material shortages dropped by 50% in the first six months. The result came from the process redesign that preceded the system configuration, not from the technology alone.

A global professional services firm took over two weeks to close month-end. The bottleneck was manual consolidation across multiple entities. After S/4HANA implementation with proper intercompany configuration and automated consolidation, close came down to just over one week. The CFO said the numbers "felt believable" for the first time. That came from data quality work done six months before go-live, not from the system itself.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is SAP used for?</summary>

SAP integrates core business functions (finance, procurement, supply chain, HR, sales) into a single system. Instead of separate platforms that don't talk to each other, everything runs from one data model.

What that means in practice: a purchase order raised in procurement automatically updates inventory levels, triggers an accounts payable entry, and flows into management reporting. No manual re-entry. No reconciliation between systems. The accuracy of your reporting depends on the accuracy of the transactions. That's why data quality and process design matter more than the software configuration.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long does an SAP implementation take?</summary>

Scope and team composition are the two variables that move the timeline most. A focused S/4HANA implementation covering FI/CO and one operational module for a single company can be done in 6-9 months. A global rollout across multiple entities, multiple modules, and multiple languages runs 18-36 months.

Factors that consistently extend timelines: data quality problems discovered late, scope added mid-project without timeline adjustment, key roles filled part-time, and testing cycles compressed to recover earlier slippage. All of these are controllable if you address them in planning.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the 5 phases of SAP Activate?</summary>

Discover: confirm the business case, scope, and system fit. Run a demo or prototype against your priority processes before committing to full implementation.

Explore: run fit-gap workshops with process owners. Design the future-state processes. Build the backlog of configuration tasks, integrations, and custom developments. The output is a confirmed scope with no unresolved gaps.

Realize: configure the system, develop extensions, migrate data, and run integration testing. This is the longest phase. Gate exit requires successful regression test.

Deploy: train users on real processes. Run cutover rehearsals. Get sign-offs. Execute go-live with a war-room team in place.

Run: hypercare support, optimization, and handover to the CoE. The system runs; the team learns to own it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the most common reasons SAP implementations fail?</summary>

Three root causes I see on almost every troubled programme.

First: process work skipped. Teams configure SAP against broken legacy processes because nobody wanted to slow down for redesign. The system goes live and makes the problems faster, not smaller.

Second: data quality ignored until cutover. By then, cleaning data takes place under live-system pressure with no time to do it properly.

Third: change management treated as training. Training tells people how to click. Change management makes them want to. Implementations where users were involved in process design from Explore phase consistently outperform those where users were handed a training deck two weeks before go-live.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I choose the right SAP implementation partner?</summary>

Four things matter more than company size or SAP certification count.

Industry experience at your scale. A partner who has delivered for manufacturers at your revenue level has already made the expensive mistakes on someone else's project. Ask for names, ask to speak with the client.

Named senior involvement. The person presenting in the pitch should be the person running the programme. Ask directly: "Will you be on-site?" If the answer is "I'll be engaged," that means no.

Independence from software revenue. Partners who earn commissions on licence or cloud subscription sales have an incentive to recommend scope. That incentive doesn't go away during implementation.

Post-go-live model. Ask specifically what happens 6 months after go-live. Who owns support? At what rate? The answer tells you whether knowledge transfer was built into the plan.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What happens after go-live?</summary>

Go-live is not the end. It's the start of a different kind of work.

Hypercare runs for at least four weeks: full team available, daily review of open issues, monitoring of system performance and data quality. Support ticket categories in week one are the most honest signal you'll get about where training was insufficient or configuration was wrong.

After hypercare, the Centre of Excellence takes over. The CoE owns enhancements, upgrade planning, user training for new joiners, and the governance that prevents uncoordinated changes from degrading the system. Companies that don't build the CoE during implementation usually spend the following two years paying consulting fees for work that should have been internal.

Phase 2 planning should start from the stabilised baseline, not from go-live enthusiasm. The scope that was deferred to phase 2 needs to be re-evaluated against what the business actually needs now, not what it wanted 18 months ago.

</details>
