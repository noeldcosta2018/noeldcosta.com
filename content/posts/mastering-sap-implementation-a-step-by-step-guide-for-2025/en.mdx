An SAP implementation is the programme that moves a company's finance, procurement, supply chain, sales and HR onto one SAP system, usually S/4HANA. It runs in six phases under SAP's Activate method, and it succeeds or fails on work done before anyone configures anything: process design, data quality and the right team.

This guide is for executives and programme leads about to start one. It walks through the phases, what each must produce, the planning that comes first, and what has changed for programmes starting now. If you read one section, read "Planning before configuration starts".

After 25 years implementing ERP, I have seen the same pattern again and again. Companies that treat implementation as a software installation struggle. Companies that treat the system as the last step, after the process work, deliver on time and get the outcomes they promised the board.

## What SAP actually covers

S/4HANA is SAP's current ERP and runs on the SAP HANA in-memory database. Older ECC systems are still running in many companies, but [ECC mainstream maintenance ends on 31 December 2027](https://news.sap.com/2020/02/sap-s4hana-maintenance-2040-clarity-choice-sap-business-suite-7/), with optional extended maintenance to the end of 2030 at a higher fee.

The core modules most implementations touch first:

| Module | What it manages |
|---|---|
| FI (Financial Accounting) | General ledger, accounts payable, accounts receivable, asset accounting |
| CO (Controlling) | Cost centres, profit centres, internal orders, management reporting |
| MM (Materials Management) | Procurement, inventory, goods movements, vendor management |
| SD (Sales and Distribution) | Order-to-cash, pricing, shipping, billing |
| PP (Production Planning) | Manufacturing orders, capacity planning, MRP |
| HCM (Human Capital Management) | HR master data, payroll, time management |

Most businesses start with FI/CO and one or two operational modules. The rest gets built out in later phases.

## The SAP Activate methodology

SAP Activate replaced the older ASAP method. It has six phases, each with a gate you clear before moving on.

<stepper title="The six SAP Activate phases" steps="Discover|Prepare|Explore|Realize|Deploy|Run" bodies="Confirm the business case and test priority processes against a trial or demo system.|Mobilise: team, governance, scope document, plan and system access.|Fit-to-standard workshops with process owners. Build the backlog of configuration, integrations and extensions.|Configure, extend, migrate data and test. The longest phase. Exit needs a clean regression run.|Train users on real processes, rehearse cutover, go live with a war room in place.|Hypercare, optimisation and handover to the Centre of Excellence."></stepper>

This table is the version I pin to the wall of the programme office: what each phase must produce, who owns it, and what has to be true before the next phase starts.

| Phase | Must produce | Owner | Gate to move on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discover | Business case, target scope, deployment choice | Sponsor and CFO | Funding approved |
| Prepare | Scope document, plan, governance, team in place | Programme director | Sponsor signs scope |
| Explore | Fit-to-standard results, backlog, extension decisions | Solution architect with process owners | No unresolved gaps |
| Realize | Configured and tested system, migrated test data | Functional and technical leads | Clean regression run; data reconciles |
| Deploy | Trained users, rehearsed cutover, go/no-go pack | Cutover manager | Three-day close rehearsal passed |
| Run | Hypercare log, CoE handover, phase 2 backlog | Service delivery lead | No open P1/P2; CoE accepts |

The templates behind each phase are in my [SAP Activate templates guide](/best-sap-implementation-templates-activate-2024/).

### The three-day close rehearsal gate

The gate between Realize and Deploy is the one teams most often skip under schedule pressure. I advise running a three-day close rehearsal before the actual go-live. If finance cannot close the books on the new system, your data migration is not ready, no matter what IT says. Skipping that gate costs more than the delay it would have caused.

## Planning before configuration starts

The order matters. Four things need doing before a single configuration decision is made.

<explainer-flow title="Four things before the first configuration decision" caption="The system is the last step. Process, data and people work comes first." steps="Map current processes|Decide standard or extension|Audit data quality|Staff the team, then fix scope" notes="As they really run, workarounds included|Standard is almost always faster|Before data migration starts|Who is available decides what you can deliver" result="Only now does configuration start"></explainer-flow>

### Map current processes as they really run

Not what the process is supposed to be. What it actually is, workarounds included. The workarounds are where the requirements nobody wrote down are hiding.

### Decide standard versus extension for each process

Identify which processes SAP's standard functionality covers and which need extending. Standard is almost always faster. Every extension adds testing cycles, upgrade risk and maintenance. Under SAP's clean core guidance, each extension also has to be placed somewhere deliberate, which makes the decision more consequential, not less.

### Audit data quality before data migration starts

The most underestimated workstream. I have seen companies spend months fixing reports because outdated customer records were loaded unchecked. One client had over 18,000 duplicate customer entries, and fixing them after go-live disrupted billing for weeks.

### Assemble the team first, then finalise scope

The scope you can deliver depends on who is available to configure, test and own each workstream. Teams that scope first and staff second spend months rebuilding what they overcommitted to.

## Common challenges and what to do about them

| Challenge | What it looks like | What to do about it |
|---|---|---|
| Scope creep | "While we are in there, just add..." requests pile up | Formal change control from day one; every request gets an impact assessment |
| Data quality | Migration reveals inconsistencies nobody knew existed | Profile data six months before go-live; clean in the source system |
| User resistance | Users drift back to Excel within two weeks of go-live | Involve end users in design from Explore; involvement, not just training |
| Integration failures | Third-party connections break in UAT | Map interfaces in Explore; test with realistic volumes early |
| Test cycles cut | Regression shortened to hit a date | Protect test phases; slippage in build must not compress test |
| Team fatigue | Sentiment drops, defect rates rise in the final stretch | Track fatigue with a simple weekly sentiment index; in my experience, when it crosses 25%, test defect rates spike |

I remember one case where a company skipped small regression tests to speed things up. A week later, finance couldn't reconcile key reports. Months of cleanup followed. It wasn't a major system flaw, just an avoidable oversight.

### Before, during and after go-live

Before go-live: run the close rehearsal, validate migrated data with reconciliation reports, train on real processes rather than demo scenarios, and test the rollback plan. Walk the steering committee through the go/no-go criteria and get explicit sign-off, not implicit nodding.

During go-live: increase monitoring and keep the cutover team available around the clock for the first 72 hours. Decisions made in those hours decide whether hypercare opens with confidence or with a queue.

After go-live: run hypercare for at least four weeks. Track support tickets by category; they tell you where training failed and where configuration needs adjusting. Plan phase 2 from the stabilised baseline. Scope deferred 18 months ago needs re-checking against what the business needs now.

## Two programmes that worked

A mid-sized manufacturer kept running out of raw materials. Procurement blamed the planners; the planners blamed spreadsheets nobody trusted. We replaced that setup with S/4HANA and relied heavily on SAP PP with proper MRP configuration. Stock levels moved from guesswork to real-time data, purchase orders triggered on need, and after six months shortages had dropped by over 50 percent. That surprised even the sceptics. The result came from the process redesign that preceded the configuration. PP without the process work would have produced faster wrong answers. Finance gained too: month-end was faster, and the CFO said the numbers "felt believable" for the first time in a while.

A global professional services firm had a different problem. Each country ran its own finance platform, nothing reconciled, and reports were rebuilt by hand every month. We rolled out SAP Finance in stages under a hands-on steering committee. Month-end close dropped from over two weeks to just over one, regional reports finally matched, and even the auditors had fewer concerns.

## What has changed for programmes starting now

A guide written for 2022 does not survive contact with a 2026 buyer. Four changes need to be designed in from kickoff.

### Cloud editions are the default

SAP now sells two cloud ERP editions: SAP Cloud ERP (the public edition, formerly S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition) and SAP Cloud ERP Private (the private edition). [RISE with SAP](https://news.sap.com/2025/04/sap-cloud-erp-private-package-accelerate-transformation/) packages the private edition with SAP-run operations and a transformation toolchain that includes SAP Signavio, SAP LeanIX and SAP Cloud ALM. SAP GROW is the package for mid-sized companies on the public edition.

The edition decision now sits above the old rollout debates. Big bang versus phased, and greenfield versus brownfield versus selective, are choices you make inside the edition, not instead of it. If you are still on ECC and need more time, SAP sells an ERP private edition transition option for 2031 to 2033, but SAP is clear it is a paid transition offer, not a maintenance extension.

### Clean core is graded, not binary

In August 2025 SAP introduced [four clean core levels, A to D](https://news.sap.com/2025/08/extend-sap-s4hana-cloud-right-way-clean-clear/). Level A uses only released, stable APIs, either side by side on SAP BTP or in the system with ABAP Cloud. Level B allows classic APIs and technologies that are still considered clean. Level C needs special measures. Level D is not clean.

The public edition only allows level A extensions. The private edition and on-premise allow classic extensions, so the discipline there comes from governance, not from the platform blocking you. The practical point for a programme: decide the level and location of every extension in Explore, and have a named person who can say no. Partners without experience of SAP BTP and ABAP Cloud create level C and D debt from week one.

### Joule and SAP Build Code in the delivery team

Joule now sits inside the SAP Activate Roadmap Viewer and SAP Cloud ALM, where it answers task questions and drafts content from the methodology. SAP Build Code, generally available since 2024, uses Joule to generate application logic, data models and tests for Java and JavaScript extensions on SAP BTP. SAP has added similar generative AI help for ABAP developers.

The honest view: AI in SAP programmes is real, but data quality decides the value. Clean process documentation and clean master data produce useful output. Dirty data produces confident noise. None of it removes the need for a person who owns each decision.

### What this means for a programme starting now

The playbook still works. The phases still apply and the order of work still matters. What has changed is the edition decision, the extension discipline and the tooling for the team. A programme that absorbs those at kickoff treats them as design constraints. One that ignores them spends its first three months finding out what changed, usually from the partner's change requests.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is SAP used for?</summary>

SAP runs core business functions (finance, procurement, supply chain, HR, sales) in one system with one data model.

In practice, a goods receipt updates inventory, triggers the accounts payable process and flows into management reporting without re-entry. Reporting is only as accurate as the transactions underneath it, which is why process design and data quality matter more than configuration.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long does an SAP implementation take?</summary>

Scope and team are the two variables that move the timeline most. A focused S/4HANA implementation covering FI/CO and one operational module for a single company can take 6 to 9 months. A global rollout across many entities, modules and languages runs 18 to 36 months.

What extends timelines: data problems found late, scope added without moving the date, key roles filled part-time, and test cycles cut to recover earlier slippage. All of these are controllable in planning.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the six phases of SAP Activate?</summary>

Discover (business case and fit), Prepare (team, governance, plan), Explore (fit-to-standard workshops and backlog), Realize (configure, extend, migrate, test), Deploy (train, rehearse cutover, go live) and Run (hypercare and handover to the Centre of Excellence).

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the most common reasons SAP implementations fail?</summary>

Three root causes appear on almost every troubled programme. Process work skipped, so SAP is configured against broken legacy processes. Data quality ignored until cutover, when there is no time to fix it properly. Change management treated as training: training shows people how to click, change management makes them want to.

A fourth is newer: a partner that builds extensions without a clean core plan, leaving debt that surfaces at the first major upgrade.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I choose the right SAP implementation partner?</summary>

Industry experience at your scale, and references you can actually call. Named senior involvement: the person in the pitch should run the programme. Independence: partners who earn on licence or subscription sales have an incentive to recommend more scope. Clean core experience: ask how many SAP BTP and ABAP Cloud extensions they have built, and ask to see them.

One more rule. The partner who will deliver the work should not write your business case. Their incentive is to start. Yours is to finish.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What happens after go-live?</summary>

Hypercare runs for at least four weeks, with the full team available and daily review of open issues. Ticket categories in week one are the most honest signal of where training fell short or configuration was wrong.

After hypercare, the Centre of Excellence takes over enhancements, upgrade planning, training for new joiners and change governance. Companies that skip building the CoE during the programme usually spend the next two years paying consultants for work that should be internal.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is RISE with SAP and is it right for my organisation?</summary>

RISE with SAP is SAP's subscription package for SAP Cloud ERP Private: the software, SAP-managed infrastructure and operations, and a toolchain for process analysis, architecture and lifecycle management. Your partner still delivers the implementation.

It suits larger organisations moving off ECC who want one SAP contract for platform and operations. Mid-sized companies that can work close to standard should look at SAP GROW on the public edition. RISE is a weaker fit where regulation demands customer-managed infrastructure, or where heavy custom code cannot be cleaned up in the time available.

</details>
