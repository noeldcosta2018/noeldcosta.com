# Why SAP data migration fails and how to fix it

A manufacturing company I worked with spent 18 months and $4.5 million on an SAP implementation. The configuration team built a solid system. Testing ran for three months. The business was trained. On cutover day, the data migration failed.

Customer records were missing or arrived with duplicated account numbers. Inventory balances in the new system did not match the balances in the legacy system. The finance team could not complete the opening period entries because the vendor balances were incomplete. The CEO halted the go-live after four hours.

The SAP configuration was fine. The training was adequate. The failure was in the data, specifically in the difference between what the business thought its data looked like and what it actually looked like.

That distinction is the starting point for understanding why SAP data migration fails.

## Why SAP data migration is different

SAP's data model is more complex than most organisations anticipate. It is not a flat import. The system has strict interdependencies between records. A material master record links to plant-specific data in MARC, to valuation data in MBEW, to MRP parameters in MDMA. Load the material header without the dependent records and the material exists in the system but cannot be used in transactions.

The dependency chain extends further. Sales orders reference customer masters, which reference credit management data, which references company code assignments. A customer record that is incomplete in a way that a legacy system tolerated will fail validation in SAP.

And then there is volume. A client I worked with in the Gulf estimated they had approximately 50,000 material records. When we extracted the legacy system and counted all variants and plant-specific records, the actual number was closer to 500,000. The migration plan had been built for 50,000 records. The timeline, the resources, and the number of mock load cycles had all been calibrated to that estimate.

That was not a data migration problem. It was a scoping problem that became a data migration problem.

## Why data quality is discovered late

The data quality assessment at the start of a project is almost always based on a business description of the data, not on an actual examination of it. The finance director says "our vendor master is well maintained." The warehouse manager says "our materials are mostly clean, with a few duplicates." These descriptions are based on impressions, not analysis.

The first extraction reveals the truth. Vendor records that were supposed to be cleaned up three years ago and were not. Material records for products that were discontinued in 2019 but never deactivated. Customer addresses with inconsistent country codes across different transactions. Bank details missing on vendors who are paid by EFT.

Each of these requires a business decision, not just a technical fix. Only the accounts payable team can decide which of two duplicate vendor records is the correct one. Only the procurement team can decide which obsolete materials should be deactivated versus archived. These decisions take time, and they require the people who know the data.

If the data quality assessment is not based on a real extract and a real analysis, the project plan is built on assumptions that will not survive contact with the actual data.

## The five most common data migration mistakes

### Treating migration as a late-stage technical activity

Data migration belongs in every phase of the project. In Explore, the data scope is defined: which objects, which legacy systems, which volumes, which quality state. In Realize, the migration templates are built and the first trial loads run. In Deploy, mock loads are executed and reconciled. In Run, the cutover migration is the final execution of a process that has been rehearsed multiple times.

Projects that start migration work in the Deploy phase are starting it six months too late. By then, the data quality issues that should have been identified and resolved during Realize are discovered in the first mock load, weeks before cutover.

### Insufficient business involvement

Data migration is a technical activity in execution. It is a business activity in decision-making. The list of which vendor records are duplicates cannot be produced by the IT team. The decision about which customer accounts to migrate active versus as history is a finance decision. The cleanup of obsolete material records requires input from procurement, warehouse, and product management.

Programmes that staff the data migration workstream entirely with technical resources and treat business involvement as a sign-off activity at the end produce data that is technically loaded but commercially wrong.

### Planning for too few mock load cycles

A well-run SAP data migration typically requires four to six mock load cycles before the cutover load. The first cycle reveals the structural mapping gaps. The second cycle validates the fixes and reveals the data quality issues that the mapping fixes exposed. The third and fourth cycles work through the business rule decisions and the edge cases. The final mock is a full dress rehearsal of the cutover sequence.

Projects that plan for two mock cycles are planning for the optimistic scenario where the data quality is good and the mapping is clean. That scenario is rare. More cycles are not a sign of a troubled migration. They are a sign of a properly planned one.

### Not reconciling trial load results

Loading data into the SAP system is not validation. Validation requires comparing what was loaded against what was expected: record counts, financial totals, open item balances, and process smoke tests that confirm the loaded data can be used in transactions.

I have worked on programmes where the trial load was considered complete when the load job finished with no errors. The reconciliation, which would have revealed that the vendor open item balances were 15% different from the legacy balances, was not done until the mock cutover three weeks later. Three weeks of investigation time that should have been used for other things was consumed chasing a reconciliation gap that the trial load had already produced.

### Cutover migration that is different from the trial loads

The cutover migration should be the final execution of a process that has been fully rehearsed. The extraction scripts, the transformation logic, the loading sequence, the validation checks, and the timing should all be identical to what was done in the final mock.

Projects that change the migration process for the actual cutover introduce untested risk at the highest-pressure point in the programme. Any deviation from the tested process is a potential failure mode that has not been validated.

## What a working migration strategy looks like

**Early data extract.** In the Prepare phase, extract the data. Not a sample. All of it. Run a data quality assessment on the actual records: completeness, accuracy, consistency, and duplicate analysis. Use the results to scope the cleanup work and to calibrate the migration timeline.

**Object-by-object migration mapping.** For each data object in scope (customer master, vendor master, material master, open purchase orders, open sales orders, financial open items, inventory balances), document the source-to-target field mapping, the transformation rules, the validation criteria, and the business rules for handling exceptions.

**Structured mock load programme.** Plan for a minimum of four mock cycles. Each cycle has a specific objective: structural validation, data quality analysis, business rule testing, full dress rehearsal. Each cycle has a reconciliation requirement before it is considered complete.

**Business data stewards.** Assign named business owners for each data domain. The finance team owns vendor and customer financial data. Procurement owns material master. Warehouse management owns inventory. Each data steward is accountable for the cleanup decisions in their domain and for signing off that the trial load results are acceptable.

**Cutover data freeze and sequence.** Define the data freeze point: the date after which no changes are made to the legacy system data that is in scope for migration. Document the cutover sequence: extract, transform, load, validate, business sign-off, go/no-go decision. The sequence is the same for the cutover as it was for the final mock.

## Migration tools

**SAP Migration Cockpit (LTMC/LTMOM)** is SAP's native migration tool for S/4HANA. It provides standard migration templates for the main object types, validates data against SAP's own business logic before loading, and provides a staging area that allows business review before data is committed to the production system. For standard object types with SAP-standard structures, it is the right starting point.

**SAP Data Services** is SAP's ETL platform for complex migration scenarios: high data volumes, complex transformation logic, non-standard source system structures, or migration programmes that require a reusable framework for ongoing data management.

**Third-party ETL tools** (Informatica, Talend, Microsoft SSIS) are used where the organisation has existing investment in these platforms or where the migration complexity requires capabilities beyond SAP's native tooling.

For most standard S/4HANA implementations, SAP Migration Cockpit covers the majority of objects. Complex scenarios involving high volumes, highly customised legacy systems, or non-standard source structures typically require Data Services or a third-party ETL tool.

<details>
<summary>What is SAP data migration?</summary>

SAP data migration is the process of extracting data from legacy systems, transforming it to match SAP's data structures and business rules, and loading it into the SAP system as part of an implementation or system conversion project.

The objects typically migrated include: customer master and vendor master data, material master data with plant and storage location assignments, open purchase orders and sales orders, inventory balances, financial open items (outstanding invoices, payments in process), and fixed asset data.

The complexity of SAP data migration comes from three factors. SAP's data model has strict interdependencies between objects: a material must exist before a purchase order can reference it, a customer must exist before a sales order can reference it. These dependencies mean the load sequence is tightly constrained. SAP also applies business validation rules during data loading that legacy systems often do not enforce, so data that was valid in the source system may fail validation in SAP. Finally, the volume and quality of data in most legacy systems is worse than the initial assessment suggests, which means the actual effort typically exceeds the estimate.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the main types of data migration in SAP?</summary>

Three approaches account for most SAP migration scenarios.

Full migration: all historical and open data is migrated from the legacy system to SAP. Used where the business needs access to full transaction history in the new system. Higher volume and higher complexity, but complete data continuity.

Open item migration: only current open items are migrated. Historical transaction data remains in the legacy system and is accessed via a read-only archive or a parallel system. Lower migration complexity and volume, but the business must run two systems in parallel for historical queries.

Selective data transition: a hybrid approach specific to SAP system conversions (brownfield). Some data moves as-is from ECC to S/4HANA. Selective objects are migrated fresh. Used where the ECC data quality is high in some domains and problematic in others.

The right approach depends on regulatory requirements for data access, the quality and volume of the legacy data, and the business's tolerance for running parallel systems post-go-live.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is SAP Migration Cockpit (LTMC) and when should you use it?</summary>

SAP Migration Cockpit, accessed via transaction LTMC (and its successor LTMOM), is SAP's native data migration tool for S/4HANA implementations. It provides pre-built migration templates for standard SAP object types (customer master, vendor master, material master, open purchase orders, financial balances, and others), validates data against SAP business rules in a staging area before loading, and produces error reports with specific field-level information on why records failed.

Use LTMC when: the implementation is a standard S/4HANA project using SAP's standard object types; the data volumes are manageable (hundreds of thousands of records rather than tens of millions); and the transformation logic is relatively straightforward.

It is less appropriate for: very high volume migrations where LTMC's performance may not meet cutover timing requirements; complex legacy source structures that require significant ETL processing before they conform to the LTMC template format; or organisations that have existing investment in a third-party ETL platform and want to reuse it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you handle data quality issues in SAP migration?</summary>

The sequence is: identify, decide, clean, validate.

Identification comes from running the data through the migration templates and reviewing the error output. SAP Migration Cockpit produces field-level error reports. Data Services produces exception reports. Either way, the output is a list of records that failed validation and the specific reason for each failure.

Decision is the business step. Technical errors (missing required fields, wrong format) can often be resolved by the migration team. Business errors (which of two duplicate vendors is correct, whether an inactive material should be archived or deactivated) require a business decision from the data owner.

Cleaning is the technical implementation of the decision. This may happen in the source system, in the staging area, or in the transformation layer depending on what the data owner has decided and what the migration architecture allows.

Validation confirms that the fix resolved the error and did not introduce new ones. Re-run the affected records through the migration process and reconcile the output against the expected result.

The most important discipline: track every error, every decision, and every fix. The data migration log is the audit trail for every data quality decision made during the project. If a balance is wrong after go-live, the migration log is where the investigation starts.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How many mock loads should a SAP data migration project plan for?</summary>

A minimum of four, with the final one being a full cutover rehearsal.

The first mock load is a structural validation run. The purpose is to identify mapping gaps, format errors, and dependency sequencing issues. The result is usually a high error rate that is expected and informative.

The second mock load tests the fixes from the first run and reveals the data quality issues that the mapping corrections exposed. Error rates should be lower but will still be significant.

The third mock load works through the business rule edge cases: how exceptions are handled, what happens with records that do not cleanly map to SAP structures, and whether the business data owners are satisfied with how specific categories of records are being handled.

The fourth mock load (or final mock, which may be the fifth or sixth on complex projects) is the full dress rehearsal. It uses the same extraction scripts, the same transformation logic, the same loading sequence, and the same timing as the actual cutover. The reconciliation results from this run are the baseline the cutover team will compare against on go-live day.

Projects that plan for two mock loads are planning for data migration to go well. Planning for four to six is planning for data migration to go right.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What causes SAP data migration to fail at cutover?</summary>

Five patterns account for most cutover failures.

Insufficient mock load cycles. The cutover is the first time the migration runs at full volume with production-quality data. Issues that would have been found and resolved in a fourth or fifth mock load appear for the first time during the cutover window.

Cutover sequence that was never fully tested. The extraction, transformation, loading, and validation steps run in a specific sequence with dependencies between them. A sequence that has not been tested end-to-end will have timing and dependency issues that are not visible in individual step tests.

Data changes after the final mock. Between the final mock load and the cutover, the legacy data continues to change: new vendors are added, open orders are updated, inventory moves happen. The delta migration that captures these changes is often the least-tested part of the process.

Reconciliation gaps that were not resolved. Financial opening balances that are 3% different from the legacy system, inventory balances that are off by a handful of records: these gaps look small in testing and catastrophic on go-live day when the finance team cannot reconcile the opening period.

Business sign-off based on extrapolation rather than validation. "The first 1,000 records looked fine, so the rest are probably fine" is not a validation methodology. Go-live readiness for data migration requires reconciliation of counts and totals, not a spot check.

</details>
