SAP works in manufacturing when four things are right before go-live: clean master data across plants, routings and BOMs that have been tested in every valid combination, a working connection between SAP and the shop floor, and operators who have practised their own transactions on their own work centres. Most manufacturing SAP delays trace back to one of those four.

This piece is for operations directors, plant managers and programme leads planning S/4HANA in a factory environment. It covers the modules you need, the integration points, the seven challenges that cause delays, and a master data readiness checklist with owners. If you are still choosing a system, start with my comparison of the [best ERP for manufacturing](/best-erp-for-manufacturing/).

The pattern shows up again and again. I saw an automotive supplier discover the same part defined five different ways across its plants and delay go-live by three months sorting it out. A metal fabrication company thought mapping more than 200 routing combinations for one product family would take six weeks. It took twelve. An electronics manufacturer had BOMs 15 levels deep and engineering change history that nearly derailed the data migration.

## The modules you actually need

### SAP PP (Production Planning)

PP is where manufacturing lives in SAP: bills of materials, work centres, routings and production orders. Configured well, it connects sales forecasts to real production capacity, so customer commitments reflect what the shop floor can deliver.

A furniture company I worked with cut production planning time by 60% after getting PP right. The change wasn't the technology. PP connected demand and capacity in a way manual planning never could. My [SAP PP guide](/sap-pp-production-planning/) goes deeper on configuration.

### SAP MM (Materials Management)

MM tracks inventory, purchasing and material movements. Its link to PP is what ends emergency procurement: when PP creates a production order, MM generates the purchasing requirements, and reservations happen as the order is released.

### SAP PM (Plant Maintenance)

Unplanned downtime is expensive. PM handles preventive maintenance scheduling, repairs and equipment history. One of my automotive clients cut unplanned downtime by 35% in the first year after implementing PM. That goes straight to throughput and unit cost.

### SAP QM (Quality Management)

QM manages inspections, quality certificates and defects. Its value is integration: a quality failure can block a production order, notify a supplier or trigger a maintenance request. It is a control that runs across processes, not a standalone quality system.

### SAP PP-PI (Production Planning for Process Industries)

For batch manufacturing in food, chemicals and pharmaceuticals, PP-PI adds what discrete manufacturing doesn't need: process orders driven by master recipes, co-products and by-products, batch management and variable yield.

### SAP Digital Manufacturing

SAP's manufacturing execution system, formerly called SAP Digital Manufacturing Cloud. It connects S/4HANA to the shop floor for production execution, labour tracking and machine integration. This is where operators interact with SAP during production, and where the gap between a working system and a bypassed one opens up.

## Integration points for manufacturing

| Integration point | What is needed | SAP capability |
|---|---|---|
| ERP to shop floor | Order and material status between planning and execution | [SAP Digital Manufacturing](https://help.sap.com/docs/sap-digital-manufacturing) integrated with S/4HANA |
| Machines (SCADA, PLC) | Machine status, alerts, counts | SAP Plant Connectivity using OPC UA or MQTT |
| Supply chain | Supplier capacity, advance shipping notices, logistics visibility | SAP Business Network, SAP Integration Suite |
| Warehouse to production | Staging and stock updates aligned to production orders | SAP EWM integrated with production supply |
| Quality | Inspection results and deviations | SAP QM integrated with PP and logistics |
| CAD and PLM | Engineering BOM changes into production | SAP Engineering Control Center with SAP PLM |
| Sensor data and analytics | Telemetry for condition monitoring | SAP Digital Manufacturing or a dedicated IIoT platform (SAP retired its standalone SAP IoT service in 2023) |

## The seven challenges that cause failures

### 1. Routing complexity

Manufacturing processes rarely follow a straight line. Parallel operations, alternative routings, rework loops and conditional steps are hard to map into SAP.

The metal fabrication company above had over 200 routing combinations for one product family. Mapping wasn't the hard part. Testing every valid combination to confirm correct behaviour was. Put that testing time in the plan. You will need it.

### 2. BOM structure and depth

BOMs can reach ten or more levels, with hundreds of components, alternative materials, lot-size-dependent quantities and engineering revision history that has to survive.

The electronics company with 15-level BOMs needed its engineering change history intact for regulatory reasons. That migration was the most expensive part of the project. BOM migration is not lift-and-shift. It needs mapping, validation and restructuring to fit SAP.

### 3. Shop floor integration

Connecting SAP to scanners, RFID, test instruments and machine controllers almost always needs integration work beyond standard content. Teams without shop floor experience create problems that surface in production rather than in testing.

### 4. Cutover of in-flight production

Open work orders, accumulated labour hours and partly consumed materials all have to be handled at cutover. Set a firm cutoff date. Close as many open orders as possible before it. For orders that must move mid-process, define the mapping rules before migration starts.

In regulated industries, decide which historical records must stay accessible. Keeping the legacy system read-only for compliance records avoids migration effort but means running two systems. The right answer depends on how often people need the data and for how long.

### 5. Master data, the most common cause of go-live delays

Materials, BOMs, routings and work centres must be clean and consistent across every plant before go-live. This is where the automotive supplier lost three months. Finding the same part defined five ways during system testing is late. By then the schedule has no room to absorb the cleanup.

The fix is a data steward per plant and functional area, accountable for resolving inconsistencies before configuration begins. Treat it as a programme workstream, not a testing prerequisite. The [SAP data migration failure patterns](/why-sap-data-migration-fails-and-how-to-fix-it/) article covers the wider migration risks.

### 6. Reporting and analytics

Factories generate plenty of data. Turning it into useful measures (OEE, rejection rates, cost per unit) means knowing what the business needs and where in SAP that data is actually recorded.

Define reporting requirements in design. Don't leave them for post-go-live optimisation.

### 7. Change management on the shop floor

This is consistently underestimated on every manufacturing programme.

Operators who have worked the same way for 15 years are being asked to use tablets, scan barcodes and enter data while the line is running. The resistance is real and rational: the system interrupts a flow they have optimised for years.

I have seen technically sound implementations fail on the shop floor because training demonstrated the system instead of simulating the job. Operators should practise the exact transactions they will use, on their own work centre, with their own materials.

When adoption fails on the floor, the data stops reflecting what is happening in production, and every benefit the rest of the programme promised goes with it.

## Master data readiness checklist

Use this before configuration starts and again before each mock load.

| Object | Check | Owner | Ready when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material master | One definition per part across plants; units of measure agreed | Data steward per plant | Duplicate report is empty |
| Bills of materials | Structure fits SAP levels; alternatives and lot-size rules mapped | Engineering with PP lead | Sample BOMs explode correctly in test |
| Routings | Every valid combination listed and tested | Production engineering | All combinations pass in the test system |
| Work centres | Capacities, shifts and cost rates agreed | Plant controller | Capacity plan matches the plant's reality |
| Engineering change history | Retention rule decided; records mapped or archived | Quality and compliance | Auditor agrees the approach |
| Open production orders | Cutoff date set; transfer rules defined | Production planning lead | Only orders that must transfer remain open |

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What SAP modules are essential for a manufacturing company?</summary>

For discrete manufacturing: PP (Production Planning), MM (Materials Management), QM (Quality Management) and PM (Plant Maintenance). For batch and process industries, PP-PI replaces or extends PP. SAP Digital Manufacturing connects these to the shop floor.

The value is in the integration: PP triggering MM purchasing, QM blocking orders on quality failures, PM feeding equipment availability into scheduling.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What makes SAP manufacturing implementations harder than other industries?</summary>

Three things. Production complexity: routings, multi-level BOMs and scheduling logic take longer to map and test than teams estimate. Shop floor integration: SAP has to talk to physical equipment, which needs specialist skills. Operational change management: shop floor adoption failures don't show up as system errors, they show up as data that doesn't match reality.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How complex is BOM data migration for a manufacturing SAP implementation?</summary>

It depends on depth, component volume, revision history and whether alternative materials and lot-size rules must be preserved. Electronics and defence suppliers regularly have BOMs 10 to 15 levels deep. Assign BOM data stewards early, validate structures against SAP in design, and plan specific time for validation after migration.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the biggest data migration risks in manufacturing SAP projects?</summary>

Master data that differs between plants, in-flight production orders at cutover, historical quality records that regulators expect you to keep, and BOM revision history that is underestimated in scope. In regulated environments that history has legal standing; truncating it creates compliance risk.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How should shop floor change management be handled in a manufacturing SAP rollout?</summary>

Train the job, not the system. Operators practise their specific transactions on their specific work centre, with data from their area, before go-live. Involve shop floor leads in design so the configuration follows how work really flows. After go-live, keep trained floor support available for the first weeks so workarounds don't set in.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does SAP PP-PI handle that standard SAP PP does not?</summary>

PP is built for discrete manufacturing: distinct items made by assembly or machining. PP-PI handles batch, recipe-driven production in food, beverages, chemicals and pharmaceuticals. It uses process orders and master recipes, manages co-products and by-products, supports batch management with traceability, and tracks variable yield against theoretical yield. In pharma and food, the batch record it produces is a compliance requirement.

</details>
