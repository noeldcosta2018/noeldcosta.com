Choose an SAP implementation partner in the USA by fit, not by tier. Tier 1 global SIs suit multi-region, heavily regulated programmes. Tier 2 specialists suit mid-market and single-industry programmes that want scale without Tier 1 overhead. Tier 3 boutiques suit well-defined scope where you want senior people in the room every day. Whichever tier you pick, the decisive question is who will actually be on your project. This guide is for CIOs, CFOs and procurement leads building a US partner shortlist for S/4HANA. Use the five questions and the cost table below before you sign anything.

I have been an ERP consultant for more than 20 years. Picking the wrong partner affects a business for years, and I have seen companies waste millions by choosing a name instead of a fit. The categorisation below is the one I would use on your selection committee, not the marketing version.

A partner mismatch has delayed more SAP projects than bad configuration. In March 2024, I watched a manufacturing client choose a Tier 1 firm for a mid-sized operation. It was overkill. The client paid twice what it should have, and the implementation took longer because the big firm's processes were too heavy for its needs.

It cuts the other way too. I have seen a government agency go with a Tier 2 firm and end up with a failed implementation, 18 lost months, litigation and embarrassment.

Tier tells you about scale, risk coverage and global capability. It does not tell you about delivery quality. The team, the fit and the governance you put around them decide that.

## How I evaluate SAP implementation partners

"We need an S/4HANA partner" is too generic to drive a decision. Sharpen it before you go to market.

You are buying four things, not a logo or a methodology deck: domain knowledge in your industry, a team that stays continuous from blueprint to hypercare, a delivery model that matches how your organisation decides, and accountability that survives staff changes on either side. Tiers package these differently, and so do firms within a tier. The most expensive mistake is buying the firm's reputation instead of the team you will work with.

### The four selection axes

**Scale of the programme.** A multi-entity, multi-country S/4HANA programme in manufacturing needs different capability from a single-entity finance and procurement rollout. Tier 1 firms bring global delivery and industry templates. Boutiques bring deep functional specialists and senior continuity.

**Industry depth.** SAP in pharmaceuticals is different from SAP in discrete manufacturing, which is different again from financial services. A partner with real industry depth has already made the design decisions your project will face. One without it learns on your budget.

**Delivery model.** Some firms lead with methodology and templates, others with experienced practitioners who adapt. Both can work. What matters is whether the model fits how your organisation makes decisions.

**The actual team.** Ask for the CVs and availability of the people who will be in your project room, and evaluate them rather than the pitch deck. My list of [essential SAP implementation team roles](/essential-sap-implementation-team-roles/) shows which CVs to ask for.

### Where the US market is different

A US shortlist is more than a global shortlist filtered for US offices. Three factors change it:

1. **Federal and defence cloud workloads run through SAP NS2.** SAP National Security Services is SAP's separate US subsidiary for national security customers. In 2025 DISA granted it a provisional authorisation to run S/4HANA Cloud Private Edition and SAP BTP at FedRAMP+ Impact Level 5. Its offerings run on [AWS GovCloud (US)](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/publicsector/run-sap-workloads-at-dod-impact-level-5-with-sap-ns2-on-aws-govcloud-us/). A partner with no NS2 experience cannot serve those workloads.
2. **Healthcare needs HIPAA-experienced configuration consultants.** Not people who have read about HIPAA: people who have designed the controls and built the audit trail for a HITRUST review. A partner that lacks it tends to find out in integration testing, when redesigning controls is slow and expensive.
3. **Defence suppliers need CMMC discipline.** The [DFARS rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/09/10/2025-17359/defense-federal-acquisition-regulation-supplement-assessing-contractor-implementation-of-cybersecurity-requirements) putting CMMC into contracts took effect on 10 November 2025. From 10 November 2026, contracts involving controlled unclassified information can require third-party Level 2 certification. A partner without the documentation habits CMMC needs will create findings during your assessment.

These factors do not change the tier you need. They change which firms within the tier can serve you.

### Five questions to ask before signing

Reference calls produce standard answers. These questions produce useful ones.

1. **Who specifically will be on my project?** Get CVs. Confirm the named people are available for your dates. Ask what happens to those commitments if the partner wins two more deals next month.
2. **How do you handle the processes specific to my industry?** A partner with depth gives specific answers. One without it says "we would assess that in blueprint".
3. **Show me a project that went wrong and how you recovered it.** Every firm has one. How they handled it tells you more than their case studies.
4. **How do you run change freeze before go-live?** Weak change control lets scope pile up late. The answer shows how they handle pressure.
5. **How will you transfer knowledge to our team?** Partners who plan for it build it into the engagement. Partners who do not create dependency.

Tier 1 partners fit large, complex programmes: multi-entity global rollouts, regulated implementations with heavy compliance, and transformations that need SAP delivery and business change at scale. The last column says where my view comes from, so you can weigh it.

### Where Tier 1 wins

Multi-region rollouts where one delivery organisation needs people in eight time zones. Regulated implementations where the audit trail across configuration, security and change control must survive external scrutiny. Transformations where SAP sits inside a broader change programme that needs board-level advice alongside delivery.

Bench depth too. If your ABAP lead resigns mid-cutover, a Tier 1 has replacements on the bench. What the replacement costs is a separate question. That one exists is not.

### Where Tier 1 fails

The most common failure: the partner you evaluated is not the team you got. The senior partner who presented the methodology rolls off after the SOW is signed. The team that arrives is the one available, not the one promised. This is the biggest source of Tier 1 disappointment I have watched play out.

Second, methodology rigidity. Pre-built templates speed delivery only when your processes match them. When they do not, the first weeks of every workstream go on untangling assumptions.

Third, commercial complexity. Tier 1 contracts are long, change requests are heavy, and the firms know the change-order game. If your team cannot govern the commercials, the budget grows quietly.

### Accenture: direct experience

Accenture brings structure. Its preconfigured industry templates cut delivery time when the client's processes fit them. One project I joined mid-stream had to untangle several standard templates before configuration could follow the real business requirements. The structure works when the fit is right, so test the template fit in the first week of blueprint, not at the end of design.

Accenture is the right call for global multi-region S/4HANA programmes with standard process scope. The risk is buying structure when you need adaptability.

### IBM: direct experience

IBM brings deep technical governance, especially in hybrid SAP environments and ECC-to-S/4HANA migrations with complex integration. The governance model is thorough. Some clients find that reassuring. Lean internal teams used to agile delivery find it heavy. The technical expertise is not in question.

If your estate includes mainframe integration, complex middleware or regulated-industry requirements, IBM is a defensible choice. Expect a heavier governance cadence than you may want once you reach steady state.

### DXC: direct experience

DXC brings technical strength in legacy integration and infrastructure. I remember one manufacturing client where DXC was already running the infrastructure when the SAP work began. Giving it the SAP programme too meant no handoff between infrastructure and SAP teams, no finger-pointing when an integration issue surfaced, and one line of accountability for the technical layer.

DXC is the right call when continuity matters more than reach. If it already runs your infrastructure or managed services, those advantages are real. If not, evaluate its SAP practice on its own merits.

### Deloitte, Capgemini, TCS, Infosys, NTT DATA and Wipro

My views on these six are more limited. I have seen Deloitte do well in healthcare and government, combining business advisory with SAP execution in a way that keeps leadership engaged. I saw Wipro run a successful SAP deployment in a US manufacturing plant: fast, practical, and comfortable with local compliance without a long set-up phase. Beyond that, the public record is limited but useful. Deloitte, TCS and Infosys have each been named a Leader in Gartner's Magic Quadrant for SAP S/4HANA Application Services: Deloitte in 2021, its seventh year running, TCS in 2021 and Infosys in 2022. [Deloitte's summary](https://www2.deloitte.com/ch/en/pages/technology/articles/magic-quadrant-for-sap-s4hana-application-services.html) describes its run of Leader placements. NTT DATA runs a dedicated SAP unit, NTT DATA Business Solutions, formerly itelligence. Capgemini's SAP base is larger in Europe than in the US, which suits European-parent companies that need continuity.

None of that tells you whether their team is right for your programme. Reference calls and CV-level checks still apply.

## Tier 2: mid-market and large business specialists

Tier 2 partners sit between global SIs and boutiques. They have real scale, established methods and specific industry practices, without Tier 1 governance overhead or commercial complexity.

| Firm | SAP focus | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| HCLTech | Cloud-first migrations, application management | Cloud S/4HANA deployments and ongoing support |
| Birlasoft | Mid-market S/4HANA, discrete manufacturing (it absorbed KPIT's SAP practice in 2019) | Manufacturers with standard scope and tight budgets |
| Tech Mahindra | Telecom, logistics, SAP BTP | Telecoms and logistics firms with BTP extension needs |
| Atos | Public sector, defence, European experience | European-parent companies needing US execution |

### Where Tier 2 fits

The choice usually comes down to industry match and delivery model. A firm with utilities or manufacturing depth brings knowledge a Tier 1 generalist would build on your programme. The commercial conversation is lighter too. Tier 2 firms negotiate. Tier 1 firms quote.

The test: give the proposed lead consultant two or three scenarios from your business that you expect to be hard, and work through them together. That session tells you more about fit than any number of reference calls.

## Tier 3: boutique and niche specialists

Tier 3 partners are smaller firms with a narrow functional or industry focus. Former SAP practitioners who built independent practices. Single-module or single-industry specialists. Regional firms with strong local relationships.

### Where Tier 3 wins

Senior consultants stay involved throughout, not just in the proposal. Decisions are faster, overhead is lower and accountability is personal. One client told me they preferred Tier 3 partners because "we get the A team every time". There is truth in it: the people named on the engagement actually turn up.

Industry depth per consultant is higher too. A boutique that has done many insurance S/4HANA programmes will usually beat a Tier 1 generalist on the next one.

### Where Tier 3 fails: three real risks

1. **Bench depth.** On one project, the ABAP lead from a small firm took leave in the middle of testing. There was no immediate backup, and we had to borrow help from another team. A fixed go-live date with a thin bench is a risk. Get the partner's backup plan for key people before you sign.
2. **Documentation and governance.** Small firms sometimes move fast by skipping documentation. I once had to rework cutover documentation that had been skipped "to save time". Good governance is not bureaucracy. It is the audit trail that protects you when decisions are contested, or when someone needs to know what was agreed eighteen months after the consultant left.
3. **Room to grow.** A boutique hired for one module will struggle to absorb scope growth. If your programme could expand, ask whether the partner can expand with it.

Tier 3 works when scope is well defined, the client team is experienced enough to supply governance, and the relationship with the named partner is established. It goes wrong when the client team is also thin and expects the partner to provide governance it cannot.

## How partner selection plays out in US programmes

### The multi-partner pattern

In large US programmes, a single partner is often the exception. A common shape is a prime SI on core S/4HANA delivery plus one or two specialists where the prime is weak: a finance-close specialist, a BTP boutique for extensions, or a separate hosting partner.

The model brings depth where the prime is shallow, an independent challenge to the prime's design and faster mobilisation on niche work. It also brings more contracts, more integration overhead and finger-pointing at workstream boundaries.

What separates a working multi-partner set-up from a mess is structure. Write the boundaries between partners down before work starts. Make one partner accountable for integration across all of them. Run one governance forum, not three. Most failures come from clients who added a second partner halfway through to fix a problem the prime created, without reshaping governance around the new arrangement.

These are the engagement sizes I see in the US market for S/4HANA programmes. Day rates are billed rates for individual senior specialists onshore. Programme-blended rates usually come out 30 to 50% lower, because most programmes mix onshore architects with offshore delivery. Treat the numbers as anchors, not quotes. |---|---|---|
| Tier 1, mid-market scope | $5M to $15M | $1,800 to $3,500 |
| Tier 1, enterprise or multi-entity | $20M to $80M+ | $1,800 to $3,500 |
| Tier 1, global multi-region | $80M to $300M+ | $1,800 to $3,500 |
| Tier 2, mid-market | $2M to $8M | $1,200 to $2,500 |
| Tier 2, enterprise | $8M to $25M | $1,200 to $2,500 |
| Tier 3, single-module boutique | $500K to $3M | $1,400 to $3,000 |
| Tier 3, multi-module boutique | $3M to $8M | $1,400 to $3,000 |

The cheapest consultant is the most expensive one. A senior advisor at the top of the range who saves you a six-month overrun costs less than a junior team at the bottom that does not. Most CFOs work this out only after the second post-mortem.

Day rate is the smallest variable. The ones that decide cost are how many people, for how long, with how much rework. A 90-day overrun on a 200-person programme dwarfs any difference in day rate across that headcount. Negotiate the rate, but do not let the rate negotiation pick your partner. My notes on [ERP contract negotiation](/erp-implementation-contract-negotiation-cost-review-cfo/) cover the commercial side.

### How RISE and GROW change the partner conversation

On RISE with SAP, SAP runs the infrastructure and technical operations. The partner's scope narrows to the application layer: configuration, migration, integration, testing and training. That removes some technical complexity, and some of the partner's control over environment availability.

Clean core changes customisation scope. Partners experienced in RISE delivery have methods for deciding which customisations become SAP standard, which become extensions on released APIs or SAP BTP, and which are retired. Partners without that experience apply the customisation-heavy habits of on-premise programmes and create technical debt in the first release.

GROW with SAP narrows the scope further. It runs on S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition, with standard processes and no core modification. Partners who have built a delivery model for it move quickly. Partners new to it treat it as a smaller S/4HANA project, which misses the point.

For 2026 programmes, ask every partner two things: how many RISE or GROW implementations have you completed, and how do you govern clean core? Vague answers mean they have not absorbed the model yet.

If I were on the buying side today, I would not start by asking which Tier 1 to put on the RFP. I would find the two or three named consultants in the US SAP market who have done the exact programme I am about to attempt, in my industry, at my scale, with my regulatory profile. Then I would call the firms they work for. Tiers are shorthand. Named practitioners are the signal.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 SAP implementation partners?</summary>

The tiers reflect scale, global delivery capability and typical engagement size, not delivery quality.

Tier 1 firms have global delivery networks, industry templates and experience across the SAP portfolio. They are chosen for large global programmes, regulated implementations and transformations that combine SAP delivery with business change.

Tier 2 firms have real scale and established methods but focus on particular industries or segments. They often suit US mid-market programmes better than Tier 1.

Tier 3 boutiques are small, with deep expertise in a module, industry or region. They offer senior access and fast decisions. Bench depth and room to grow are the risks.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you evaluate SAP implementation partner proposals?</summary>

On four things: team, fit, method and commercials.

Team: request CVs for the programme manager, solution architect and functional leads, and confirm their availability for your dates. Fit: run a working session on two or three of your hardest scenarios. Method: ask how they run change freeze and how they handle scope added informally in workshops. Commercials: what the fixed price includes, how change requests work, what credits apply for missed milestones and what knowledge transfer they commit to.

Ask for references from projects that hit serious trouble, not just the successes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the risks of choosing a Tier 3 SAP partner?</summary>

Three: bench depth (no backup when a key person is unavailable), room to grow (struggling to absorb added scope) and documentation discipline (cutover plans and decision logs cut short under time pressure).

All three are manageable if your own programme manager sets documentation standards at the start, holds the partner to them and escalates quickly when a gap in cover appears.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Should I use the same SAP partner for implementation and ongoing support?</summary>

Continuity is the advantage: the people who built the system support it, with no handover gap. Dependency is the risk: switching costs are high and your leverage at renewal is low.

A common compromise is to keep the implementation partner through hypercare, typically the first 30 to 90 days after go-live, then run a competitive selection for application management. If the same partner keeps application management, build in a re-tender trigger, typically every three years, with notice.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What questions should I ask SAP implementation partner references?</summary>

Ask about a problem and how the partner handled it. Ask whether the people named in the proposal stayed on the project; most clients wish they had asked this harder. Ask to see a key design document, because documentation quality is a proxy for governance quality. Ask whether they would hire the firm again for the next phase, and why.

Insist on a reference in your industry at similar complexity. A public sector reference tells a manufacturer very little.

</details>
