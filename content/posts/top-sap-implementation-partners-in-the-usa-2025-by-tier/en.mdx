A partner mismatch has delayed more SAP projects than bad configuration. I have seen companies select a Tier 1 firm by brand reputation. The senior partner disappeared after the kick-off dinner. They spent the next eighteen months working with a team that did not understand their industry.

I have also seen Tier 3 boutique firms with twenty consultants deliver more cleanly than firms with thousands of SAP practitioners. The delivery team knew the client, knew the industry, and were in the room from start to go-live.

Tier matters for scale, risk coverage, and global capability. It does not determine delivery quality. That is determined by the team, the fit, and the governance structure the client puts around them.

## How to frame the selection decision

Before evaluating firms, define what you are actually buying.

**Scale of the programme.** A multi-entity, multi-country S/4HANA implementation in manufacturing needs different partner capability than a single-entity Finance and Procurement rollout in the mid-market. Tier 1 firms have global delivery capacity and pre-built industry templates. Tier 3 boutiques have deep functional specialists and senior continuity. The right choice depends on which matters more for your scope.

**Industry depth.** SAP configuration in pharmaceuticals is different from SAP configuration in discrete manufacturing, which is different from SAP in financial services. Partners with genuine industry depth have done the specific design decisions your project will face. Partners without it are learning on your project.

**Delivery model.** Some firms lead with methodology and templates. Others lead with experienced practitioners who adapt to the situation. Both can work. Neither is inherently superior. What matters is whether the model fits how your organisation makes decisions.

**The actual team.** The proposal team is the sales team. The delivery team is the project team. Ask specifically for the CVs and availability of the people who will be in your project room. Evaluate them, not the pitch deck.

## Tier 1: global enterprise firms

Tier 1 partners are the right choice for large-scale, complex programmes. That includes multi-entity global rollouts and regulated industry implementations with significant compliance requirements. It also includes transformations that require both SAP delivery and broad business change capability.

| Firm | SAP specialisation | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Accenture | RISE with SAP, S/4HANA global rollouts, industry cloud | Large global transformations with standard process scope |
| Deloitte | Finance transformation, BTP, public sector, life sciences | Healthcare, government, finance-heavy programmes requiring business advisory |
| IBM | Hybrid SAP cloud, ECC to S/4HANA migration, regulated industries | Complex technical landscapes, heavily regulated environments |
| Capgemini | SAP S/4HANA Cloud, CX, utilities | European-origin firms with US operations, utilities sector |
| TCS | Global template rollouts, SAP managed services | Large global rollouts with standard templates and ongoing AMS |
| Infosys | SAP BTP, manufacturing and supply chain | Automation-driven delivery, manufacturing industry |
| NTT DATA | SAP application management, cloud hosting | AMS and managed services where integration with infrastructure matters |
| Wipro | SAP modernisation, CPG and retail | Consumer goods and retail industry with cloud migration focus |
| DXC Technology | Public sector SAP, managed services, legacy modernisation | Clients already using DXC for infrastructure or managed services |

**On Accenture:** they bring structure. Their preconfigured solutions and industry templates can reduce delivery time when the client's processes align with the template. One project I joined mid-stream had to spend three weeks untangling template assumptions before the configuration could be adapted to actual business requirements. The structure works when the fit is right. Evaluate the template fit early.

**On Deloitte:** I have seen their teams do well in healthcare and government. They combine business advisory with SAP technical delivery in a way that keeps executive sponsors engaged rather than confused. A colleague described a Deloitte SteerCo update as the first time a vendor had actually translated technical decisions into business language without being asked. That capability matters in programmes with mixed business and IT sponsorship.

**On IBM:** deep technical governance. Especially effective in hybrid SAP environments and ECC to S/4HANA migrations with complex integration landscapes. The governance model is thorough, which some clients find reassuring and others find laborious. For lean internal teams used to agile delivery approaches, the IBM model can feel like overhead. The technical expertise is not in question.

**On DXC:** technical strength, particularly in legacy system integration and infrastructure. If DXC is already managing your infrastructure, adding them to lead the SAP programme has natural advantages in continuity and accountability. One manufacturing client I worked with made this choice and the integration work was smoother because there was no handoff between infrastructure and SAP teams.

## Tier 2: mid-market and large business specialists

Tier 2 partners sit between global SIs and boutique firms. They have meaningful scale, established methodologies, and specific industry practices, without the governance overhead or commercial complexity of Tier 1 engagements.

|---|---|---|
| Birlasoft | Mid-market S/4HANA, discrete manufacturing | Manufacturing companies with standard scope, cost-conscious programmes |
| Tech Mahindra | Telecom, logistics, SAP BTP | Telecoms and logistics with BTP extension requirements |
| HCLTech | Cloud-first migrations, AMS | Cloud S/4HANA deployments, ongoing application management |
| Atos | Public sector, defence, EU-experience | Government and defence organisations requiring security governance |
| BearingPoint | Energy and utilities | Utilities companies valuing industry-specific scenario workshops |
| Syntax Systems | SAP hosting, mid-market manufacturing | Mid-sized manufacturers wanting stable hosting and steady-state management |
| Delaware | Template-based industrial rollouts | Industrial companies with standard process scope fitting SAP templates |
| Seidor | SAP Business One, S/4HANA Cloud for SMEs | Latin American and Spanish-speaking market SME deployments |

The Tier 2 choice typically comes down to industry match and delivery model. Firms like BearingPoint with utilities depth or Atos with public sector experience bring genuine domain knowledge that a generalist partner would need to develop on your programme.

The selection test: present the proposed lead consultant with two or three specific scenarios from your business that you expect to be complex. How they respond tells you more about fit than any number of reference calls.

## Tier 3: boutique and niche specialists

Tier 3 partners are typically smaller firms with narrow functional or industry focus. Former SAP practitioners who built independent practices. Specialists in a single module or industry. Regional firms with strong local relationships.

The potential advantages are real: senior consultants who are directly involved throughout (not just on the proposal), faster decisions, lower overhead, and genuine personal accountability for the outcome. One client told me they preferred Tier 3 partners because "we get the A team every time." There is truth to that. The partners named on the engagement actually show up.

The risks are also real.

**Bench depth.** On one project, the ABAP lead from a boutique firm took personal leave during system testing. There was no backup. We had to borrow resource from another engagement at short notice. A fixed go-live date with a thin bench is a risk. Understand the partner's backup plan for key consultants before signing.

**Documentation and governance.** Smaller firms sometimes move fast by skipping formal documentation. I once had to rebuild cutover documentation for a client because the Tier 3 firm had summarised it into two pages to "save time." Good governance is not bureaucracy. It is the audit trail that protects the client when decisions are contested later.

**Scope growth.** A boutique firm hired for a single module will not have the capacity to absorb scope growth cleanly. If your project could expand, consider whether the partner can grow with it.

Tier 3 works well when the scope is well-defined, the internal client team is experienced and can provide governance support, and the relationship with the named partner is established.

## How to evaluate any partner

Five questions that matter more than reference calls:

**1. Who specifically will be on my project?** Get CVs. Check that the people named in the proposal are available for your timeline. Ask what would happen to those commitments if the partner won two other engagements simultaneously.

**2. What is your approach to the processes that are specific to my industry?** A partner with genuine industry depth will have specific answers. A partner without it will default to "we would assess that in the blueprint phase."

**3. Show me a project that went wrong and how you recovered it.** Every firm has had project problems. How they handled them tells you more than their successful case studies.

**4. What is your approach to change freeze before go-live?** Firms with weak change control discipline allow scope to accumulate late. Their answer to this question reveals how they manage scope pressure.

**5. How do you handle knowledge transfer to our internal team?** Sustainable post-go-live operation requires internal capability. Partners who plan for knowledge transfer build it into the engagement structure. Partners who do not create ongoing dependency.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 SAP implementation partners?</summary>

The tier classification reflects scale, global delivery capability, and typical engagement size rather than delivery quality.

Tier 1 firms (Accenture, Deloitte, IBM, DXC, Capgemini, TCS, Infosys) have global delivery networks, pre-built industry templates, and experience across the full SAP portfolio. They are typically selected for large-scale global programmes, regulated industry implementations, or transformations that require both SAP delivery and business change capability at scale. Their overhead and commercial complexity reflect the infrastructure required to deliver at that scale.

Tier 2 firms have meaningful scale and established methodologies but operate in specific industry or geographic segments. They are often better positioned than Tier 1 for mid-market programmes where the client does not need or cannot afford global SI overhead.

Tier 3 boutiques are typically smaller, with deep expertise in a specific module, industry, or geography. They offer senior practitioner access, faster decision-making, and lower commercial complexity. The risk is bench depth and capacity to absorb scope growth.

The tier matters for selecting the right scale of partner for the programme. Within each tier, quality varies significantly by team, industry match, and delivery model fit.

<details>
<summary>How do you evaluate SAP implementation partner proposals?</summary>

Partner proposals should be evaluated on four factors: team, fit, methodology, and commercial terms.

Team: who will actually be on the project. The proposal team is not the delivery team. Request CVs for the key roles: programme manager, solution architect, functional leads for each module in scope. Confirm their availability for your specific timeline. Evaluate their industry experience, not just their SAP certifications.

Fit: does the partner's industry experience match your context? Have they delivered what you need before, in an organisation similar to yours? Present two or three specific scenarios from your business that you expect to be complex. How the proposed team responds in a working session reveals fit more reliably than references.

Methodology: how do they handle scope control, change management, and quality gates? Ask specifically about their change freeze approach. Ask how they handle scope additions that come in informally during workshops. Their answers reveal delivery discipline.

Commercial terms: what is included in the fixed price (if applicable), what is the change request mechanism, what are the penalty or credit terms for missed milestones, and what are the knowledge transfer obligations?

Reference checks are useful but curated. Ask for references from projects that encountered significant challenges, not just successful ones. Ask specifically about the scenario you are most worried about in your own programme.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the risks of choosing a Tier 3 SAP partner?</summary>

Three risks account for most Tier 3 partner problems.

Bench depth. A boutique firm with ten to twenty consultants does not have backup for key roles. If the ABAP lead, the project manager, or the FICO consultant is unavailable for any reason, there is no immediate replacement. Understand the partner's contingency plan before signing. If there is no credible answer, treat that as a red flag.

Scope growth capacity. A boutique firm hired for a specific scope will struggle to absorb significant scope additions cleanly. If your programme might expand during delivery, the partner needs to have a credible plan for additional resource. Otherwise the growth creates pressure that compromises delivery quality.

Documentation and governance. Smaller firms sometimes skip formal documentation under time pressure. A cutover plan summarised to two pages, a test script documented at summary level rather than step level, a decision log that is not maintained. These shortcuts create problems when decisions are later contested or when new team members need to understand what was agreed. Establish documentation standards at engagement start and enforce them.

These risks are manageable with the right governance structure from the client side. An experienced internal programme manager who maintains accountability on documentation and escalates quickly when bench depth becomes a risk can work effectively with a Tier 3 partner. The mismatch happens when the client's internal team is also thin and looks to the partner for governance that the partner cannot provide.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Should I use the same SAP partner for implementation and ongoing support?</summary>

Using the same partner for implementation and post-go-live support has natural advantages: continuity of system knowledge, no handover gap, and a relationship that has been tested through the most demanding phase of the programme.

The risk is dependency. A partner who knows the system deeply and who has embedded relationships with the IT team is difficult to replace later if performance declines or commercial terms need renegotiation. The switching cost is high. The leverage in the support renewal negotiation is low.

A common compromise: use the implementation partner for the hypercare period (the first 30 to 90 days post-go-live) but run a competitive selection for the longer-term application management services (AMS) contract. This gives the implementation partner the natural opportunity to compete for AMS while creating genuine market pressure on the pricing and terms.

If the implementation partner also manages AMS, build in a formal re-tendering trigger (typically every 3 years) with a 12-month notice period. This maintains commercial discipline without forcing a disruptive handover.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What questions should I ask SAP implementation partner references?</summary>

Standard reference calls produce standard answers. The questions that produce useful information:

Ask about a project problem and how they handled it. Every reference client has had something go wrong. How the partner responded, whether they escalated transparently, whether they absorbed appropriate responsibility, reveals delivery culture more clearly than success stories.

Ask about team continuity. Did the people named in the proposal stay on the project? If people rolled off, how was that managed? This is the single question that most clients wish they had asked more directly before engagement.

Ask about documentation quality. Can you pull the key design documents from the programme and show them to me? Documentation quality is a proxy for governance quality across the whole programme.

Ask about the relationship now, two years post-go-live. Would you work with this firm again for the next phase? Why or why not?

Ask for a reference that is in the same industry and at similar complexity to your own programme. A reference from a manufacturing company about a public sector implementation tells you very little.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does an SAP implementation partner do differently on RISE with SAP programmes?</summary>

RISE with SAP changes several aspects of the partner's role compared to traditional on-premise or private cloud implementations.

On RISE, the infrastructure is managed by SAP. The partner's scope is focused on the application layer: configuration, migration, integration, testing, training. Infrastructure provisioning, patching, and performance are SAP's responsibility. This removes some of the technical complexity from the partner engagement but also removes some of the partner's control over environment availability and performance.

The clean-core requirement in RISE has significant implications for customisation scope. Partners experienced in RISE delivery have developed approaches to identifying which customisations can be replaced by SAP standard functionality or BTP extensions. Partners without RISE experience may apply the same customisation-heavy approach they used in on-premise programmes, which conflicts with the clean-core requirement and creates technical debt from the first delivery.

Migration tooling is also specific to RISE. The RISE with SAP migration service provides accelerators for data migration and system conversion. Partners who have used these tools in prior programmes have a meaningful advantage in migration planning and execution.

When evaluating partners for RISE programmes, ask specifically about their RISE delivery experience: how many RISE implementations they have completed, what clean-core governance approach they use, and how they manage the scope boundary between SAP-managed infrastructure and partner-managed application configuration.

</details>
