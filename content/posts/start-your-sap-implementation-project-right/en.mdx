I spent the early part of my career believing that if the system was configured correctly, the implementation would run fine. Blueprint, build, test, go live. A logical sequence with a predictable outcome.

After 24 years implementing ERP systems in the Middle East, South-East Asia, and Europe, my view is different. The issues that derail SAP projects are almost never technical. They are governance problems. Scope problems. Change management problems. Assumptions that were never validated. Blueprint phases that moved too fast for real business alignment. Cutover plans that started three weeks before go-live.

These problems look harmless at the start. A workshop that did not get the right people in the room. A risk that was documented and never assigned. A data migration assessment based on what the business thought its data looked like rather than what it actually looked like.

Once these cracks spread, no amount of late-stage effort fixes what early visibility could have prevented.

## What an SAP implementation actually involves

An SAP implementation is not a software installation project. It is a business transformation project that includes a software component.

The scope covers: process redesign (aligning business operations to SAP's standard processes, or configuring SAP to match the business's requirements); configuration (setting up the system to support those processes); data migration (extracting, cleaning, transforming, and loading data from legacy systems); integration (connecting SAP to the other systems in the landscape); testing (unit, integration, and user acceptance); training (equipping users to operate the new system); and change management (preparing the organisation for the operational changes that come with the new system).

Each of these workstreams has its own timeline, dependencies, risks, and ownership requirements. Treating implementation as purely a technical configuration exercise and underfunding the non-technical workstreams is one of the most consistent causes of difficult go-lives.

## The SAP Activate phases

SAP Activate is SAP's delivery methodology. It structures the implementation into six phases, each with defined deliverables and phase gate criteria.

**Discover.** Business case validation, executive alignment, high-level scope definition. The decision to proceed. This is where the realistic assessment of what the programme will cost and how long it will take should happen. Optimistic assumptions made here become project delivery problems later.

**Prepare.** Project governance established. Project charter approved. Team assembled. Risk register created. Infrastructure planning initiated. The deliverables in this phase create the foundation that every subsequent phase depends on.

**Explore.** Fit-to-standard workshops: the business requirements are mapped against SAP's standard processes. Gaps are identified. Customisation decisions are made. This is the most strategically important phase. The configuration that happens in Realize is determined by the decisions made in Explore.

**Realize.** Configuration and development based on the design confirmed in Explore. Integration development. System integration testing. The build phase.

**Deploy.** User acceptance testing. Data migration execution. Training delivery. Cutover planning and rehearsal. Go-live readiness assessment.

**Run.** Go-live execution and hypercare. Post-go-live stabilisation. Transition to support operations.

The most common schedule failure: delays in Explore compress Realize, which compresses Deploy. UAT gets shortened. Data migration rehearsal gets skipped. Cutover planning is inadequate. The go-live happens anyway because the date is fixed. The first 90 days post-go-live are the consequence.

## The most common SAP project failures

### Blueprint without business alignment

The Explore phase produces a design document. The quality of that document depends on whether the business process owners who signed off on it genuinely understood and agreed to what they were signing.

I have seen projects where the fit-to-standard workshops were attended by IT and the SAP consultants but not by the business process owners who would actually use the system. The design was technically correct. The business did not recognise it.

The test is to ask a business process owner three months after sign-off: "Can you walk me through how the purchase order process will work after go-live?" If they cannot, the sign-off was not genuine.

### Weak governance

A project without clear governance drifts. Scope expands informally. Decisions get deferred. Issues accumulate without resolution.

Clear governance means: a named executive sponsor with authority over the programme; a steering committee with defined decision authority; a project manager with authority to escalate; a change control process with a named approver; and a documented escalation path from working group to steering committee.

I have seen projects where the governance structure existed on paper but was not enforced. The project manager did not have the authority to hold phase gates. Scope changes were accepted informally in workshops without going through change control. By the end of Realize, the project was carrying three months of undocumented scope additions.

### Late cutover planning

Cutover is the most operationally complex part of the programme. It involves executing a precisely sequenced set of activities while the business is waiting to operate on the new system.

A cutover plan that starts three weeks before go-live will not be adequately tested, will not have identified all the dependencies, and will not have a realistic rollback decision point.

Cutover planning should start in the Realize phase. The cutover sequence should be documented and reviewed. A dress rehearsal should be completed before the actual cutover begins. The rollback criteria (what conditions would trigger a decision to return to the legacy system) should be agreed and documented in advance.

Cutover decisions made under pressure, by people who have been awake for twenty hours, without pre-agreed criteria, are where post-go-live disasters originate.

### Integration testing skipped or minimal

Unit testing confirms that a transaction works in isolation. It does not confirm that the process works end-to-end across modules.

The integration failures that cause the most damage after go-live are almost never detectable in unit testing. They emerge when real transaction volumes flow through the full process: the goods receipt that fails because the purchase order is in the wrong status, the billing run that blocks because an account determination is missing, the production order that cannot be confirmed because a QM inspection lot is holding the goods movement.

End-to-end testing scripts should trace the full process chain from order creation to payment receipt, or from production planning to goods shipment. Running these tests requires both the modules to be configured and integrated. The tendency to delay integration testing until Realize is complete means these tests get compressed into the final weeks before UAT.

### Data migration underestimated

Data migration is consistently the highest-risk workstream in SAP implementations. It is also the one most frequently underestimated.

Source data is almost always in worse condition than the initial assessment suggests. Field mappings that look straightforward in the extract produce errors in the load. Data volumes that were estimated from system counts turn out to include inactive records that need to be excluded. Business rules for cleaning data require business decisions that take time.

A data migration that was planned for two extraction cycles typically takes four. The plan should account for this from the start, including time for business decision-making on data quality issues.

### Change management treated as optional

Change management is the workstream that prepares users and the organisation for the changes that come with the new system. It covers communication, training, process redesign, and the support structure that helps users through the transition.

When change management is cut to save budget, the first symptom is visible at go-live: users who do not know how to use the system, managers who cannot resolve issues, and workarounds that develop within the first week and become permanent. The second symptom is visible in the support ticket volume, which is higher than it should be because nobody has a clear answer to basic process questions.

The cost of adequate change management is a fraction of the cost of a difficult go-live. This is not a theoretical argument. The projects I have been on that invested properly in change management had materially better go-lives than the projects that cut it.

## Implementation approaches

| Approach | What it means | Best suited for |
|---|---|---|
| Big-bang | All modules and entities go live at the same time | Smaller organisations with standard scope; willing to accept higher go-live risk |
| Phased by module | Finance first, then supply chain, then HR | Organisations where modules have minimal cross-dependencies; allows learning between phases |
| Phased by geography | Core entity first, then regional rollouts | Global organisations with a template model; leverages learning from earlier phases |
| Brownfield (system conversion) | Existing SAP ECC converted to S/4HANA with process and data intact | Organisations with mature SAP ECC implementations and stable processes |
| Greenfield (new implementation) | New S/4HANA from scratch | Organisations with legacy non-SAP systems, or SAP ECC implementations with significant technical debt |
| Selective data transition | Hybrid: retain historical data in legacy; migrate current open items | Common for brownfield where full data migration is not required |

The approach selection should be driven by risk tolerance, business complexity, and the organisation's change management capacity. Big-bang implementations carry higher go-live risk but lower ongoing complexity. Phased implementations distribute risk but increase the period of parallel operation and require more careful integration sequencing.

## What to get right before configuration starts

Three things that, if not right at the start, will cause problems throughout:

**Project charter with signed scope.** The scope document must define what is included, what is explicitly excluded, who has decision authority, what the success criteria are, and what the change control process is. Verbal scope agreements evaporate. Written, signed scope creates accountability.

**Named business counterparts for every workstream.** Each module needs a business process owner who has the authority to approve the design and the availability to attend workshops. A business counterpart who is nominally assigned but unavailable produces a design that does not have genuine business buy-in.

**Realistic timeline validated against comparable projects.** The project plan presented by the SI will often reflect the optimal scenario. Build in contingency for the inevitable delays in Explore, the data migration extra cycles, and the integration testing rework. A plan that assumes everything goes right is not a plan. It is an optimistic narrative.

<summary>What is an SAP implementation project?</summary>

An SAP implementation project is the process of deploying SAP software to support an organisation's business operations. It covers the full lifecycle from business case and scoping through configuration, data migration, testing, training, and go-live.

The scope is broader than software installation. It includes process redesign (aligning business operations to the new system), configuration (setting up SAP to support those processes), data migration (extracting and loading data from legacy systems), integration (connecting SAP to other systems in the landscape), testing (validating that the system works as designed), training (preparing users to operate the new system), and change management (preparing the organisation for operational changes).

SAP implementations are typically delivered using the SAP Activate methodology, which structures delivery into six phases: Discover, Prepare, Explore, Realize, Deploy, and Run. Each phase has defined deliverables and phase gate criteria that must be met before the next phase begins.

The scope, complexity, and risk of an SAP implementation vary significantly with organisation size, industry, number of modules, number of entities, and the condition of existing data. A single-entity mid-market implementation in Finance and Procurement is materially different from a multi-entity global manufacturing rollout covering PP, MM, SD, FI/CO, and QM.

<details>
<summary>What are the key phases of an SAP implementation?</summary>

SAP Activate defines six phases: Discover, Prepare, Explore, Realize, Deploy, and Run.

Discover validates the business case and executive alignment. It confirms that the programme has a justified business case, defined scope, and leadership commitment before significant investment begins.

Prepare establishes project governance, assembles the team, initiates infrastructure planning, and creates the risk register. The project charter is approved in this phase.

Explore is the design phase. Fit-to-standard workshops map business requirements against SAP standard processes. Gaps are identified and the customisation and configuration decisions are made. This is the most strategically important phase. The quality of Explore decisions determines the quality of the build.

Realize is the build phase. Configuration is executed based on the Explore design. Integration is developed and tested. System integration testing validates the full process flows.

Deploy covers user acceptance testing, data migration execution, training delivery, cutover planning, and the go-live readiness assessment. The most common schedule failure is when Explore delays compress this phase.

Run is go-live execution followed by the hypercare period. The first 30 to 90 days post-go-live are where adoption is established or fails. Adequate hypercare resource is as important as adequate go-live preparation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long does an SAP implementation take?</summary>

Implementation timelines depend heavily on scope, complexity, and the organisation's internal readiness.

A focused mid-market implementation covering Finance, Procurement, and a single entity typically takes 9 to 15 months. A full enterprise implementation covering multiple modules, multiple countries, and complex manufacturing processes typically takes 18 to 36 months.

The most consistent cause of timeline overruns: delays in the Explore phase that compress the Realize and Deploy phases. Workshop scheduling slips. Business decision-making on gap resolution takes longer than planned. Data quality issues surface that require business decisions before migration mapping can proceed.

Every month of delay in Explore propagates to every subsequent phase. The project timeline should be built with explicit contingency for these delays, not as a consequence of the plan but as a structural assumption about how SAP projects run. A plan with no contingency is a plan that will slip.

The RISE with SAP model and SAP BTP-based cloud deployments can reduce some infrastructure complexity and accelerate certain deployment paths, particularly for greenfield S/4HANA implementations with standard scope. They do not eliminate the business decision-making time, the data migration complexity, or the change management requirements.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the most common reasons SAP implementations fail?</summary>

Five patterns account for most implementation failures.

Weak governance. Projects without clear decision authority drift. Scope expands informally. Issues accumulate without resolution. The steering committee does not have the composition or the mandate to make binding decisions. The project manager does not have the authority to hold phase gates.

Blueprint without real business alignment. Design sign-off from business leads who attended workshops without genuinely reviewing the design. The gap between what was signed off and what the business expected surfaces in UAT, not in Explore.

Data migration underestimated. Source data quality worse than initial assessment. Migration timeline too short for the actual volume and quality work required. Business decision-making on data quality issues not built into the plan.

Testing shortcuts. Integration and end-to-end testing compressed or skipped. Unit testing does not reveal integration failures. The failures appear post-go-live.

Inadequate change management. Users arrive at go-live without sufficient training or process understanding. Workarounds develop in the first week and become permanent. Support volume is higher and longer than it should be.

The common thread across all five: problems that were visible early and not addressed. Governance gaps visible in the Prepare phase. Business alignment gaps visible in Explore. Data quality visible in the first migration trial run. Testing gaps visible in the test coverage review. Change management gaps visible in the training resource plan. Each one fixable at the point it surfaces. Each one expensive to fix after it becomes a go-live crisis.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What should be in the SAP project charter?</summary>

The project charter is the foundational document for an SAP implementation. Its job is to make visible the things that will cause arguments later if they are left implicit.

The charter should include: project objectives tied to measurable business outcomes; scope definition specifying modules, entities, countries, and integrations in scope; explicit exclusions listing what is out of scope; decision authority structure with named individuals and their mandate; stakeholder roles and responsibilities; governance structure and escalation paths; success criteria that define what a successful implementation looks like; change control process defining how scope changes are requested, assessed, and approved; major milestones with defined completion criteria; and key assumptions and constraints.

The most important elements: scope and exclusions, success criteria, and decision authority. These are the three that most commonly become sources of dispute mid-project when they were not defined at the start.

The charter must be signed by the executive sponsor and the major business stakeholders before configuration begins. A charter that was drafted but never formally approved has limited force when contested decisions arise later.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is hypercare after SAP go-live?</summary>

Hypercare is the intensive support period that begins at go-live and continues for the first 30 to 90 days. During hypercare, the implementation team and the business work side-by-side to resolve issues, answer user questions, and stabilise operations.

The purpose of hypercare is to bridge the gap between go-live readiness (which is never perfect) and operational stability. Users who were trained but have not yet done the job in the live system will encounter issues that training did not cover. Data that looked clean in the migration validation will produce processing errors in the first month-end run. Edge cases that were not tested will appear in real transactions.

Hypercare should have a named lead, a defined escalation path for critical issues, a daily standup to track open issues and resolution progress, and a communication channel for users to report problems.

The most common hypercare failure: reducing the support resource too early because the immediate go-live appeared stable. The first full month-end close, the first payroll run, and the first procurement cycle after go-live each expose issues that the initial go-live week did not reveal. Hypercare resource should be maintained through at least one full business cycle.

Underfunding hypercare to save on implementation cost is a trade-off that consistently costs more than it saves.

</details>
