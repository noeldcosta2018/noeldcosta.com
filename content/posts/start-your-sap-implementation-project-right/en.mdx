I spent the early part of my career believing that if the system was configured correctly, the implementation would run fine. Blueprint, build, test, go live. A logical sequence with a predictable outcome.

After 24 years implementing ERP systems and 21 SAP S/4HANA programmes that I have led, rescued, or watched go sideways across the Middle East, South-East Asia, and Europe, my view is different. The issues that derail SAP projects are almost never technical. They are governance problems. Scope problems. Change management problems. Assumptions that were never validated. Blueprint phases that moved too fast for real business alignment. Cutover plans that started three weeks before go-live.

These problems look harmless at the start. A workshop that did not get the right people in the room. A risk that was documented and never assigned. A data migration assessment based on what the business thought its data looked like rather than what it actually looked like.

Once these cracks spread, no amount of late-stage effort fixes what early visibility could have prevented.

## What an SAP implementation actually involves

An SAP implementation is not a software installation project. It is a business transformation project that includes a software component.

The scope covers process redesign, configuration, data migration, integration, testing, training, and change management. Process redesign means aligning business operations to SAP's standard processes, or configuring SAP to match the business's requirements. Configuration sets up the system to support those processes. Data migration covers extracting, cleaning, transforming, and loading data from legacy systems. Integration connects SAP to the other systems in the landscape. Testing covers unit, integration, and user acceptance. Training equips users to operate the new system. Change management prepares the organisation for the operational changes that come with the new system.

Each of these workstreams has its own timeline, dependencies, risks, and ownership requirements. Treating implementation as purely a technical configuration exercise and underfunding the non-technical workstreams is one of the most consistent causes of difficult go-lives.

For 2026 programmes, add a tenth consideration: the cloud edition decision (RISE with SAP, GROW with SAP, or on-premise). This is the first scope-shaping choice and changes how each of the other workstreams runs.

## The SAP Activate phases

SAP Activate is SAP's delivery methodology. It structures the implementation into six phases, each with defined deliverables and phase gate criteria.

### Discover

Business case validation, executive alignment, high-level scope definition. The decision to proceed. This is where the realistic assessment of what the programme will cost and how long it will take should happen. Optimistic assumptions made here become project delivery problems later. The cloud edition decision should also start here.

### Prepare

Project governance established. Project charter approved. Team assembled. Risk register created. Infrastructure planning initiated. For RISE programmes, the named SAP customer success contact and escalation path are confirmed in this phase. The deliverables in this phase create the foundation that every subsequent phase depends on.

### Explore

Fit-to-standard workshops. The business requirements are mapped against SAP's standard processes. Gaps are identified. Customisation decisions are made. This is the most strategically important phase. The configuration that happens in Realize is determined by the decisions made in Explore.

For 2026 programmes on cloud editions, clean-core compliance is a mandatory consideration in this phase. Every customisation decision needs to satisfy clean-core principles: configure first, BTP extension second, in-system modification rejected. Joule for SAP Activate accelerates the fit-gap drafting work by 30 to 50% on programmes with clean source documentation.

### Realize

Configuration and development based on the design confirmed in Explore. Integration development. System integration testing. The build phase. SAP Build Code (GenAI for ABAP, released 2024) accelerates custom development by 20 to 40% on customisations that still genuinely require code.

### Deploy

User acceptance testing. Data migration execution. Training delivery. Cutover planning and rehearsal. Go-live readiness assessment.

### Run

Go-live execution and hypercare. Post-go-live stabilisation. Transition to support operations.

The most common schedule failure: delays in Explore compress Realize, which compresses Deploy. UAT gets shortened. Data migration rehearsal gets skipped. Cutover planning is inadequate. The go-live happens anyway because the date is fixed. The first 90 days post-go-live are the consequence.

## The most common SAP project failures

### Blueprint without business alignment

The Explore phase produces a design document. The quality of that document depends on whether the business process owners who signed off on it genuinely understood and agreed to what they were signing.

On a European manufacturing programme in 2024, the fit-to-standard workshops were attended by IT and the SAP consultants but not by the business process owners who would actually use the system. The design was technically correct. The business did not recognise it. UAT became a discovery exercise instead of a validation exercise.

The test is to ask a business process owner three months after sign-off: "Can you walk me through how the purchase order process will work after go-live?" If they cannot, the sign-off was not genuine.

### Weak governance

A project without clear governance drifts. Scope expands informally. Decisions get deferred. Issues accumulate without resolution.

Clear governance means a named executive sponsor with authority over the programme. It means a steering committee with defined decision authority. It means a project manager with authority to escalate. It means a change control process with a named approver. And it means a documented escalation path from working group to steering committee. For RISE programmes, it also means a clean-core review forum and an SAP customer success engagement cadence.

On a GCC retail programme in 2023, the governance structure existed on paper but was not enforced. The project manager did not have the authority to hold phase gates. Scope changes were accepted informally in workshops without going through change control. By the end of Realize, the project was carrying three months of undocumented scope additions.

### Late cutover planning

Cutover is the most operationally complex part of the programme. It involves executing a precisely sequenced set of activities while the business is waiting to operate on the new system.

A cutover plan that starts three weeks before go-live will not be adequately tested, will not have identified all the dependencies, and will not have a realistic rollback decision point.

Cutover planning should start in the Realize phase. The cutover sequence should be documented and reviewed. A dress rehearsal should be completed before the actual cutover begins. The rollback criteria (what conditions would trigger a decision to return to the legacy system) should be agreed and documented in advance.

On a European retail programme in 2024, cutover planning started six weeks before go-live, with two full dress rehearsals completed. The go-live ran cleanly. The contrast with programmes where cutover planning started three weeks out is consistent.

Cutover decisions made under pressure, by people who have been awake for twenty hours, without pre-agreed criteria, are where post-go-live disasters originate.

### Integration testing skipped or minimal

Unit testing confirms that a transaction works in isolation. It does not confirm that the process works end-to-end across modules.

The integration failures that cause the most damage after go-live are almost never detectable in unit testing. They emerge when real transaction volumes flow through the full process. The goods receipt that fails because the purchase order is in the wrong status. The billing run that blocks because an account determination is missing. The production order that cannot be confirmed because a QM inspection lot is holding the goods movement.

End-to-end testing scripts should trace the full process chain from order creation to payment receipt, or from production planning to goods shipment. Running these tests requires both the modules to be configured and integrated. The tendency to delay integration testing until Realize is complete means these tests get compressed into the final weeks before UAT.

### Data migration underestimated

Data migration is consistently the highest-risk workstream in SAP implementations. It is also the one most frequently underestimated.

Source data is almost always in worse condition than the initial assessment suggests. Field mappings that look straightforward in the extract produce errors in the load. Data volumes that were estimated from system counts turn out to include inactive records that need to be excluded. Business rules for cleaning data require business decisions that take time.

On a European pharmaceutical programme in 2023, the data migration was planned for two extraction cycles. It took five. The plan should account for this from the start, including time for business decision-making on data quality issues.

### Change management treated as optional

Change management is the workstream that prepares users and the organisation for the changes that come with the new system. It covers communication, training, process redesign, and the support structure that helps users through the transition.

When change management is cut to save budget, the first symptom is visible at go-live. Users who do not know how to use the system. Managers who cannot resolve issues. Workarounds that develop within the first week and become permanent. The second symptom is visible in the support ticket volume, which is higher than it should be because nobody has a clear answer to basic process questions.

The cost of adequate change management is a fraction of the cost of a difficult go-live. This is not a theoretical argument. The projects I have been on that invested properly in change management had materially better go-lives than the projects that cut it.

## How RISE, GROW, clean-core, and AI change the start in 2026

The fundamentals above still apply. Three considerations that need to be wired into the start of a 2026 SAP programme explicitly.

### Cloud edition decision is the first scope-shaping choice

For 2026 programmes, the cloud edition decision (RISE with SAP, GROW with SAP, or on-premise) is the first scope item, not a downstream technical choice. It determines:

- Whether SAP holds infrastructure responsibility (RISE, GROW) or the client does (on-premise)
- The customisation surface available (constrained on public edition under GROW, more flexible on private edition under RISE, fullest on on-premise)
- The clean-core enforcement model (mandatory on cloud editions, voluntary on on-premise)
- The subscription versus licence-plus-maintenance economics
- The presence of SAP customer success engagement (RISE/GROW) or absence (on-premise)

This decision should happen in Discover, not in Realize. Programmes that defer it spend the first months of Explore relitigating it.

### Clean-core compliance must be in the project charter

Clean-core compliance is mandatory across SAP Cloud editions since 2024-2025. The project charter for any RISE or GROW programme must include:

1. Clean-core review forum named and operational by end of Explore
2. Customisation approval criteria with clean-core as a gate
3. Partner BTP extension experience verified and documented
4. Clean-core compliance rate as a charter success metric

Without these, partners default to in-system modifications anyway, producing technical debt that surfaces at the first major upgrade.

### AI tooling absorbed into role definitions

The 2024-2025 AI rollout reached implementation workflows. Three capabilities should be in role definitions from kickoff:

**Joule for SAP Activate** drafts requirements from sponsor interview transcripts, fit-gap analyses from process descriptions, test scenarios from configuration scope. Functional consultants should be expected to use it during Explore and Realize.

**Microsoft Copilot in M365** drafts status reports, steering committee briefs, change communication content from prepared briefs. Project managers and change leads should be expected to use it.

**SAP Build Code** is GenAI-assisted ABAP code generation. Where customisation requires code, technical consultants should be expected to use it.

What AI does not change: the team's accountability for the decisions the tools accelerate. AI drafts faster. Humans still own what the draft says. The role definitions need to reflect this from the start, not add it as a side activity in month six.

## Implementation approaches

| Approach | What it means | Best suited for |
|---|---|---|
| Big-bang | All modules and entities go live at the same time | Smaller organisations with standard scope; willing to accept higher go-live risk; GROW with SAP defaults here |
| Phased by module | Finance first, then supply chain, then HR | Organisations where modules have minimal cross-dependencies; allows learning between phases |
| Phased by geography | Core entity first, then regional rollouts | Global organisations with a template model; leverages learning from earlier phases |
| Brownfield (system conversion) | Existing SAP ECC converted to S/4HANA with process and data intact | Organisations with mature SAP ECC implementations and stable processes |
| Greenfield (new implementation) | New S/4HANA from scratch | Organisations with legacy non-SAP systems, or SAP ECC implementations with significant technical debt |
| Selective data transition (Bluefield) | Hybrid: retain historical data in legacy; migrate current open items | Common for brownfield where full data migration is not required; mergers and carve-outs |

The approach selection should be driven by risk tolerance, business complexity, and the organisation's change management capacity. For 2026 programmes, the cloud edition decision interacts with the approach choice: GROW typically big-bang on standardised scope; RISE typically phased on enterprise scope; on-premise covers the full range.

## What to get right before configuration starts

Three things that, if not right at the start, will cause problems throughout:

**Project charter with signed scope.** The scope document must define what is included, what is explicitly excluded, who has decision authority, what the success criteria are, what the change control process is, and (for 2026 cloud programmes) what the clean-core compliance approach is. Verbal scope agreements evaporate. Written, signed scope creates accountability.

**Named business counterparts for every workstream.** Each module needs a business process owner who has the authority to approve the design and the availability to attend workshops. A business counterpart who is nominally assigned but unavailable produces a design that does not have genuine business buy-in. For RISE programmes, add a named SAP customer success contact.

**Realistic timeline validated against comparable projects.** The project plan presented by the SI will often reflect the optimal scenario. Build in contingency for the inevitable delays in Explore, the data migration extra cycles, and the integration testing rework. A plan that assumes everything goes right is not a plan. It is an optimistic narrative.

<details>
<summary>How long does an SAP implementation take?</summary>

Implementation timelines depend heavily on scope, complexity, and the organisation's internal readiness.

A focused mid-market implementation covering Finance, Procurement, and a single entity typically takes 9 to 15 months. A full enterprise implementation covering multiple modules, multiple countries, and complex manufacturing processes typically takes 18 to 36 months. GROW with SAP targets a six-month go-live for standardised mid-market scope.

The most consistent cause of timeline overruns: delays in the Explore phase that compress the Realize and Deploy phases. Workshop scheduling slips. Business decision-making on gap resolution takes longer than planned. Data quality issues surface that require business decisions before migration mapping can proceed.

Every month of delay in Explore propagates to every subsequent phase. The project timeline should be built with explicit contingency for these delays, not as a consequence of the plan but as a structural assumption about how SAP projects run. A plan with no contingency is a plan that will slip.

For 2026 programmes, Joule for SAP Activate (accelerating Explore by 30-50%) and SAP Build Code (accelerating Realize custom development by 20-40%) contribute compression where the source data is clean. They do not eliminate the business decision-making time, the data migration complexity, or the change management requirements.

<summary>What are the most common reasons SAP implementations fail?</summary>

Five patterns account for most implementation failures.

Weak governance. Projects without clear decision authority drift. Scope expands informally. Issues accumulate without resolution. The steering committee does not have the composition or the mandate to make binding decisions. The project manager does not have the authority to hold phase gates.

Blueprint without real business alignment. Design sign-off from business leads who attended workshops without genuinely reviewing the design. The gap between what was signed off and what the business expected surfaces in UAT, not in Explore.

Data migration underestimated. Source data quality worse than initial assessment. Migration timeline too short for the actual volume and quality work required. Business decision-making on data quality issues not built into the plan.

Testing shortcuts. Integration and end-to-end testing compressed or skipped. Unit testing does not reveal integration failures. The failures appear post-go-live.

Inadequate change management. Users arrive at go-live without sufficient training or process understanding. Workarounds develop in the first week and become permanent. Support volume is higher and longer than it should be.

For 2026 RISE programmes, add: partner without clean-core experience creating in-system modifications that should have been BTP extensions. The technical debt surfaces at the first major upgrade.

The common thread across all six: problems that were visible early and not addressed. Each one fixable at the point it surfaces. Each one expensive to fix after it becomes a go-live crisis.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What should be in the SAP project charter?</summary>

The project charter is the foundational document for an SAP implementation. Its job is to make visible the things that will cause arguments later if they are left implicit.

The charter should include: project objectives tied to measurable business outcomes; scope definition specifying modules, entities, countries, integrations, and cloud edition decision; explicit exclusions listing what is out of scope; decision authority structure with named individuals and their mandate; roles and responsibilities; governance structure and escalation paths; success criteria that define what a successful implementation looks like; change control process defining how scope changes are requested, assessed, and approved; major milestones with defined completion criteria; key assumptions and constraints.

For 2026 cloud programmes, add: clean-core review forum named with operational date; clean-core compliance success metric; SAP customer success contact (RISE) named with escalation path; clean-core review forum approval criteria.

The most important elements: scope and exclusions, success criteria, decision authority, and (for 2026) clean-core approach. These are the four that most commonly become sources of dispute mid-project when they were not defined at the start.

The charter must be signed by the executive sponsor and the major business stakeholders before configuration begins. A charter that was drafted but never formally approved has limited force when contested decisions arise later.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is hypercare after SAP go-live?</summary>

Hypercare is the intensive support period that begins at go-live and continues for the first 30 to 90 days. During hypercare, the implementation team and the business work side-by-side to resolve issues, answer user questions, and stabilise operations.

The purpose of hypercare is to bridge the gap between go-live readiness (which is never perfect) and operational stability. Users who were trained but have not yet done the job in the live system will encounter issues that training did not cover. Data that looked clean in the migration validation will produce processing errors in the first month-end run. Edge cases that were not tested will appear in real transactions.

Hypercare should have a named lead, a defined escalation path for critical issues, a daily standup to track open issues and resolution progress, and a communication channel for users to report problems. For RISE programmes, SAP customer success engagement should be active throughout hypercare with a defined cadence for adoption reviews.

The most common hypercare failure: reducing the support resource too early because the immediate go-live appeared stable. The first full month-end close, the first payroll run, and the first procurement cycle after go-live each expose issues that the initial go-live week did not reveal. Hypercare resource should be maintained through at least one full business cycle.

Underfunding hypercare to save on implementation cost is a trade-off that consistently costs more than it saves.

</details>

The best way to start an SAP implementation in 2026 is not to follow the most elaborate methodology or assemble the longest team. It is to make the cloud edition decision in Discover, lock the clean-core compliance approach in the project charter, name the people with real decision authority before workshops begin, build the timeline with explicit contingency for the inevitable Explore delays, and absorb the AI tooling into role definitions rather than treat it as a side activity. The cracks that show up at month nine were already visible at month one. Build for the visibility, and most of the recoverable failures never have to be recovered from.
