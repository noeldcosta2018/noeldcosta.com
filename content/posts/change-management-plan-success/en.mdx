Most resistance in ERP programmes does not come out of nowhere. It builds slowly, well before the kickoff. You hear it in sideways comments during early meetings. You notice it in key business leads going quiet. By the time formal change plans are drafted, the damage is usually already done.

Across 21 SAP programmes that I have led, rescued, or watched go sideways, the same pattern shows up: change management cannot start at training. Planning for adoption has to be tied to upstream design, governance, and resource allocation decisions. That is the connection that usually goes missing.

Where resistance typically begins:

1. Critical users excluded from early design phases
2. Department heads caught off guard by functional impacts they were not consulted on
3. Vague communication that breeds second-guessing
4. Past failed projects causing quiet distrust before the current one starts

These are not purely communication issues. They are structural. If the steering committee is passive or disconnected, expect friction. If the project charter says nothing about behaviour or adoption metrics, you have already lost a lever.

## What a change management plan actually needs to do

A change plan is not a communication calendar. It is a structured approach to helping people move from how they work today to how they will need to work in the new system. The job is to make that shift feel manageable rather than imposed.

The core components:

| Component | Purpose | Who owns it |
|---|---|---|
| Role and influence mapping | Map everyone affected; track influence, not just title | Change lead |
| Change impact assessment | Analyse how roles, processes, and tools change per group | Business process owners plus OCM |
| Communication plan | Goals, channels, audiences, timing, with feedback loops | OCM lead / comms manager |
| Training and enablement plan | Role-based curriculum, delivery format, readiness assessment | Training manager |
| Leadership engagement | Leaders aligned, equipped with talking points, visibly backing the change | Change lead / executive sponsor |
| Adoption KPIs | Behaviour metrics anchored during blueprint, tracked through go-live | Change lead |
| Resistance monitoring | Early warning indicators: missed meetings, passive silence, vague feedback | All workstream leads |

## Communication: clarity over volume

Communication gets thrown around in every change plan, but it usually means newsletters, email updates, or town halls. That is not what matters.

On a European manufacturing programme in 2024, we communicated everything on time. Status updates went out weekly. But no one could explain why the process was changing. We had volume, but no clarity. The training metrics looked fine. Adoption lagged for months.

### Tailor the message by audience

Not everyone needs the same message:

1. Senior managers want business impact first, not technical logic
2. End users need to hear from their direct manager, not a generic programme lead
3. Functional leads respond better when given ownership of part of the messaging

### Build feedback in, not just outbound

Feedback is not passive. If your plan only includes outbound communication, it is already incomplete. Build feedback loops into quality gate reviews and link user sentiment to the risk register. That connection reveals weak spots before they surface publicly.

### Get the timing right

Communicating too soon creates confusion. Too late and it feels forced. The window to earn trust is narrow.

On a GCC retail programme in 2024, end users became convinced their jobs were being replaced. Nobody on the project said that. The silence filled in the gaps. By the time leadership addressed it directly, the rumour had been running for six weeks and adoption was already at risk.

## Role and influence mapping

Reusing old lists from previous projects is one of the most common mistakes. Influence varies programme to programme. Build the map from scratch, and update it monthly.

Track three things for each person: their likely impact from the change, their current sentiment (support, neutral, resistant), and their influence over others. A resistant middle manager with high influence over their team is a higher priority than a resistant end user with low influence.

Look for informal history. Past failed projects shape behaviour in ways that do not show up in formal lessons-learned documents. Spend a few hours gathering what people remember, not just what the reports say. It reveals more about what you are dealing with.

## Training and adoption

Training is not change management. Training is one component of it.

The common failure: training happens too late, is delivered in a format that does not fit the audience, and stops before users are genuinely confident. Sending someone to a one-day course two weeks before go-live and calling it done is a setup for failure.

### What works

1. **Role-based content.** Teach people what they need for their specific job, not a generic system overview
2. **Hands-on practice.** Run exercises with actual data from the business, not demo scenarios
3. **Power users as trainers.** People learn better from colleagues they already trust than from external trainers they have never met
4. **Post-go-live support.** Readiness drops after go-live, not before. Plan for hypercare with people who can answer real questions quickly

### Adoption gates, not just functional sign-offs

Embed adoption thresholds into quality gates. A functional sign-off that says "the system works" is not the same as a readiness sign-off that says "users are prepared to use it". Most programmes miss this distinction.

## How RISE, WalkMe, and AI change the change management plan in 2026

The change management playbook written before 2024 does not survive contact with three 2026 realities.

### RISE adds an SAP customer success cadence

Under RISE with SAP, SAP itself runs a customer success engagement with the client throughout the implementation and post-go-live. This is not the same as project governance; it sits in parallel and focuses on adoption metrics, BTP usage, roadmap alignment, and feature enablement.

The change management plan needs to integrate this cadence rather than run alongside it. SAP customer success has visibility into adoption signals that the internal change team does not (Joule usage, Fiori tile launches, configuration drift). The change lead should treat the SAP customer success contact as a data source and a co-owner of the adoption narrative, not as a separate engagement to manage.

For GROW with SAP, the equivalent cadence is lighter but exists. For on-premise programmes, this dimension does not apply.

### WalkMe is now an SAP product

SAP acquired WalkMe in 2024 for around $1.5 billion. WalkMe sits alongside [SAP Enable Now](https://www.sap.com/products/technology-platform/enable-now.html) in the SAP digital adoption portfolio. The strategic direction is tighter integration with Joule, deeper coverage of SAP Fiori and S/4HANA workflows, and unified pricing under the SAP commercial umbrella.

For change management plans in 2026, the practical implications:

- Evaluate WalkMe and SAP Enable Now together as the SAP digital adoption stack, not as a vendor-versus-vendor decision
- WalkMe's in-application guidance can extend across SAP Fiori plus third-party applications, which matters when the user experience spans multiple systems
- SAP Enable Now is stronger for structured training content creation; WalkMe is stronger for live in-application coaching at the moment of use

Whatfix remains the credible non-SAP alternative for buyers who want adoption tooling that is vendor-independent of SAP.

### AI for adoption signals

The 2024-2025 AI rollout reached adoption monitoring.

**Joule sentiment signals** on SAP Sales Cloud, Service Cloud, and the broader CX portfolio surface user engagement patterns that are hard to see manually. Useful on large programmes (500+ users) where cohort analysis can identify disengaging groups. Mostly theatre below that scale because the signal is weak and the political cost of being seen to monitor sentiment is real.

**Microsoft Copilot in M365** generates first-draft change communication content from briefs. Steering committee updates, manager talking points, end-user newsletters all benefit from Copilot drafting plus human review. The quality is acceptable when the source brief is specific.

**Dashboard AI overlays** (Power BI, SAP Analytics Cloud) flag adoption KPIs that deviate from established patterns. Worth using on login rates, transaction completion rates, and workaround frequency. Not worth using on metrics with high natural variance.

What AI does not solve: the political work of acting on what the data shows. The dashboard can show falling adoption in a specific department for six weeks. If the steering committee does not act on it, the workaround becomes a habit.

## Handling resistance mid-flight

Even with good preparation, new friction will appear. Over-controlling it often backfires. The goal is to detect it early, before it festers.

Early warning signals: missed workshops, passive silence in meetings, vague feedback during testing, key users creating unofficial workarounds. Each of these is data. Build a simple table that maps signals to team zones so you can act before the issue becomes visible to the steering committee.

One approach that works: rotate change ownership by phase. One person managing change across a two-year programme usually burns out and loses perspective. As the nature of resistance evolves, so should the people responsible for addressing it.

## Embedding change into the project structure

The difference between change management that works and change management that gets added late is whether it is wired into the project structure.

Behavioural outcomes belong in the project charter, not in a separate change workstream document no one references. Adoption KPIs should be locked in before the build starts, not set after go-live when there is nothing left to anchor them to. People-related risks (team overload, cultural resistance, legacy distrust) belong in the risk register alongside technical risks.

When change management is a separate stream that reports into a generic PMO update, it becomes a tick-box. When it is embedded in governance, design workshops, and quality gate criteria, it actually influences the programme.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What are the 5 C's of change management?</summary>

The 5 C's describe the working conditions that need to be in place for change to land:

**Clarity.** People need to know what is changing and what it means for them specifically. Vague updates leave space for rumour. On a GCC retail programme in 2024, users became convinced their jobs were being replaced. Nobody on the project said that. The silence filled in the gaps.

**Consistency.** Mixed messages undermine trust fast. If one leader says a process is staying and another says it is not, people stop believing either.

**Commitment.** A sponsor who shows up early and then disappears sends a clear signal. Visible, sustained backing from leadership is what keeps teams grounded when things get difficult.

**Communication.** More is not better. Relevant, timely, and easy to absorb matters more than frequency. Short manager check-ins often do more than long email updates, and it must go both ways. If there is no feedback mechanism, it is just noise.

**Capability.** If people are not ready, they will revert. Training, support, and time to adjust are all part of this. You cannot expect real change without building the capability to sustain it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the 7 R's of change management?</summary>

The 7 R's are a framework for evaluating change requests before acting on them. In ERP projects, even a small change can cause bigger issues elsewhere.

1. **Who raised it?** Understand their role and whether the request is based on a real operational issue or just preference.
2. **What is the reason?** Is it fixing a gap, avoiding a risk, or solving something urgent? Not all reasons carry equal weight.
3. **What return is expected?** What is the gain if this goes through? If there is no clear value, reconsider.
4. **What risks are involved?** Could it disrupt something that already works? Does it affect another team or module?
5. **What resources are required?** Time, people, cost. Missed here leads to delays and overload.
6. **Who is responsible?** Without a named owner for build, test, and delivery, it falls through the cracks.
7. **What is the relationship to other changes?** One update can break another. Always check for dependencies before proceeding.

The 7 R's are not about blocking change. They help you handle it with fewer surprises.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the five steps of a change management plan?</summary>

1. **Define the change.** Be specific about what is changing, which teams are affected, and what it means for daily work. General statements do not help people prepare.
2. **Assess the impact.** Look at how deep the change goes: processes, job roles, reporting lines, data entry patterns. Some impacts are obvious; others surface only if you are watching closely.
3. **Build the strategy.** Set your communication approach, training plan, and feedback mechanisms. Do not copy-paste from another project. Build something that fits your people and culture.
4. **Execute.** Roll out in phases where possible. Use trusted team leads to carry the message. Communicate clearly, directly, and more than once. People rarely absorb everything the first time.
5. **Review and adjust.** After go-live, check what worked. Are people using the system as expected? If not, why? One plan will not cover everything. That is normal. Build in follow-up as a programme activity, not an afterthought.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Should I use WalkMe or SAP Enable Now in 2026?</summary>

Both. They are complementary, not competitive, since the 2024 SAP acquisition of WalkMe.

SAP Enable Now is stronger for structured training content creation: screen recording captures processes once and generates documentation, simulations, and test scripts from the same source. Best fit for the pre-go-live training plan.

WalkMe is stronger for live in-application guidance at the moment of use: tooltips, guided paths, and walk-throughs inside SAP Fiori. Best fit for hypercare and ongoing adoption support, especially when the user experience spans SAP plus third-party applications.

Evaluate the two together as the SAP digital adoption stack rather than as a vendor choice. Whatfix remains the credible non-SAP alternative when vendor independence from SAP matters.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between organisational change management and technical change management?</summary>

Organisational change management addresses the human side: how people are prepared, communicated with, trained, and supported through a change to how they work.

Technical change management addresses system stability: controlling what changes go into the SAP landscape, when, and through what approval process. It covers transport management, change requests, testing gates, and rollback procedures.

Both are necessary. The confusion comes when organisations have strong technical change control but no organisational change process, or the reverse. On ERP programmes, the technical side is often well-managed; the organisational side is where adoption failures originate.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you measure change management success in an ERP implementation?</summary>

Adoption KPIs need to be set before go-live, not after. Useful metrics: system login rates by user group in the first 90 days, error rates in key transactions compared to baseline, support ticket volume and category, workaround frequency (are people going around the system?), and manager-reported confidence levels from short pulse surveys.

For 2026 programmes with AI capabilities deployed (Joule on Sales Cloud, Copilot in M365), add AI feature adoption rates broken down by user role. These tell you whether the AI value claimed in the business case is actually being realised.

The goal is not to prove the change plan worked. It is to catch where adoption is lagging and address it before it becomes permanent. The window to influence behaviour is narrow. Three months after go-live, workarounds become habits.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When should change management start on an ERP project?</summary>

At mobilisation, before the first design workshop. By the time formal training is happening, change management is already mid-execution, not just beginning.

The early actions that matter most: identifying people and mapping their influence, gathering informal history about past project failures, aligning the project charter to include behavioural outcomes, and getting the executive sponsor's communication cadence established. These are the foundations. Without them, even a well-resourced change workstream is working uphill.

</details>

The best change management plan in 2026 is not the most elaborately documented one. It is the smallest set of structural commitments (behavioural outcomes in the charter, adoption gates in quality reviews, named owners on every people-risk, the right blend of SAP Enable Now plus WalkMe in the adoption stack) that the team will actually maintain through fourteen months of execution and the first six months of hypercare. Most change plans fail not because the methodology was wrong but because they were treated as a parallel workstream rather than a structural commitment. Build for the structural version. The parallel one ends up as a slide deck nobody reads.
