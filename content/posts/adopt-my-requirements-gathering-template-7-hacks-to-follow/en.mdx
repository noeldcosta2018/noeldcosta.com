On a European retail programme in 2023, I watched a six-figure project completely implode because nobody did proper requirements. The client expected one thing. The development team built something else entirely. Everyone got thrown under the bus. That is when I was called in to salvage something from the wreckage.

Roughly 40% of projects fail specifically because of poor requirements gathering. That is not wasted time. That is real money. Across 21 SAP and ERP programmes that I have led, rescued, or watched go sideways, requirements quality is the single most reliable predictor of go-live outcome.

On a GCC retail programme in 2024, I nearly stepped into the same situation. Stakeholders were all over the place. Developers were guessing. We pumped the brakes, built a decent requirements template, and delivered exactly what the business needed, on time and within budget.

## The real cost of skipping this

A peer at a different company watched their organisation spend a six-figure sum on a custom CRM that nobody uses. Sales needed one thing, marketing wanted something else, and the developers built what they thought everyone wanted.

On a US healthcare programme in 2023, the client wasted 18 months on an EMR implementation that doctors flat-out refused to use. Nobody asked them what they actually needed in their workflow. The project was scrapped and started over.

Fixing a requirement issue in production costs 100x more than catching it during planning. For enterprise projects, that is not thousands. It is hundreds of thousands.

## The five non-negotiable sections

After learning this the hard way across dozens of projects, these are the sections no requirements template should skip.

### 1. Executive summary with a signature block

Busy executives will not read a 30-page requirements document. I had a sponsor approve a project without understanding what he was signing off on, then blow a gasket when he saw the final product. One page max. Business impact, timeline, resources needed, expected ROI, and a signature block on this page, so approvers cannot claim they did not see the critical details.

### 2. Role and influence mapping

Not just a list of names. A power map. You need to know who can sink your project, who needs to be consulted, and who just needs updates. On a European retail programme in 2023, six months into one engagement, legal showed up with requirements that forced a complete redesign. Nobody thought to include them early. Map every department impacted, their representatives, and their influence level.

### 3. Business objectives (not technical requirements)

What problem are we solving? How will we measure success? On a European manufacturing programme in 2024, the client perfectly implemented inventory software that ended up slowing their warehouse operations by 20%. The template should force stakeholders to define what business success looks like, not just system features.

### 4. Functional requirements that developers can actually use

Drop the business jargon. Be specific and testable. "The system should improve customer experience" is useless. "Users must be able to process a return and issue a refund in under 3 minutes" is a requirement. If you cannot verify it was done correctly, rewrite it.

### 5. Non-functional requirements

Performance expectations, security needs, compliance requirements, scalability. This is the section almost everyone skips, then acts shocked when the system crashes under load or fails a security audit. On a European retail programme in 2024, the project collapsed on Black Friday because nobody specified performance requirements. Get specific about response times, uptime expectations, user loads, and compliance mandates.

## 7 hacks that actually work

### 1. Use the Five Whys in stakeholder interviews

Forget "what do you need?": you will get a wish list. Ask about pain points: "What makes you want to throw your computer out the window?" Use the Five Whys: ask why they need something, then why about that reason, repeat five times. You will find the real requirement, which is usually completely different from what they initially asked for.

I had a stakeholder insist they needed complex reporting capabilities. After hearing their actual use cases, they just needed three simple dashboards.

### 2. Create a requirements parking lot

About 30% of what stakeholders ask for will never actually be used. I build a "requirements parking lot" section in my template. When someone insists on something questionable, I do not argue. I document it in the parking lot and send monthly reminders asking if it should be moved to active requirements. Most stay parked forever.

### 3. Use the rule of three for stakeholders who keep changing their minds

They get to change direction twice without consequence. On the third flip-flop, they need to email their boss explaining the change and the impact. Nobody wants to send that email. The changes stop.

### 4. Use the funding allocation technique to force prioritisation

Give each stakeholder 100 virtual dollars to spend across all requirements. They cannot have everything, so they are forced to put money where it matters most. On a European financial services programme in 2024, the client had 200+ "critical" requirements. Within an hour, we identified the real top 20.

### 5. Number every requirement uniquely

Use a consistent format like REQ-FUN-023. This makes referencing specific items in discussions infinitely easier and prevents the "which requirement are we talking about?" confusion that wastes meeting time. Include the origin (who requested it and why) for when you need to cut items later.

### 6. Play back requirements in stakeholder language, not technical terms

After documenting, read requirements back to stakeholders in their own words. Create simple prototypes or wireframes for complex requirements before development starts. This surfaces misunderstandings when they are cheap to fix.

### 7. Document what was rejected, not just what is in scope

This saves you from rehashing the same arguments when someone inevitably brings up a previously rejected requirement in month five. "We discussed this in April and here is why we decided against it" is a conversation that ends quickly.

## How RISE, clean-core, and AI change requirements gathering in 2026

The seven hacks above still work. Three considerations need to be wired into requirements gathering for 2026 SAP programmes specifically.

### Cloud edition decision in the requirements baseline

For 2026 SAP programmes, the cloud edition decision (RISE with SAP, GROW with SAP, or on-premise) needs to be captured in the requirements baseline before functional requirements are gathered. The decision shapes what is even possible. GROW (S/4HANA Cloud public edition) constrains customisation most. RISE (S/4HANA Cloud private edition) allows more flexibility within clean-core principles. On-premise allows the fullest range.

Gathering functional requirements without knowing the cloud edition produces requirements that may need to be reshaped weeks later when the edition decision lands. Capture the edition first.

### Clean-core compliance as a non-functional requirement

Clean-core compliance is mandatory across SAP Cloud editions since 2024-2025. Customisations belong on SAP BTP, not in-system modifications. This is a non-functional requirement that needs to appear in section 5 of the requirements template alongside performance and security.

The specific clean-core requirements to capture: every customisation request needs a documented decision on whether to configure (no extension), build as a BTP extension (clean-core compliant), or reject. The clean-core review forum approval criteria. The partner BTP extension experience verification.

### Joule for SAP Activate accelerates fit-gap drafting

The 2024-2025 AI rollout reached requirements work. Joule for SAP Activate can draft a first-pass fit-gap from process descriptions and interview transcripts. Microsoft Copilot in M365 drafts requirements summaries, stakeholder briefs, and meeting minutes from prepared briefs.

The honest assessment after using these tools on programmes in 2024: AI accelerates the draft production by 30-50% on programmes with clean source data. It does not change the stakeholder interview itself. The Five Whys still need to be asked by a human; the draft of what was heard can be Joule-assisted.

What AI does not solve: the political work of getting stakeholders to commit to requirements. Joule cannot make a department head sign the executive summary. The human work of validation, prioritisation, and sign-off is still human.

## Adapting the template to your project type

**For software development:** Add technical constraints and integration points, user flows (not just features: the actual steps users take), and specific pass/fail acceptance criteria. We missed this on a customer portal project and spent three months in arguments about whether features were "working right".

**For process improvement:** Include current-state mapping with all the messy reality and workarounds people do not mention in meetings, impact analysis by role, and hard metrics on current performance. A manufacturing client overhauled their warehouse process without capturing baselines. Six months later, they had no way to prove the improvement or justify the spend.

**For vendor selection:** Define must-haves versus nice-to-haves explicitly, create scoring criteria with actual weights, and include support and implementation expectations in painful detail. A company I advised chose a vendor almost entirely on demo impressions. They completely ignored support requirements and ended up with a system they could not implement without massive additional consulting fees.

## Tools that help

For smaller projects or tight budgets: Trello for visualising and moving requirements through approval stages; Google Docs with comments for collaborative review; Miro for process mapping during workshops.

For serious enterprise projects: Jira with the Requirements Management add-on; Confluence (with Atlassian Intelligence for AI-assisted summarisation) for living documents that stay current; Modern Requirements if you are in a Microsoft shop. SAP Cloud ALM for SAP-native requirements management on RISE / GROW programmes.

The tool matters less than the integration. Connect your requirements tool to your project management system. Create templates that match your requirements structure. Set up automated notifications when requirements change. The "I did not know that changed" problem kills more projects than poor tooling.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What are the 5 stages of requirements gathering?</summary>

1. **Elicitation**: collecting information from stakeholders through interviews, workshops, and observation
2. **Analysis**: organising, prioritising, and resolving conflicts between requirements
3. **Documentation**: creating formal requirements specifications (BRD, FRD, or user stories depending on methodology)
4. **Validation**: confirming requirements accurately represent stakeholder needs and are testable
5. **Management**: tracking changes and maintaining requirements throughout the project lifecycle

Each stage builds on the previous. Rushing elicitation (which most teams do) creates cascading problems in every subsequent stage.

For 2026 SAP programmes, Joule for SAP Activate accelerates the documentation stage by drafting first-pass content from interview transcripts and process descriptions. The other four stages still require human work.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between a BRD and an FRD?</summary>

A Business Requirements Document (BRD) focuses on business needs and objectives: project background, business goals, stakeholder information, constraints, and high-level requirements. It answers "what does the business need?"

A Functional Requirements Document (FRD) details how the system will actually work: user stories, system behaviours, interface requirements, and acceptance criteria. It answers "what must the system do?"

I always start with the BRD to get business alignment before moving to the FRD. Teams that jump straight to FRD often build technically correct systems that solve the wrong problem.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the 3 types of requirements?</summary>

1. **Functional requirements**: what the system should do (specific features and behaviours)
2. **Non-functional requirements**: how the system should perform (performance, security, scalability, compliance). For SAP cloud editions in 2026, add clean-core compliance as a non-functional requirement
3. **Business requirements**: why the project exists (the business objectives and success criteria)

Most project failures I have seen were caused by missing non-functional requirements. The system does exactly what was asked for functionally, and falls over under real-world load or fails a compliance audit.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does the cloud edition decision change requirements gathering in 2026?</summary>

For SAP programmes in 2026, the cloud edition decision (RISE with SAP, GROW with SAP, or on-premise) needs to be captured in the requirements baseline before functional requirements are gathered.

The decision shapes what is even possible:

- GROW (S/4HANA Cloud public edition) constrains customisation most. Requirements that depend on non-standard processes may need to be rejected or reshaped to fit public edition's standardised methodology

- On-premise allows the fullest range, with clean-core recommended but not enforced

The other 2026 addition: clean-core compliance as a non-functional requirement that appears in section 5 of the template alongside performance and security.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What makes a requirement testable?</summary>

A testable requirement has a clear, measurable pass/fail condition. "The system should be fast" is not testable. "The system must return search results in under 2 seconds for 95% of queries under standard load" is testable.

The test I use: can you write a test case for this requirement right now, with clear pass/fail criteria? If not, the requirement needs rewriting. Non-testable requirements are the single most common cause of go-live disputes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you prevent requirements from constantly changing?</summary>

Three mechanisms that work:

1. **Change control process**: any requirement change after sign-off requires documenting the timeline, budget, and resource impact before anyone approves it. Make the cost of changing visible.
2. **Requirements parking lot**: new requests go into the parking lot rather than directly into scope. Review monthly. Most things that seem urgent do not survive the wait.
3. **Quality gates**: define what "requirements complete" means before moving to design. Do not start design until requirements are signed off. Scope that sneaks in during design is the most expensive kind.

For 2026 RISE programmes, the clean-core review forum adds an architectural constraint on top of the governance constraint. Every customisation request must satisfy clean-core principles before approval. This strengthens scope discipline by design rather than by willpower alone.

</details>

The best requirements gathering template in 2026 is not the longest or most elaborate one. It is the smallest set of structured sections (executive summary with signature, role mapping, business objectives, functional requirements that are testable, non-functional requirements including clean-core for cloud editions) that the team will actually maintain through the seven hacks above. The 2026 additions (cloud edition decision in the baseline, clean-core as non-functional requirement, Joule for fit-gap drafting) are useful when added to a working discipline. They are theatre when added to a template that nobody opens after sign-off. Build the discipline first.
