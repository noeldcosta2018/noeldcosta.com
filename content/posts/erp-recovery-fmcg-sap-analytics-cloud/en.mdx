This story begins with a stalled ERP analytics project in a well-known FMCG group headquartered in Singapore. The company held a 26% stake in a UK energy drinks business. Despite the minority share, management control rested in Singapore, which meant boardroom strategy in Asia was shaping daily reporting and planning in Europe.

The technology setup made things harder. Singapore ran Oracle ERP. The UK ran SAP ERP, each platform worked on its own. Together, they created a reporting problem that had already drained months of effort before I joined.

Numbers rarely matched. Reconciliations dragged on for days. Even something as basic as revenue reporting came out differently depending on which system you checked.

Within six months of the recovery engagement, what looked like a failed initiative became a working cross-border reporting model that finance and operations both trusted.

## What the setup looked like

| Challenge | Impact | Resolution with SAP Analytics Cloud |
|---|---|---|
| Dual ERPs: Oracle in Singapore, SAP in UK | Numbers didn't align; reconciliations consumed days | Live connections pulled both into one reporting model |
| Unclear reporting ownership across borders | Boardroom strategy in Asia clashed with operational data in Europe | Standardized KPIs ensured both entities reported the same metrics |
| Slow revenue reporting cycles | Basic figures differed by source system, delaying executive decisions | Unified planning and reporting cut cycle times |
| Cross-border planning misalignment | Singapore controlled strategy, UK executed with different assumptions | Shared planning models in SAC aligned both subsidiaries |

## What was going wrong

### Reporting across two ERPs

With Oracle in Singapore and SAP in the UK, reporting felt like running two separate businesses. Finance teams pulled the same metric from each system and ended up with different answers. In one monthly review, Singapore presented a revenue figure and the UK challenged it immediately with a different number. The argument ran longer than the review itself.

People fell back on Excel because it felt safer. Hours of exporting, reconciling, and building "truth", which worked short-term but created chronic delays in group reporting.

### Month-end consolidation

Month-end was the hardest. A cost sitting under "operations" in one ERP sometimes ended up under "admin" in the other. I once saw a consolidation draft where the same expense appeared twice under different headings. That killed trust in the numbers. Publishing results late became the norm, when the group finally published, revisions usually followed.

### Operational reporting

The problems extended beyond finance. Oracle tracked shipments, SAP tracked stock, and there was no single source. A warehouse manager told me he'd received three different stock reports in one week, all with different balances. He laughed when he said it, but you could see it was slowing down real decisions. Replenishment lagged. Shipping delays were harder to explain because operations didn't trust the dashboards.

### The tool selection debate

Choosing a reporting tool became a project in itself. Power BI was raised as an alternative; some managers liked its cost profile, others pushed for SAP because of the longer roadmap. I sat through a workshop where half the time was spent debating "why SAC and not Power BI" rather than discussing reporting needs. That debate lasted months and drained momentum.

### User resistance

Even after the initial dashboards went live, adoption stayed low. I remember watching someone in a finance meeting open a dashboard, glance at the numbers, close it, and go back to their Excel sheet. Nobody questioned it. People trusted what they knew, and the dashboards hadn't yet earned that trust.

## The intervention: governance, scope, and change management

### Resetting governance

The first thing I tackled was governance. Meetings felt endless. People left the room unsure who had made the call. I cut the steering group down to real decision-makers. Sessions became shorter but more decisive. Escalations reached the right people quickly. It wasn't perfect, but things started moving.

### Getting scope under control

With Oracle on one side and SAP on the other, people argued constantly about which numbers belonged in the group view. The requirement list had grown to an unmanageable size. I remember sitting in a workshop where the whiteboard was covered end to end with requests, and half of them had nothing to do with real decisions.

The framing that worked: "Reporting only needs to cover what drives decisions." Once that was agreed, delivery picked up.

### Communication that felt relevant

Earlier updates were too generic: finance, operations, and IT all received the same broad message and left with different interpretations. I shifted to tailored updates for each group. Finance got focused reporting timelines. Operations got logistics process updates. IT got their own technical roadmap. People started asking better questions because the updates finally spoke their language.

### Local champions

Training alone hadn't worked. People sat through sessions and went back to Excel the next morning. The change came when I identified local champions: colleagues who already had credibility in their teams. They explained dashboards informally, in their own words. I sat in on one of those sessions and the difference was striking. People asked questions they never would have in a formal class. Adoption improved one team at a time.

| Focus area | What I did | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Governance reset | Cut steering group to real decision-makers; shorter, sharper sessions | Decisions made in meetings; escalations moved quickly |
| Scope control | Trimmed requirement list to reporting that drove actual decisions | Less debate, clearer data models, fewer moving targets |
| Tailored communication | Finance, operations, IT each got audience-specific updates | Teams understood what mattered to them; trust returned |
| Local champions | Peer coaching in small groups instead of formal classroom training | Adoption improved team by team; Excel reliance dropped |

## Why SAP Analytics Cloud won the debate

When the tool selection finally concluded, SAC won out, partly because it could connect live to both Oracle and SAP without heavy custom work. That took some heat out of the discussion.

The live connection was the critical capability. Reports reflected changes as they happened, removing overnight refresh cycles. I remember one financial controller saying it was the first time she hadn't needed to wait for an overnight data refresh to start her day.

The first time the finance manager saw Oracle data and SAP data side by side in one dashboard, a weight lifted. That moment closed the debate.

SAC also covered more than finance. Operations wanted a view of logistics and warehousing. HR wanted workforce planning. Having planning, reporting, and visualization in one platform was the difference between a tactical fix and a longer-term foundation.

The prebuilt templates gave the team a head start and kept delivery moving, even if some felt generic. The speed of that initial delivery restored confidence after months of delays.

## Implementation highlights

The first milestone was designing the data model. Both Oracle and SAP had to feed into one structure, which was more complicated than it looked. Fields carried the same label but meant different things in each system. The design work involved weeks of mapping definitions until finance could agree on what revenue, cost, and margin actually meant.

Dashboards rolled out step by step. Finance came first. Sales and operations followed, each getting reports tailored to their actual work. Early versions felt too rigid: users said so, and they were right, but the dashboards improved and KPI alignment replaced what had been constant monthly arguments.

## Business outcomes at six months

The relaunch completed within six months. Finance noticed the change immediately. For the first time, Oracle and SAP data sat in one model inside SAC. Reconciliation arguments dropped. Executives reviewed numbers without waiting for Excel files to circulate.

Planning cycles improved. What had taken weeks now took days. Planners could model scenarios and align them with actuals. Some managers asked for more detail than the dashboards provided, but the fact that they trusted the numbers was already significant.

A warehouse manager mentioned that for the first time, the stock levels in his report matched what finance was showing. That quiet alignment across departments was the real indicator. Nobody asked to go back to the old process.

## Lessons learned

**Governance matters more than the tool.** Without clear ownership in a multi-ERP setup, reporting falls apart regardless of what analytics platform you're running. Once the governance reset was in place, things started moving again.

**Data model alignment before tool selection.** The debate about Power BI versus SAC missed the point. If your definitions don't match across systems, no tool will fix it. Once alignment happened, SAC became the natural fit.

**Change management makes or breaks adoption.** Training, local champions, and targeted communication were what made adoption stick. Without that investment, the dashboards would have stayed unused.

| Mistake | What it caused | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Unclear governance | Endless meetings, no clear decisions, delays mounted | Reset roles and decision rights early |
| Scope drift | Reporting goals kept shifting; confusion grew | Keep scope tight and business-focused |
| Ignoring users | Users lost confidence; adoption slowed | Engage stakeholders early and give them context |
| Over-customization | Wasted time rebuilding reports | Use templates and live connectors first; customize later |
| Weak change management | Training missed the mark; users stuck to old habits | Bring in local champions and peer coaching early |
