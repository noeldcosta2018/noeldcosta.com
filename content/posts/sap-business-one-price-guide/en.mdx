SAP Business One has no public price list. SAP sells it through partners, and the partner sets the price for your country. Going by 2026 partner pricing, budget about $3,200 per Professional user and $1,700 per Limited user for perpetual licences. In the cloud, expect roughly $50 to $220 per user per month. Implementation adds $25,000 to $150,000 or more. On-premise maintenance adds 18 to 20% of the licence value every year.

This guide is for owners, finance leads and IT managers at small and mid-sized companies who need a defensible budget before the first partner meeting. Use the worksheet further down to build your own number.

Most clients who reach out to me ask the same four questions. What does the licence cost? What does implementation add? What keeps costing after go-live? How do I bring the total down? In 2026 there is a fifth: when do I stay on Business One, and when do I move up to GROW with SAP? All five are answered below.

## Where Business One sits in SAP's range

[SAP Business One](https://www.sap.com/products/erp/business-one.html) is SAP's ERP for small and midsize companies. It puts finance, sales and customer management, purchasing, inventory and reporting in one system. It is not trying to be the most powerful ERP on the market. Its job is to replace the stack of spreadsheets and point tools a growing business collects.

It runs on SAP HANA or on Microsoft SQL Server. Same application on both. The HANA version is faster for analytics and heavy reporting; SQL Server is cheaper on the database side.

Business One is not SAP S/4HANA. Above it sit two other routes:

1. **GROW with SAP**, built on SAP S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition (marketed since 2025 under the SAP Cloud ERP name). Standard processes, multi-tenant cloud, upgrades every six months.
2. **RISE with SAP**, usually built on S/4HANA Cloud Private Edition. More room for custom processes, a bigger programme and a bigger bill.

Is Business One being discontinued? No. SAP's [Business One maintenance page](https://support.sap.com/en/offerings-programs/support-small-medium-enterprises/business-one/maintenance.html) lists release 10.0 in mainstream maintenance until 31 December 2028 and states that version 11 will follow. The end date applies to the release, not the product.

## The four cost components

A Business One budget has four parts: licences, deployment, implementation and the costs that keep running after go-live. Implementation is usually the largest.

### Licences

There are three main licence types. Pick by what each person actually does in the system, not by job title.

| Licence type | What it covers | One-time (on-premise) | Monthly (cloud) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Professional User | Full access: finance, sales, purchasing, inventory, admin | ~$3,200 | ~$100–$220 |
| Limited User: Financial | Accounting, banking, financial reports | ~$1,700 | ~$50–$160 |
| Limited User: CRM | Customers, sales pipeline, service | ~$1,700 | ~$50–$160 |
| Limited User: Logistics | Purchasing, inventory, warehouse | ~$1,700 | ~$50–$160 |
| Starter Package | Core functions for a maximum of 5 users | ~$1,400 | Partner bundle |

These are indicative. Partners set the actual price by country and volume. Cloud prices spread widely because some partners host on shared infrastructure and others give you a dedicated environment. Starter Package availability also varies by country, so ask your partner whether it is offered locally.

A common and expensive mistake is buying Professional licences for everyone. Most people in a small business work in one area. A Limited licence covers them at roughly half the price.

The arithmetic makes the point. A 10-person company with 3 Professional and 7 Limited users pays about $21,500 for perpetual licences. Maintenance at 18 to 20% then adds about $3,900 to $4,300 a year. Give all ten people Professional licences and the bill becomes $32,000, with maintenance of $5,800 to $6,400 a year. Same company. Same processes.

### Cloud or on-premise

This choice changes the shape of the cost more than the size of it.

**On-premise.** Perpetual licences paid up front. You provide the server and the database licence. Annual maintenance at 18 to 20% of licence value covers patches, updates and support access. Upgrades are yours to plan, test and apply. You keep full control of the environment and the data.

**Cloud.** A monthly fee per user. No server to run. Upgrades are included. Lower spend in year one, less control over upgrade timing.

Cloud nearly always looks cheaper in year one. On-premise often looks cheaper by years four and five, once the licences are paid off. Take the 10-user example above and assume a blended cloud rate of $120 per user per month: that is $14,400 a year, or $72,000 over five years. On-premise is $21,500 plus five years of maintenance, roughly $41,000 to $43,000, before you add the server, the database licence and the IT time to run them. Run both models over five years with your own partner's numbers before you decide.

### Implementation

Licences are the starting point. Implementation is usually the bigger number, and the one that moves most.

| Business size | Typical implementation range | What drives it |
|---|---|---|
| Small (5–15 users) | $25,000–$50,000 | Core modules, light data migration, standard configuration |
| Mid-sized (15–50 users) | $50,000–$100,000 | More modules, integrations, heavier data migration |
| Larger or complex | $100,000–$150,000+ | Custom workflows, legacy integration, extensive data cleanup |

Four things push the number up:

1. **Users and modules.** More of either means more configuration, testing and training.
2. **Data quality.** Clean records migrate quickly. Customer and item data scattered across spreadsheets does not.
3. **Integrations.** Almost every business connects Business One to something: a webstore, a payment gateway, a logistics provider. Each one is scope.
4. **Customisation.** Bending standard processes to match old habits adds build time and makes every future upgrade harder.

Most deployments take 90 to 150 days. Simple processes and fast decisions shorten that. Messy data and slow sign-offs stretch it.

### Ongoing costs after go-live

Go-live is not the end of the spend.

**Maintenance (on-premise).** 18 to 20% of licence value per year. On $30,000 of licences, expect $5,400 to $6,000 annually.

**Upgrades (on-premise).** Maintenance gives you the right to new releases. The work of applying them is extra: someone has to plan, test and apply each one. With heavy customisation that means consultant days. Defer upgrades too long and the jump gets harder and more expensive.

**Training.** Key-user training from a partner is billed at consultant day rates, typically $1,000 to $2,000 a day. It is not a one-off. People leave, processes change, and habits drift. An internal knowledge base built early reduces how often you pay for it again.

**Cloud subscriptions.** The fee itself, plus extra users and add-ons as the business grows.

## A budget worksheet you can take to a partner

Fill in each line before the first partner meeting. If a partner's quote leaves a line blank, ask why.

| Cost line | How to estimate it | Indicative range (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Professional licences | Count only people who work across several areas | ~$3,200 each, or ~$100–$220 per month |
| Limited licences | Everyone else, by area: Financial, CRM or Logistics | ~$1,700 each, or ~$50–$160 per month |
| Database and hosting | HANA or SQL Server; your server or the partner's cloud | Partner quote |
| Implementation | Size band from the table above | $25,000–$150,000+ |
| Integrations | List every external system that must exchange data | Priced per interface by the partner |
| Data migration and cleanup | Count customer, supplier and item records; note where they live today | Often inside implementation; confirm |
| Training | Key users at go-live, plus refreshers each year | $1,000–$2,000 per consultant day |
| Annual maintenance (on-premise) | 18–20% of total licence value | e.g. $3,900–$4,300 a year on $21,500 of licences |

## How to bring the total down

1. **Map your processes before you ask for a quote.** Write down how an order becomes an invoice and how month-end closes today. A partner can only scope what you can describe. Without it, you pay for discovery twice.
2. **Start small.** If you have five users or fewer and simple processes, ask about the Starter Package. Upgrade when the business needs it, not when the sales deck suggests it.
3. **Match licences to roles.** Professional for the few who cross areas. Limited for everyone else.
4. **Stay close to standard.** Every customisation is paid for twice: once to build, again at every upgrade.
5. **Train at go-live, not after things break.** Untrained users build workarounds, and workarounds cost more to unwind than the training would have cost.

One more test. A partner who recommends maximum scope before asking about your processes is selling services, not solving your problem. Start with what you need, prove the value, then add modules.

## When to stay on Business One and when to look at GROW with SAP

Stay on Business One when your processes are standard, you run one or a few companies, and your user count is in the tens rather than the hundreds. For that profile it is the cheaper, faster route, and it remains under active maintenance.

Look at GROW with SAP when the business is heading somewhere Business One was not built for. The signals to watch for:

1. You are adding subsidiaries in several countries and need group reporting without spreadsheets.
2. Your Business One system depends on a growing pile of partner add-ons to do core work.
3. You need functions from SAP's enterprise suite, such as deeper manufacturing or project accounting, that Business One does not cover.
4. You expect your user count to multiply within five years.

GROW is a different product, with a different implementation approach, fixed six-monthly upgrades and a higher price. If you are only at one of these signals, Business One is probably still the right call. If you are at three, start the conversation now, while you still have time to plan the move.

## Is it worth the cost?

For a business running on disconnected spreadsheets, the consolidation alone usually pays for it. Fewer inventory errors. A faster month-end close. Orders that flow to purchasing and fulfilment without being re-keyed.

For a business with simple operations that are already well run, the payback is slower, and some modules will not earn their keep.

A simple test helps. If month-end reporting is a manual bottleneck, or you lose orders and stock between systems, run the numbers with the worksheet above. If neither is true, you may not need a full ERP yet. My [Business One implementation page](/sap-implementation/business-one/) covers how I approach the scoping conversation.

<summary>What is SAP Business One and who is it designed for?</summary>

SAP Business One is SAP's ERP for small and midsize businesses. It brings finance, sales, inventory, purchasing and reporting into one system, replacing the mix of spreadsheets, separate accounting tools and disconnected databases that growing businesses collect.

It is not SAP S/4HANA, which is SAP's enterprise ERP. Business One is a separate, lighter product for companies that have outgrown basic accounting software but do not need a full enterprise system.

It is not being discontinued. SAP lists release 10.0 in mainstream maintenance until 31 December 2028, with version 11 to follow.

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between Professional and Limited licenses?</summary>

Professional licences give full access to all modules: finance, sales, purchasing, inventory and administration. They are for people who work across the whole system. At about $3,200 one-time, or roughly $100 to $220 a month in the cloud, they cost the most per user.

Limited licences restrict access to one area: Financial (accounting and banking), CRM (customers and sales) or Logistics (purchasing and inventory). They cost roughly half as much: about $1,700 one-time.

Most users only need one area. Buying Professional licences for everyone is the most common way the licence line runs over budget.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How much does SAP Business One implementation cost?</summary>

Small businesses with basic needs and clean data typically spend $25,000 to $50,000. Mid-sized companies with more users and integrations usually land between $50,000 and $100,000. Larger or more complex deployments reach $150,000 or more.

The drivers are user count, number of modules, data quality and the number of systems to integrate. Customisation adds cost now and at every upgrade.

Most implementations take 90 to 150 days. Messy data and slow internal decisions stretch the timeline.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the ongoing costs of SAP Business One after go-live?</summary>

On-premise deployments carry annual maintenance of 18 to 20% of licence value, covering updates, patches and support access. The work of applying upgrades is separate and sometimes needs a consultant.

Cloud subscriptions include upgrades in the monthly fee. The ongoing cost is the per-user subscription plus any users or add-ons added later.

Training recurs. Teams change and processes evolve, so budget for refresher training every year.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Cloud or on-premise: which is better for SAP Business One?</summary>

Cloud suits you if you want predictable monthly costs, no server to run and upgrades handled for you. The first-year spend is lower, which helps when cash is tight.

On-premise suits you if you want full control of the environment, already run servers and have the IT capability to maintain and upgrade the system.

Cloud usually looks cheaper in year one. On-premise often looks cheaper by years four and five. Model both over five years with your partner's actual quotes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between SAP Business One and SAP HANA?</summary>

SAP HANA is an in-memory database built to process large data volumes quickly. SAP Business One is the ERP application, and it can run on SAP HANA or on Microsoft SQL Server.

They are not competing products. Business One on HANA is faster, especially for analytics and reporting. Business One on SQL Server is cheaper on the database side and is adequate for smaller data volumes.

If real-time reporting or inventory visibility matters to you, the HANA version is worth the extra cost.

</details>
