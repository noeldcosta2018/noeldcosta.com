The best ERP for defence manufacturing is the one that costs, bills and reports by contract, and runs the work as projects. Shop-floor features come second.

I have implemented ERP in defence manufacturing, and the areas that mattered most were contract costing and project systems (SAP Project System, PS). Get those two right and the rest of the design has something to hang on. Get them wrong and finance spends years reconciling by hand.

For the wider vendor comparison across all manufacturing types, start with my guide to the [best ERP for manufacturing](/best-erp-for-manufacturing/). This article covers what is different when your customer is a defence ministry or a prime contractor.

## What defence manufacturing asks of an ERP

A general manufacturer sells products from stock or to order. A defence manufacturer usually delivers against a contract: a programme of work with its own budget, milestones, billing terms and reporting obligations.

That changes what the ERP has to do. Five requirements sit on top of normal production, purchasing and inventory:

1. **Contract costing.** Every cost has to land on the right contract, and the customer may audit how it got there.
2. **Project structure.** Engineering, production, testing and delivery run as one project with phases and milestones, not as unrelated orders.
3. **Serial and configuration traceability.** You need to know which components, at which revision, went into which delivered unit.
4. **Export control.** Controlled items and technical data need classification, licences and screening before anything ships.
5. **Data security.** Who can see which contract, drawing or customer record is a design question, not an afterthought.

Most ERP systems can be made to do all five. The difference between them is how much of it is standard and how much you build.

## Contract costing and project systems come first

In the defence work I have done, contract costing and project systems mattered most. Everything else in the design depended on how those two were set up.

The reason is simple. A defence contract is often priced, billed and audited on its own terms, and finance reports on it to the customer. If the ERP cannot show cost, revenue and margin by contract, the controllers rebuild it in spreadsheets every month.

### How SAP handles it

In SAP S/4HANA, a contract is usually modelled as a project in SAP Project System. A work breakdown structure (WBS) holds the budget and the cost collectors. The sales order items for the contract are assigned to WBS elements, so revenue and cost meet in the same place.

Billing follows the contract type. Fixed-price contracts usually bill by milestone.

Contracts paid on cost incurred can use resource-related billing. SAP's [Project Financials Control course](https://learning.sap.com/courses/project-financials-control-in-sap-s-4hana/performing-resource-related-billing) describes it as billing from actual costs through the Dynamic Item Processor, which can be combined with a milestone billing plan.

Revenue recognition, work in progress and margin reporting then run against the WBS. That is where most of the design effort goes, because it touches Controlling (CO), Financial Accounting (FI), Sales and Distribution (SD) and Production Planning (PP) at once.

One check before you design: some older Project System functions sit in SAP's compatibility scope in S/4HANA. SAP has moved several of them into the permanent scope in recent releases. Confirm the status of each function you plan to use against SAP Note 2269324.

### The questions to answer in design

These are the decisions that shape contract costing in any ERP, not only SAP:

1. **What is one contract in the system?** One project per contract, one per lot or one per delivery batch.
2. **Where do production costs settle?** Production orders settle to the WBS element, and the settlement rules decide what the contract report shows.
3. **How is revenue recognised?** By milestone, by cost incurred or by units delivered, and how that maps to your accounting standard.
4. **How are overheads applied?** The customer may audit the rates, so they need to be traceable in the system.
5. **What does the customer report look like?** Design the report first and work back to the cost objects.

The finance side of SAP is covered in more depth in my [SAP FICO guide](/sap-fico/). Production orders, MRP and the planning cycle that feed the project are in my [SAP PP guide](/sap-pp-production-planning/).

## Traceability, compliance and export control

The second group of requirements is about proving what you built and who you can ship it to. These are design decisions across several systems, and each one needs a single owner.

### Serial and configuration traceability

Defence customers often expect to know exactly what went into each delivered unit: part numbers, serial numbers, revisions and the changes made after delivery. In SAP, serial numbers are controlled through a serial number profile on the material master, as SAP's [serial number lesson](https://learning.sap.com/courses/configuring-organizational-and-master-data-in-sap-s-4hana-service/configuring-and-creating-serial-numbers) shows.

Serialisation alone does not give you configuration control. The engineering structure usually lives in a product lifecycle management (PLM) system.

The as-built record may be captured on the shop floor, and the ERP holds the stock and the cost. Decide which system owns the as-designed, as-built and as-maintained records before configuration starts.

Shop-floor capture is usually the job of a manufacturing execution system (MES) such as SAP Digital Manufacturing, which sits on top of the ERP. My article on [ERP and MES in manufacturing](/erp-and-mes-manufacturing/) covers where that line falls.

### Export control and trade compliance

Export control rules, such as the US International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR) and Export Administration Regulations (EAR), apply to the item, the technical data and the destination. Your ERP needs to know how each item is classified and stop a shipment that lacks a licence.

In the SAP world this sits in SAP Global Trade Services (GTS). SAP's [GTS learning content](https://learning.sap.com/courses/exploring-the-principles-of-sap-governance-risk-and-compliance/describing-international-trade-requirement-processes_c4311e5d-9612-40b2-b990-a14c9e5ce4ff) describes compliance management covering legal control, sanctioned party list screening and embargo checks, with licence monitoring for procedures that need one.

Treat export control as its own workstream with its own sign-off. Your trade compliance officer, not the ERP team, decides the classifications.

### A note on SAP's defence industry naming

SAP sells a Defense & Security solution in S/4HANA, and it is easy to assume it is for defence manufacturers. It is not. SAP's [Defense & Security course](https://learning.sap.com/courses/exploring-organizational-flexibility-in-sap-s-4hana-defense-security) covers force elements, force structures, operations and exercises, which is the world of armed forces.

A defence manufacturer normally runs standard SAP S/4HANA with Project System, plus trade compliance and, where needed, PLM and MES. Check which industry functions you are actually buying before the licence is signed.

## Why the finance design decides the outcome

The most common reason I see manufacturing ERP projects fail is that the design is not agreed in its totality, especially in finance. In defence manufacturing that risk is higher, because so much of the design is contract finance.

It happens in a predictable way. Production, supply chain and engineering agree their designs.

Finance signs off a chart of accounts and a cost centre structure. Nobody signs off the full flow from contract to WBS to production order to settlement to revenue to the customer report.

The gaps show up in testing, or worse, at the first month-end close. By then the configuration is built and every fix is a change request.

Planning matters as much as design. Across the 21 SAP programmes I have led or rescued, the biggest mistake I see is not planning properly at the start. Budgets get hit in the Explore phase, as the list of reports, interfaces, conversions, enhancements, forms and workflows (WRICEF) grows.

Two numbers to plan for. Data migration takes 30 to 40 percent of the effort in my experience, and testing about 35 percent. Give open contracts, with their costs, billing and history, a migration plan of their own.

## SAP, IFS or Infor for defence manufacturing

Three systems usually reach a defence manufacturer's shortlist. The right one depends on your size, how project-driven your work is and what your customers and partners already run.

The table compares how each vendor positions itself for the sector. It is based on the vendors' own material, so test every claim against your contracts in a scripted demo.

| System | How the vendor positions it | Where it tends to fit | What to test in the demo |
|---|---|---|---|
| SAP S/4HANA with Project System | Core ERP with project, contract billing and trade compliance (GTS) alongside | Large, multi-site manufacturers, primes and suppliers already on SAP | Contract costing from WBS to customer report; compatibility scope status of the PS functions you need |
| IFS Cloud | Positioned on [ifs.com](https://www.ifs.com/en/industries/aerospace-and-defense/manufacturers) for aerospace and defence manufacturers, with programme delivery and compliance | Project-based and asset-heavy manufacturers that also run service or maintenance | Project accounting depth, and how manufacturing and service share one record |
| Infor CloudSuite Aerospace & Defense | Described by [Infor](https://www.infor.com/resources/cloudsuite-aerospace-defense-introduction) as a multi-company ERP for the sector, covering compliance, quality and project delivery | Mid-sized suppliers that want a sector-specific cloud product | Contract cost reporting, serial traceability and how much is configured versus built |

Whichever you shortlist, give the vendor one of your real contracts. Ask them to show the contract set up, a cost posted, a milestone billed and the margin reported, in their system, before you see anything else.

## Where to start

Contract costing is where defence ERP programmes are won or lost, so start there. Before any vendor demo, write down your three most common contract types and how each one is costed, billed and reported to the customer today.

That one page becomes your demo script and the first test of every design. It also shows the gaps your finance team is filling by hand right now.
