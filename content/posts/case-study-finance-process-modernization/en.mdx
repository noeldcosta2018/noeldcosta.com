When I sit with finance leaders in family-owned conglomerates, one theme comes up repeatedly. The teams are still leaning on Excel to pull the group numbers together. It worked for years, no doubt, but once you're running businesses across the UK, Africa, and Asia, the cracks start to show. What was once manageable suddenly feels unreliable.

One CFO told me quietly that by the time his finance team finished consolidating the numbers, the board had already moved on to asking new questions. "We close in 15 days," he said, "but the world changes in five." That's not just a process problem. It's a strategic one.

## The problem: spreadsheet-driven consolidation across three continents

Each region sent its own spreadsheets. They looked different every time, and sometimes they arrived late. Pulling them together was a difficult process: people spent days chasing numbers, fixing formats, and checking balances that never matched. By the time the close was complete, the board reports were already out of date.

The numbers were questioned more often than not:

1. Reconciliation took far too long
2. Intercompany balances became arguments instead of solutions
3. Reports came late, which left leadership without confidence in what was presented

Auditors noticed the same issues. They raised concerns about running such a large consolidation process on disconnected spreadsheets. The CFO needed clear numbers for the board and instead had to explain delays and uncertainty.

| Common problem | Impact | What needed to change |
|---|---|---|
| Over-reliance on spreadsheets | Errors accumulate; reporting slows; the team spends more time reconciling than analysing | Move consolidation into SAP Group Reporting for structured data flows |
| Weak data governance | Inconsistent master data leads to mismatched results across subsidiaries | Invest in governance: chart of accounts alignment, ownership, controls, before automation |
| Mixing consolidation and planning | Teams confuse compliance reporting with forecasting | Keep S/4HANA Group Reporting for consolidation; SAP Analytics Cloud for planning |
| Cultural resistance to change | Finance stays locked in spreadsheet firefighting | Communicate the shift early; highlight quick wins; change performance metrics |
| Lack of adoption across subsidiaries | Modern tools get underused; visibility remains fragmented | Run onboarding programmes by entity and track usage to drive consistent adoption |

## The approach

After a systems evaluation, the group chose SAP S/4HANA Group Reporting to handle financial consolidation across all entities. SAP Analytics Cloud was deployed alongside it for planning and rolling forecasts.

The sequence mattered. Before any automation went live, the team spent time on data governance: aligning the chart of accounts, establishing group hierarchies, clarifying ownership of each data element. Some found this slow. In retrospect, it was what made everything else work.

The migration ran entity by entity rather than all at once: with Data Monitor checks and intercompany matching validated before each entity closed its first period in the new system.

## The outcome: 15 days to 5

The change became obvious during the first close after go-live. What had taken 15 days finished in 5.

**Faster monthly close.** Numbers now flowed into a single structure instead of being chased across disconnected spreadsheets. The pressure that used to build each month-end eased significantly.

**Finance team time returned.** For the team, this was personal. The late nights spent fixing Excel-driven errors ended. Some admitted they almost missed the deadline drama, though most welcomed the steadier pace.

**Audit-ready consolidation.** Reports now carried the structure auditors expect. Reviews became faster, and arguments about version mismatches nearly disappeared. The team could stand behind the numbers with confidence: which hadn't been the case before.

**Scenario planning with SAC.** The introduction of SAP Analytics Cloud added rolling forecasts that could test oil price volatility, foreign exchange shifts, and the implications of entering new markets. Forecasts updated whenever assumptions changed.

**CFO insights for capital allocation.** For the CFO, the win was strategic. Reliable numbers arrived faster, giving the board the visibility it needed for capital allocation decisions when opportunities still mattered, not two weeks later.

| Area | What changed | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| Financial consolidation | Integrated inside ERP; fewer reconciliations; audit-ready structure | One version of the truth for CFOs and auditors |
| Compliance reporting | Pre-built IFRS and GAAP structures; transparent audit trails | Reduced reporting risk; built confidence with regulators |
| Planning and forecasting | Scenario modelling for FX, commodity, and market changes; rolling forecasts | Shifted from static annual budgets to continuous forecasting |
| Executive decision support | Reliable KPIs with drill-down; faster access to trends | Board could act on opportunities before they closed |
| Subsidiary integration | Standardised reporting structures; unified templates | Global visibility with comparable data from all entities |

## Lessons for other executives

**Data governance before automation.** The temptation is to start with the technology, but if chart of accounts alignment, intercompany partner codes, and data ownership aren't sorted first, the results fall apart. This project got it right, and it paid off.

**Keep consolidation and planning separate.** SAP S/4HANA Group Reporting handled consolidation. SAP Analytics Cloud handled planning and rolling forecasts. Mixing the two creates confusion about accountability and ownership. Audit-ready rules stay in ERP. Scenarios and versions stay in SAC.

**Cultural change takes longer than the software rollout.** Moving people away from firefighting in spreadsheets was hard. At first, some staff preferred the old ways because they felt familiar. Over time, most admitted they had more space to do real analysis. Leaders had to guide this shift, and they had to be patient.

**Training and adoption by entity.** Every subsidiary needed clear training. When teams understood why the changes were being made, adoption was smoother. Small misunderstandings, left alone, grew into bigger issues that required rework.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What should the first 90 days focus on in a finance modernisation project?</summary>

Set scope, owners, and a repeating close calendar. Align entities, currencies, and calendars so consolidation rules have a stable home. Prioritise the record-to-report process first. Publish clear ways of working: one page is enough. Hold short weekly checkpoints to surface blockers early.

The temptation is to try to do everything at once. The 90-day goal is to have the foundation solid: governance in place, data aligned, and one clean close cycle completed in the new system.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you migrate from file-based consolidation to SAP Group Reporting without disruption?</summary>

Cut over by entity and dataset, not all at once. Run Data Monitor checks and intercompany matching before the group opens each period. Load opening and trial balances first. Turn on validations early. They will catch problems before they compound. Reconcile FX and intercompany partners every cycle.

The biggest risk is assuming data is cleaner than it is. Companies that run a data quality review before migration find it slows them down initially and saves them far more time post-go-live.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the clean split between consolidation and planning in SAP?</summary>

Consolidation, intercompany elimination, and currency translation belong inside ERP: in S/4HANA Group Reporting. Audit-ready rules, compliance structures, and the statutory close stay here.

Driver-based plans, rolling forecasts, and scenarios belong in SAP Analytics Cloud. The ownership is clearer, the tools are better suited, and the risk of contaminating compliance data with working forecasts disappears.

The rule: if it needs to be defensible to auditors, it stays in ERP. If it's a working assumption subject to change, it belongs in SAC.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you speed up the monthly close without weakening controls?</summary>

Shorten the path, but keep the gates. Identify the two or three bottlenecks that consume the most time each cycle: usually reconciliation loops and late journal approvals, and address those first.

Time-box reconciliations so teams aren't chasing perfection past the deadline. Fail fast on validations: the system should block period close until intercompany breaks are cleared, even if it feels strict. Fix one bottleneck per close cycle rather than trying to redesign everything simultaneously.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Which KPIs prove value beyond days to close?</summary>

Fewer audit notes each period is a strong signal. It means data quality is improving. Forecast accuracy compared to actuals shows whether the planning process is maturing. Decision cycle time, from a board question to a data-backed answer, captures the strategic value that faster closes enable.

The vanity metric to retire: number of reports produced. The useful metric: whether those reports changed a decision. Publish a small weekly dashboard and tie trends to actual decisions taken by leadership.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you maintain a clean core while meeting local entity requirements?</summary>

Prefer configuration and side-by-side extensions over modifying the core. Review custom items each quarter and remove code that's no longer in use. Document exceptions with an expiry date and owner: if it can't be justified at the next review, retire it.

The discipline is saying no by default to core edits. This isn't about being rigid: it's about making future upgrades tractable and keeping the compliance layer clean. Local requirements that genuinely need custom handling are the exception, not the pattern.

</details>
