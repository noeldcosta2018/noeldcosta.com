# Resource allocation planning for SAP projects

Resource allocation planning often looks fine during the early stages. Everything seems covered. Tasks line up, people are assigned, and the spreadsheet checks out, but once execution begins, gaps appear.

A key person is juggling two initiatives. Another gets pulled into audits. On one engagement, a team lost an entire week because nobody realized the security lead had back-to-back leave and training. It wasn't flagged, it wasn't tracked. A critical system access review slipped by nine days.

Most ERP project plans assume too much stability. They rely on neat estimates, full-time availability, and predictable workflows. That version of the world rarely holds true past the first month.

## The four warning signs

**Your team is constantly dealing with emergencies.** Successful SAP projects keep unplanned work under 15% of total effort. Struggling ones spend around 40% there, when your best people get pulled into every crisis, it means resource planning didn't anticipate fragility; a single person being unavailable shouldn't be able to derail a workstream.

**Business users vanish when you need them.** Design sessions, testing cycles, and UAT stall because business users are unavailable or not engaged. This is cited as a major factor in the majority of failed SAP implementations. The cause is almost always the same: the time wasn't formally committed and signed off; it was assumed.

**Technical people are spread too thin.** ABAP developers or functional consultants juggling three or more tasks at the same time deliver at around 60% of their actual capacity. Context-switching between modules wastes 20-40 minutes per switch. Quality drops by roughly 30% per added assignment. The plan looks efficient. The output doesn't.

**The critical path changes every week.** When the definition of what's critical shifts constantly, resource planning breaks down entirely. Constant reshuffling, late-starting workstreams, and weekly priority changes usually trace back to unclear scope or poorly planned sequencing.

## The most common problems

| Problem | What it looks like | How to handle it |
|---|---|---|
| Fake availability | Listed as 100% available but also running month-end close and production support | Ask specifically: how many hours per week, what else are they working on, has their line manager confirmed? |
| Shared roles with no boundaries | One person playing solution design, testing, and change management simultaneously | Split responsibilities clearly by task, not by title. Avoid the same person being critical in two areas at the same time. |
| Missing business user time | Design workshops delayed, UAT signoffs taking weeks longer than planned | Get time committed in writing, signed by their department head. Track attendance. Escalate patterns early. |
| No buffer capacity | One person's absence stalls an entire workstream | Build buffer at the task level, not just the phase level. Cross-train at least one person on each core role. |
| Plan never updated | Resource plan built at kickoff, never revised as the project evolves | Review alignment weekly during active delivery. Tie it into phase-end gates. Update when real life shifts. |

## How to build a resource allocation plan that holds

**Start with confirmed availability, not assumed.** Go to line managers before the project starts. Confirm actual hours per week. Identify what else each person is committed to. Document it, when someone's availability changes mid-project, you have a baseline to compare against and a legitimate basis to escalate.

**Phase resourcing by project phase, not flat allocation.** An ABAP developer's workload in Explore is different from their workload in Realize. Business users are needed heavily in Explore for process design and in Realize for UAT. Flattening allocation across the whole timeline produces plans that look balanced but don't match actual demand.

**Map cross-stream dependencies explicitly.** Data migration completion feeds integration testing. Integration testing feeds UAT. UAT drives the cutover schedule. Every dependency should have a named owner, a due date, and a flag if it slips, when a dependency delay is identified, the downstream impact needs to be visible immediately, not discovered three weeks later.

**Protect business user time at steering level.** This is the most consistently underestimated challenge on SAP programmes. Business users are not dedicated to the project. Their day jobs continue. Without explicit sign-off from their management and a clear expectation of how many hours per week are needed, they will deprioritize the project when operational pressure arrives. Escalate early if attendance patterns suggest the commitment isn't holding.

**Update the plan weekly during active delivery phases.** A resource plan that hasn't been touched in two weeks is probably out of date. Track actual utilization against planned utilization. If someone is running at 120% for two weeks straight, that's a signal: either they're overloaded or the plan is wrong. Both need to be addressed.

## What the frameworks actually mean in practice

Most resource allocation frameworks (RACI, Resource Breakdown Structure, workload histograms) are useful as thinking tools. The value isn't in producing the document. It's in the conversations the document forces.

A RACI matrix is only valuable if it's used to resolve conflicts when two people think they own the same decision. A workload histogram is only valuable if the team actually reviews it in weekly standups and acts on imbalances. A resource plan template is only valuable if it gets updated when reality changes.

The framing I find most useful: treat resource allocation as a live activity, not a planning artifact. The plan is version one. Every week of execution is a revision.

<details>
<summary>What is resource allocation planning in SAP projects, and why does it matter?</summary>

Resource allocation planning is the process of identifying which people are needed, when they're needed, and how much of their time the project requires, and then actively tracking whether that plan matches reality.

It matters because SAP projects depend heavily on specific individuals: the FI/CO lead who understands your chart of accounts, the data migration specialist who knows your legacy system, the change management person who has relationships with the business units, when any of these people aren't available at the right time, work stops or gets done wrong.

Most project delays that look like technical problems are actually resource problems; the right person wasn't available when the work required them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does poor resource allocation cause SAP project delays?</summary>

Usually through dependencies. A configuration lead gets pulled to another project during the Realize phase. Their work stalls. That delays integration testing. Integration testing delays UAT. UAT delays cutover readiness. A two-week absence in week eight becomes a six-week schedule slip by go-live.

The mechanism is compounding. Small gaps early become large delays late. By the time the impact is visible, the recovery cost is several times what it would have been if the gap had been identified and addressed in week two.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I get business users committed to an SAP project when they have day jobs?</summary>

Get written commitment from their line manager before the project starts. Specify the number of hours per week required, which phases require the heaviest involvement, and what approval is needed if their availability changes.

Treat business user time the same way you treat any other project resource. Track attendance at design sessions and testing cycles, when attendance drops below expectations, escalate at steering level, not just project level. The people who can actually enforce business user commitment are their department heads, not the project team.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How should I handle a key person leaving mid-project?</summary>

First: never have a single point of failure on a critical role. Cross-training is not optional on an 18-month programme. At least one person should understand enough about each critical workstream to keep it moving if the primary lead is unavailable.

When someone leaves: assess the knowledge they hold immediately. What decisions did they make that aren't documented? What configuration rationale is only in their head? The knowledge recovery exercise is often harder than finding a replacement.

For backfill: be specific about what the incoming person needs to know. "Take over the MM workstream" is not a useful handoff. A proper handover document, recorded sessions, and a one-week overlap period are the minimum.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When should I escalate a resource problem?</summary>

Earlier than feels comfortable. Most project managers wait too long to escalate resource issues because they're hoping the situation resolves itself. It usually doesn't.

Escalate when: a named dependency owner has been unavailable for more than a week, when a business user is consistently missing sessions, when a technical resource is tracking at 120%+ utilization for two consecutive weeks, or when a workstream start is blocked by a resourcing decision that's been deferred.

The cost of escalating unnecessarily is a brief uncomfortable conversation. The cost of not escalating when needed is weeks of schedule slip.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What's the right way to handle shared resources across multiple projects?</summary>

Assume shared resources will always prioritize something else when pressure hits. That's not cynicism; it's the reality of how people operate when they're accountable to multiple sponsors.

For shared technical resources: agree on a specific number of hours per week and get that commitment confirmed by their primary manager. Build buffer into any work that depends on them. Don't schedule shared resources on your critical path unless you have a fallback.

For shared business users (the most common pattern): the same applies, but the conversation needs to happen at a senior level. A project manager asking a department head's team for time will lose to operational priorities every time. The project sponsor making that request carries more weight.

</details>
