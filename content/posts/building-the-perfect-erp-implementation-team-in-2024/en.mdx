On a European manufacturing programme in 2023, the client had two ERP implementations running at the same time: one on SAP, one on Oracle. The Oracle project had 4,500 people working on it. The SAP project had 38. One system went live smoothly. The other was a never-ending disaster. The difference was the team.

Across 21 ERP programmes that I have led, rescued, or watched go sideways over 20+ years, the pattern is consistent. ERP implementation is not about software. It is about getting the right people in place. If you hire the wrong team, or structure the wrong team, your project will drag on and costs will spiral. By the time the system arrives, employees will have already decided they hate it.

## Core roles every team needs

| Role | What they actually do | When they are involved |
|---|---|---|
| **Project sponsor** | Clears roadblocks, secures budget, keeps leadership engaged | All phases |
| **Project manager** | Manages timeline, scope, risk, and vendor coordination | All phases |
| **Business process owners** | Validate design, test scenarios, represent real-world workflows | Explore → Deploy |
| **Functional consultants** | Gather requirements, configure modules, support testing | Explore → Deploy |
| **Technical consultants** | Custom development, interfaces, system setup | Realize → Deploy |
| **Integration lead** | Middleware design, data flows across systems | Explore → Test |
| **Data migration lead** | Data strategy, cleansing, migration loads, cutover data | Prepare → Deploy |
| **Change and training lead** | Training content, comms plan, adoption metrics | Explore → Run |
| **Testing lead** | Test scripts, SIT, UAT, defect tracking | Realize → Deploy |
| **Cutover manager** | Production switchover, downtime management, rollback plans | Deploy |
| **Clean-core architect (RISE / cloud editions)** | BTP extension governance, clean-core compliance | Explore → Run |
| **SAP customer success contact (RISE)** | Platform escalations, adoption metrics, SAP cadence | Prepare → Run |

## What makes each role succeed or fail

### Project sponsor

The sponsor's job is not to sign off on the project charter and disappear. I have seen projects stall for months because nobody above the project manager had authority to make decisions when conflicts arose between departments. The sponsor needs to be accessible, willing to make hard calls, and present through stabilisation, not just the kickoff.

What goes wrong: sponsors who hand everything off to IT. ERP is a business transformation, not an IT upgrade. If leadership is not driving it, it fails.

### Project manager

An ERP project manager needs to understand how SAP or Oracle projects actually run, not just general IT project management. The risks are different, the dependencies are different, and the pressure during cutover is unlike most IT projects.

What goes wrong: a project manager who defers to consultants on scope decisions, or who cannot hold the business accountable to testing deadlines.

### Business process owners

IT does not run your business. Operations, finance, procurement, and HR do. Business process owners are the people who make sure the system works for real-world processes, not just on paper. Ignore them, and you will end up with a configuration that made sense in a workshop but fails in week one of go-live.

They need to be involved from the beginning, not brought in at UAT to validate decisions they were not part of making.

### ERP consultants

Good consultants push back. If yours agree with everything and never challenge a requirement, they are not experts. They are billing hours. The best ones help you avoid mistakes before they cost you months and budget.

One sign of a weak consultant: they over-customise because it is easier than explaining why the business needs to change its process. Custom code accumulates. Every Z program is something to maintain, test at upgrade, and explain to the next team. In the clean-core era for SAP cloud editions, the customisation discipline is enforced architecturally; weak consultants who default to in-system modifications produce technical debt that surfaces at the first major upgrade.

### Data migration lead

ERP is only as good as the data inside it. Bad data in the old system becomes bad data in the new one. I have seen implementations where financial reports did not match reality on day one because nobody owned the data quality conversation before migration.

Data migration is a business process that requires business ownership. IT can move the data. Business teams have to confirm it is right.

### Change management and training lead

Change management is not training. It is communication, early involvement, and finding champions inside the business who support the change before go-live. On a GCC manufacturing programme in 2024, the factory thought training was enough. Workers did not trust the new system, went back to their spreadsheets, and work slowed down. Fixing this after go-live was expensive.

The training lead should be building content, running pilots, and measuring readiness, not handing users a PDF two weeks before go-live. For 2026, the SAP digital adoption stack (SAP Enable Now for pre-go-live content, WalkMe for in-application guidance post-2024 SAP acquisition) belongs to this lead.

### Clean-core architect (for RISE / cloud editions)

Clean-core compliance is mandatory across SAP Cloud editions since 2024-2025. Customisations belong on SAP BTP, not in-system modifications. On larger programmes, this requires a dedicated clean-core architect or BTP extension lead. On mid-market programmes, the solution architect typically absorbs the responsibility.

Partners without BTP extension experience cannot fill this role. The evaluation question: how many BTP extensions have they delivered under clean-core principles, with examples.

### SAP customer success contact (for RISE programmes)

Under RISE with SAP, SAP itself runs a customer success engagement throughout implementation and post-go-live. The CIO needs a named SAP contact for platform escalations, adoption metric reviews, and roadmap alignment. This is not optional and not just a steering committee invite. Name the contact in the team roster from the Prepare phase.

## How many people do you need?

Team size should match complexity, not headcount.

| Company type | Typical team size | What drives complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Small (single entity, under 500 employees) | 10-25 | Mostly standard functionality, few integrations. GROW with SAP defaults |
| Mid-market (multi-site, 500-5,000 employees) | 30-75 | More integrations, regional process variants, change management at scale |
| Enterprise (global, 5,000+ employees) | 100-500+ | Custom configuration, multiple integrations, compliance across jurisdictions. RISE typical |

A 50-person plant running complex make-to-order manufacturing may need a larger and more specialised team than a 500-person company running standard retail processes. Do not size by headcount. Size by what needs to get done.

## How RISE, clean-core, and AI change ERP team design in 2026

The core team model still works. Three considerations need to be wired into team design from kickoff.

### RISE adds two named roles to the team roster

Under RISE with SAP, the project team roster grows by two roles that did not exist on pre-2024 implementations:

1. **Named SAP customer success contact** for platform escalations and SAP cadence. Confirmed in Prepare, active through Run.
2. **Clean-core architect or BTP extension lead** for customisation governance. Dedicated role on larger programmes, absorbed by solution architect on mid-market.

For GROW with SAP at smaller scale, the same two roles apply at lighter weight. For on-premise programmes, the SAP customer success role does not apply; the clean-core role applies as a voluntary discipline.

### Clean-core changes the consultant evaluation criteria

Beyond the standard "do they push back on requirements?" question, 2026 partner evaluation needs to include: how many BTP extensions have they delivered under clean-core principles, with examples. Partners without BTP extension experience create in-system modifications anyway, producing technical debt that surfaces at the first major upgrade.

This evaluation question filters partner shortlists materially in 2026. Vague answers are a red flag.

### AI tooling shifts productivity, not headcount logic

Joule for SAP Activate, Microsoft Copilot in M365, and SAP Build Code accelerate parts of the implementation work. Joule drafts requirements and fit-gap from interview transcripts. Copilot drafts status reports, steering committee briefs, and change communication. SAP Build Code accelerates custom ABAP development.

The honest assessment after using these tools on programmes in 2024: productivity gains of 20 to 40% on workflow-heavy roles when used consistently. The team size for a 2026 programme is somewhat smaller than the equivalent 2022 programme at the same scope, but not dramatically.

The role definitions need to absorb the AI tooling explicitly. Functional consultants should be expected to use Joule for fit-gap drafting. Project managers should be expected to use Copilot for status reports. ABAP developers should be expected to use SAP Build Code where appropriate.

What AI does not change: the team's accountability for the decisions the tools accelerate. AI drafts faster. Humans still own what the draft says.

## Internal team versus implementation partner

Most businesses use a combination: an internal core team who understand the business and an implementation partner who brings the technical and methodological depth.

The internal team must be genuinely involved, not just observers. If your internal team is just attending status meetings, the project will deliver a system the partner understood but nobody inside the company knows how to use.

What to expect from a partner: they bring structure, accelerate decisions, and have seen your kind of mistakes before. What not to delegate: scope decisions, process design sign-off, and user readiness. Those need internal ownership.

The model that works consistently: pair employees with consultants in shadow roles. Each consultant has an internal counterpart who will own that area post-go-live. The consultant delivers. The internal person learns. Knowledge stays when the consultants leave.

**Vetting a partner in 2026:** ask for references from companies of similar size and industry, not the vendor's showcase clients. Ask specifically about clean-core experience and BTP extension examples. Ask how they integrate with SAP customer success on RISE programmes. The vague answers tell you who is genuinely current and who is selling the version of SAP they understood three years ago.

<details>
<summary>What is the role of the project sponsor in an ERP implementation?</summary>

The project sponsor provides executive-level support: securing budget, clearing roadblocks, and making decisions when cross-department conflicts arise. They are not there to manage the project; they are there to ensure it can be managed.

The most important thing a sponsor does is stay engaged through stabilisation, not just up to go-live. Projects that lose executive attention after the cutover weekend tend to develop workarounds that persist for years.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why do ERP implementations need business process owners?</summary>

Because IT does not run your business. Business process owners (people from finance, operations, HR, and procurement) know how work actually gets done. Without them, the implementation team designs a system that makes sense on paper but fails in practice.

They need to be involved from the start, in workshops and design decisions, not brought in at UAT to validate choices they were not part of making. By that point, it is too expensive to change what is wrong.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What should I look for in an ERP consultant in 2026?</summary>

The most important quality remains willingness to push back. A consultant who agrees with everything is not helping you avoid mistakes. They are making the project easier for themselves. Good consultants challenge bad requirements, flag scope creep, and explain why the standard solution usually serves you better than the custom one.

For 2026, add a clean-core evaluation: how many BTP extensions has the consultant or their firm delivered under clean-core principles? Can they show examples? Vague answers are a red flag, because partners without this experience default to in-system modifications that produce technical debt at the first major upgrade.

Also ask: have they worked on projects of similar size and industry? Do they have references you can actually call? And are they proposing to solve your problem or to sell you the engagement they already know how to deliver?

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you handle data migration in an ERP implementation?</summary>

Data migration needs a dedicated lead, not a shared role, not something tacked onto the functional consultant's responsibilities. The data migration lead owns the strategy, cleansing rules, migration loads, and go-live data validation.

The business is responsible for data quality. IT can move the data from one system to another. The business has to confirm that what arrives in the new system is correct. This distinction matters because IT teams often do not know whether a vendor record, a product code, or a chart of accounts entry is actually right. The business does.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is change management in an ERP project and why does it matter?</summary>

Change management is not training. It is the process of preparing people for a significant change to how they work, before, during, and after go-live.

It includes communication (telling people what is changing and why, early), involvement (getting key users into design and testing so they have ownership), and support (identifying champions inside the business who help their colleagues adapt).

Projects that skip change management or treat it as a training calendar routinely face the same outcome: users find workarounds, revert to spreadsheets, and flood IT with complaints. The system works, but nobody trusts it.

For 2026, the SAP digital adoption stack (SAP Enable Now for pre-go-live content, WalkMe for in-application guidance post-2024 SAP acquisition) is the standard tooling for the change and training lead. Whatfix remains the credible non-SAP alternative.

</details>

The best ERP implementation team in 2026 is not the largest one or the most certified one. It is the team with named, dedicated owners for every critical role, with the right hybrid of employees and consultants in shadow pairs, with the clean-core architect and the SAP customer success contact explicitly added for RISE programmes, and with the AI tooling absorbed into the role definitions rather than treated as a side activity. Most team failures happen because part-time commitments were accepted, accountability was diffused, and the new 2026 roles were added as afterthoughts rather than core team members. Build for the structural version. The part-time version becomes a delivery story you spend years explaining.
