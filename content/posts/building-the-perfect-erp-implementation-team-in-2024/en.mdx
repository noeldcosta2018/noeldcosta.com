A company I worked with had two ERP implementations running at the same time: one on SAP, one on Oracle. The Oracle project had 4,500 people working on it. The SAP project had 38. One system went live smoothly. The other was a never-ending disaster. The difference was the team.

ERP implementation isn't about software. It's about getting the right people in place. If you hire the wrong team, or structure the wrong team, your project will drag on, costs will spiral, and employees will have already decided they hate the system before they've touched it.

## Core roles every team needs

| Role | What they actually do | When they're involved |
|---|---|---|
| **Project sponsor** | Clears roadblocks, secures budget, keeps leadership engaged | All phases |
| **Project manager** | Manages timeline, scope, risk, and vendor coordination | All phases |
| **Business process owners** | Validate design, test scenarios, represent real-world workflows | Explore → Deploy |
| **Functional consultants** | Gather requirements, configure modules, support testing | Explore → Deploy |
| **Technical consultants** | Custom development, interfaces, system setup | Realize → Deploy |
| **Integration lead** | Middleware design, data flows across systems | Explore → Test |
| **Data migration lead** | Data strategy, cleansing, migration loads, cutover data | Prepare → Deploy |
| **Change and training lead** | Training content, comms plan, adoption metrics | Explore → Run |
| **Testing lead** | Test scripts, SIT, UAT, defect tracking | Realize → Deploy |
| **Cutover manager** | Production switchover, downtime management, rollback plans | Deploy |

## What makes each role succeed or fail

### Project sponsor

The sponsor's job is not to sign off on the project charter and disappear. I've seen projects stall for months because nobody above the PM had authority to make decisions when conflicts arose between departments. The sponsor needs to be accessible, willing to make hard calls, and present through stabilisation, not just the kickoff.

What goes wrong: sponsors who hand everything off to IT. ERP is a business transformation, not an IT upgrade. If leadership isn't driving it, it fails.

### Project manager

An ERP project manager needs to understand how SAP or Oracle projects actually run, not just general IT project management. The risks are different, the dependencies are different, and the pressure during cutover is unlike most IT projects.

What goes wrong: a PM who defers to consultants on scope decisions, or who can't hold the business accountable to testing deadlines.

### Business process owners

IT doesn't run your business. Operations, finance, procurement, and HR do. Business process owners are the people who make sure the system works for real-world processes, not just on paper. Ignore them, and you'll end up with a configuration that made sense in a workshop but fails in week one of go-live.

They need to be involved from the beginning, not brought in at UAT to validate decisions they weren't part of making.

### ERP consultants

Good consultants push back. If yours agree with everything and never challenge a requirement, they're not experts. They're billing hours. The best ones help you avoid mistakes before they cost you months and budget.

One sign of a weak consultant: they over-customise because it's easier than explaining why the business needs to change its process. Custom code accumulates. Every Z program is something to maintain, test at upgrade, and explain to the next team.

### Data migration lead

ERP is only as good as the data inside it. Bad data in the old system becomes bad data in the new one. I've seen implementations where financial reports didn't match reality on day one because nobody owned the data quality conversation before migration.

Data migration is a business process that requires business ownership. IT can move the data. Business teams have to confirm it's right.

### Change management and training lead

Change management is not training. It's communication, early involvement, and finding champions inside the business who support the change before go-live. A factory I worked with thought training was enough. Workers didn't trust the new system, went back to their spreadsheets, and work slowed down. Fixing this after go-live was expensive.

The training lead should be building content, running pilots, and measuring readiness, not handing users a PDF two weeks before go-live.

## How many people do you need?

Team size should match complexity, not headcount.

| Company type | Typical team size | What drives complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Small (single entity, under 500 employees) | 10–25 | Mostly standard functionality, few integrations |
| Mid-market (multi-site, 500–5,000 employees) | 30–75 | More integrations, regional process variants, change management at scale |
| Enterprise (global, 5,000+ employees) | 100–500+ | Custom configuration, multiple integrations, compliance across jurisdictions |

A 50-person plant running complex make-to-order manufacturing may need a larger and more specialised team than a 500-person company running standard retail processes. Don't size by headcount. Size by what needs to get done.

## Internal team vs. implementation partner

Most businesses use a combination: an internal core team who understand the business and an implementation partner who brings the technical and methodological depth.

The internal team must be genuinely involved, not just observers. If your internal team is just attending status meetings, the project will deliver a system the partner understood but nobody inside the company knows how to use.

What to expect from a partner: they bring structure, accelerate decisions, and have seen your kind of mistakes before. What not to delegate: scope decisions, process design sign-off, and user readiness. Those need internal ownership.

**Vetting a partner:** ask for references from companies of similar size and industry, not the vendor's showcase clients: real clients who will tell you what the onboarding was actually like.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>How many people are on an ERP implementation team?</summary>

It depends on the complexity of the implementation, not just the size of the company. Small businesses with straightforward processes typically need 10–25 people. Mid-market companies with multiple locations need 30–75. Large enterprises with global operations can need 100–500 or more.

The mistake is sizing by headcount rather than by what needs to be done. A manufacturing company running complex engineer-to-order processes with multiple integrations needs more team capacity than a larger company running standard retail functions.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the role of the project sponsor in an ERP implementation?</summary>

The project sponsor provides executive-level support: securing budget, clearing roadblocks, and making decisions when cross-department conflicts arise. They're not there to manage the project; they're there to ensure it can be managed.

The most important thing a sponsor does is stay engaged through stabilisation, not just up to go-live. Projects that lose executive attention after the cutover weekend tend to develop workarounds that persist for years.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why do ERP implementations need business process owners?</summary>

Because IT doesn't run your business. Business process owners, people from finance, operations, HR, and procurement, know how work actually gets done. Without them, the implementation team designs a system that makes sense on paper but fails in practice.

They need to be involved from the start, in workshops and design decisions, not brought in at UAT to validate choices they weren't part of making. By that point, it's too expensive to change what's wrong.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What should I look for in an ERP consultant?</summary>

The most important quality: willingness to push back. A consultant who agrees with everything is not helping you avoid mistakes. They're making the project easier for themselves. Good consultants challenge bad requirements, flag scope creep, and explain why the standard solution usually serves you better than the custom one.

Also ask: have they worked on projects of similar size and industry? Do they have references you can actually call? And are they proposing to solve your problem or to sell you the engagement they already know how to deliver?

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you handle data migration in an ERP implementation?</summary>

Data migration needs a dedicated lead, not a shared role, not something tacked onto the functional consultant's responsibilities. The data migration lead owns the strategy, cleansing rules, migration loads, and go-live data validation.

The business is responsible for data quality. IT can move the data from one system to another. The business has to confirm that what arrives in the new system is correct. This distinction matters because IT teams often don't know whether a vendor record, a product code, or a chart of accounts entry is actually right. The business does.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is change management in an ERP project and why does it matter?</summary>

Change management is not training. It's the process of preparing people for a significant change to how they work: before, during, and after go-live.

It includes communication (telling people what's changing and why, early), involvement (getting key users into design and testing so they have ownership), and support (identifying champions inside the business who help their colleagues adapt).

Projects that skip change management or treat it as a training calendar routinely face the same outcome: users find workarounds, revert to spreadsheets, and flood IT with complaints. The system works, but nobody trusts it.

</details>
