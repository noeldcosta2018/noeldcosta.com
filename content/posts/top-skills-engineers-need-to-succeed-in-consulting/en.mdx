# Skills engineers need to succeed in consulting

Plenty of engineers think consulting is just about solving hard problems. Deliver the fix, explain the logic, move on. That is part of it.

But what separates engineers who build lasting consulting careers from those who do two or three years and return to technical IT roles is usually softer than a technical skill and harder to quantify. It comes down to how well you navigate ambiguity, how accurately you read business signals, and how you sustain delivery pace when the requirements keep changing.

The first pattern I noticed, across dozens of ERP programmes, is a team structure blind spot. Engineers are trained to work in defined scope. Consulting rarely gives you defined scope. It gives you a project, a deadline, a client who is not always sure what they want, and a team where the boundaries between roles are fluid. Strong engineers who do not understand how their deliverable connects to the functional consultant's design, or how the test manager's workplan depends on their configuration output, slow the whole project down without realising it.

## Why the mindset has to shift

Engineering rewards correctness. Consulting rewards usefulness.

An engineer who produces a technically correct solution that the business cannot understand, cannot use, or that solved the wrong problem is not a successful consultant. They are a technically correct failure. This sounds obvious. It is not obvious when you are under delivery pressure and your instinct is to solve the problem in front of you.

The questions that matter in consulting are different from the questions that matter in engineering. Not "is this right?" but "is this what they actually need?" Not "how does this work?" but "what decision does this enable?" That reframe is not natural for people trained to optimise systems. It requires deliberate practice.

## The skills that actually matter

### Communication

Not presentation skills. Not slide formatting. The ability to translate technical decisions into language that the people making the decisions can understand and act on.

I worked on an S/4HANA implementation where the configuration team had resolved a complex pricing determination issue. Technically solid. The SteerCo update described it in full technical detail. The sponsor asked: "Does this affect our customer invoicing?" The answer was yes, but nobody in the room had framed it that way. The sponsor had been sitting in the meeting for twenty minutes before understanding the business relevance of the decision.

That gap is a communication failure, not a technical one. Training yourself to answer "so what does this mean for the business?" before someone asks is the skill.

### Business acumen

Not finance knowledge, specifically. The ability to understand why the client cares about a decision, not just what the decision is.

Why does the finance director care so much about the goods receipt and invoice verification matching rule? Because it affects when the company can take input tax credit. That is a working capital question. Understanding that context changes how you configure, how you present the options, and how you handle exceptions.

Business acumen develops by being genuinely curious about the business, asking questions that go beyond the requirements document, and reading the business case before you read the functional specification.

### Structured thinking under pressure

The skill that separates good consultants from great ones is the ability to decompose a vague problem in a client meeting and offer a clear path forward without needing a week to go away and think about it.

A client calls an urgent session. A process has broken down post-go-live. Everyone is pointing at different causes. The consultant who can say "let me suggest we look at this in three parts: the system configuration, the master data, and the process execution" and get the room moving has done something that a technically brilliant but unstructured engineer cannot.

That skill is buildable. It requires deliberate practice on problem framing, not just problem solving.

### Adaptability

Consulting projects change. Requirements discovered in week three invalidate decisions made in week one. A key business stakeholder leaves mid-project and their replacement has different priorities. A go-live date is moved by the board without the project team's input.

Engineers who handle change well in consulting are not the ones who stop caring about quality or accuracy. They are the ones who recalibrate quickly, assess what the change actually affects, communicate clearly about the impact, and keep moving. The instinct to resist change because it undermines good technical work is understandable. It is also a career limiter in consulting.

### Ownership and follow-through

The most consistent differentiator I have seen between consultants who build strong client relationships and those who struggle is this: do you own your work from start to finish?

Consultants who say "that is not my area" when they notice a problem outside their defined scope create gaps. Consultants who notice a gap, flag it, and either step in or escalate are the ones clients ask for by name.

This is not about scope creep or doing other people's jobs. It is about taking responsibility for the outcome rather than the deliverable. The deliverable is what you agreed to produce. The outcome is whether it actually worked.

## The skills engineers already have

Most engineers underestimate how much they already have.

Systematic problem diagnosis. Attention to technical detail. Comfort with complexity. The ability to work through uncertainty by building models and testing assumptions. These are consulting skills. They just need to be pointed at the right problems.

The reframe is usually this: engineers are good at analysing systems. Consulting needs people who can analyse organisations, which are messier than systems but respond to the same diagnostic logic. Process mapping, root cause analysis, fit-gap assessment in SAP Activate workshops: these are all versions of the same structured thinking engineers do naturally.

## Common mistakes in the transition

**Optimising for being right rather than being useful.** If the client makes a decision you think is technically suboptimal, your job is to make sure they understand the trade-offs and then support the decision they make. Not to keep arguing after the decision is taken.

**Underinvesting in relationships.** Trust is built between people, not between roles. The relationship with the client's finance lead or the programme manager is worth as much as any technical output over the course of a project. Engineers who see relationship maintenance as overhead rather than delivery are slower to earn the trust that makes difficult conversations possible.

**Confusing activity with progress.** Consulting is measured on outcomes. Being busy does not mean the project is moving forward. The habit of reviewing whether your current activity is on the critical path, or whether it is something you are doing because it feels productive, is a discipline worth building early.

**Avoiding soft escalations.** When something is going wrong and you have doubts about whether to raise it, raise it. The instinct to keep working through a problem before surfacing it is technically sensible and politically often wrong. By the time you have confirmed the problem exists, the stakeholder would have preferred to know earlier.

<details>
<summary>Do engineers make good consultants?</summary>

Yes, with the right mindset shift. Engineers have strong analytical skills, comfort with complexity, and disciplined problem-solving approaches. These translate directly to consulting work, particularly in technical domains like ERP implementation, systems integration, and process design.

The transition challenge is not technical competence. It is communication style, ambiguity tolerance, and the shift from optimising for correctness to optimising for usefulness. Engineering trains you to produce the right answer. Consulting requires you to produce the useful answer in the time available, communicated in a way the client can act on.

Engineers who make this shift successfully tend to build strong consulting careers because they combine technical credibility with structured thinking. Those who struggle tend to have difficulty accepting client decisions they disagree with technically, or to underinvest in the relationship side of the work.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the most important skill for an engineer moving into consulting?</summary>

Communication is the most cited answer, and it is partly right. But the underlying skill that communication depends on is perspective-taking: the ability to understand what the person you are talking to needs to know, not what you want to tell them.

An engineer who masters this can explain a complex technical dependency to a finance director in two sentences and a configuration decision to a developer in precise technical terms. The technical knowledge is the same. The framing is entirely different.

Structured thinking is the second most valuable skill. The ability to decompose an ambiguous problem into its component parts in real time, in a client room, under pressure, is what separates good consultants from people who are technically competent but require clear scope to be effective.

Both skills are learnable. Communication improves with deliberate practice of the "so what does this mean for the business?" framing. Structured thinking improves with practice on issue trees and hypothesis formation on real problems.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long does it take to transition from engineering to consulting?</summary>

The technical transition is usually fast. An engineer who knows a domain well can be productive in a consulting context within months, once they understand the project structure and the client's business context.

The mindset transition takes longer. Consistently choosing usefulness over correctness, building trust with clients rather than just delivering to them, and owning outcomes rather than deliverables: these require deliberate practice and tend to develop over the first two to three years of consulting work.

The engineers who transition fastest are usually the ones who have already had some exposure to ambiguous, client-facing work, either as internal advisors, in pre-sales roles, or on cross-functional project teams. They have already started to develop the interpersonal and framing skills that pure technical roles do not require.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can I stay technical in a consulting role?</summary>

Yes. Technical depth is a competitive advantage in consulting, not a limitation. The most sought-after consultants in ERP and SAP work are people who can talk to the business in business terms and then go into a system and do the technical work themselves.

The risk is being typecast as a pure technical resource. If you only show technical capability and never develop the communication and business acumen skills, you will be deployed on technical tasks indefinitely and excluded from the client conversations where the interesting and career-building work happens.

The goal is both, not one or the other. Technical depth plus the ability to communicate it, translate it to business terms, and work with ambiguity is the combination that defines the strongest consultants at senior levels.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the hardest part of the engineering to consulting transition?</summary>

Ambiguity. Engineering problems, at least in structured development environments, have defined inputs, acceptance criteria, and correct answers. Consulting problems often have none of these.

The client's requirements are incomplete. The business context is politically complex. The right answer depends on constraints that change week to week. The skills that made you effective as an engineer, thoroughness, precision, the drive to find the correct answer, can become liabilities when the problem is fundamentally ambiguous and a good-enough answer now is worth more than a perfect answer in six months.

Learning to be productive under ambiguity, to make reasonable assumptions and document them rather than waiting for certainty, and to commit to a direction while remaining open to revision: this is the hardest shift for most engineers. It does not happen automatically. It requires deliberate practice and a willingness to be uncomfortable.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How important is industry knowledge for an engineer entering consulting?</summary>

More important than most engineers expect. Technical skills transfer across industries. Business judgment does not.

An engineer who understands SAP configuration in manufacturing has to relearn significant ground to be effective in financial services or healthcare. The system knowledge transfers, but the business processes, regulatory context, and client sensitivities are entirely different.

Industry depth is what enables an engineer to anticipate a client's concern before it is stated, to know which configuration decisions will create audit problems later, or to recognise that a proposed process change conflicts with a regulatory requirement. That anticipation is what separates an advisor from an implementer.

The practical implication: early in a consulting career, invest in depth in one or two industries before trying to build breadth. The generalist credibility comes later, once you have enough domain anchors to triangulate from.

</details>
