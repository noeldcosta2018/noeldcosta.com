Engineers succeed in consulting when they add four skills to their technical depth: explaining technical decisions in business terms, understanding why the client cares, breaking a vague problem into parts on the spot, and owning the outcome rather than the task. Most of them already have the analytical habits underneath. What changes is where they point them. If you are an engineer thinking about ERP or SAP consulting, this is what to practise first.

I have noticed that plenty of engineers think consulting is just about solving hard problems. Deliver the fix, explain the logic, move on. That is part of it. In my experience across many ERP programmes, the people who last do more than build. They listen with intent, reframe questions without sounding condescending, and build trust during difficult escalations.

One of the first gaps I noticed in new consultants is that they rarely understand how ERP project teams are structured. You can be a great developer, but if you do not know how your deliverable connects to the functional consultant's design, the test manager's plan or the cutover sequence, you slow the whole team down without realising it.

## Correct is not the same as useful

Engineering rewards correctness. Consulting rewards usefulness.

A technically correct solution that the business cannot understand, cannot use, or that solves the wrong problem is not a consulting success. The questions change. Not "is this right?" but "is this what they need?" Not "how does this work?" but "what decision does this enable?"

I noticed that shift myself during an early SAP rollout. Reading the project charter helped me see the bigger trade-offs behind a single line of code, and I wanted more of that view. Budgets and timelines stopped being abstract too. I remember my first negotiation over shift work during cutover. It was tense, perhaps clumsy, but I saw how a simple head-count change saved real money.

## The skills that matter most

### Communication

Not slides. The ability to say what a technical decision means for the people who have to act on it.

A habit that helps: before any update to a sponsor, answer "so what does this mean for the business?" yourself. If a pricing configuration change affects customer invoices, lead with the invoices. The technical detail goes second, if at all.

Switching from debug mode to steering committee language in the same morning takes more energy than most engineers expect. I keep a three-line cue card that reminds me of audience, risk and next step. It feels minimal, yet it resets my head between meetings. Long travel weeks add another layer. No chart covers how strange it feels to explain a design at 9 p.m. after a delayed flight. Recognising that fatigue early helps prevent the terse emails that spark escalations.

### Business sense

Not accounting knowledge as such. Understanding why the client cares about a decision.

Why does a finance director care so much about the tolerance limits in the purchase order, goods receipt and invoice match? Because they decide how many supplier invoices get blocked, which affects supplier relationships, early-payment discounts and the cash forecast. Knowing that changes how you configure the tolerances and how you present the options.

Learning who really owns each decision matters as much. Mapping who controls which budget felt soft to me at first. It saved me from pushing a change that finance had no appetite to fund. My guide to [what consultants actually do](/what-consultants-actually-do-beyond-using-buzzwords/) covers this side of the job.

### Structured thinking under pressure

A process breaks after go-live. Everyone points at a different cause. The consultant who says "let's look at this in three parts: configuration, master data and how the process was executed" gets the room moving. That is a learnable skill, built by practising issue trees and hypotheses on real problems. My article on [structured thinking and problem solving](/structured-thinking-problem-solving/) shows the method.

### Adaptability

Requirements found in week three overturn decisions made in week one. A key business lead leaves and the replacement has different priorities. A board moves the go-live date. Engineers who handle this well do not stop caring about quality. They work out what the change actually affects, say so clearly and keep moving.

### Ownership

Consultants do not say "that is not my area". If you see a gap, step into it, or at least raise it. That is not scope creep. It is taking responsibility for whether the work succeeded, not just whether you produced what was agreed.

## Practise before you switch

You do not need a consulting title to start. The smartest transitions I have seen came from engineers who quietly began adjusting how they worked months earlier.

| Skill | What good looks like | How to practise in your current job |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | A sponsor understands the impact of your work in two sentences | Write a three-line business summary for every technical change you ship |
| Business sense | You can say why the business cares about the requirement | Read the business case before the specification; ask finance what a change costs them |
| Structured thinking | You can split a vague problem into parts in the meeting | Sketch an issue tree before every incident review |
| Adaptability | You reassess quickly when scope changes | After each change request, write down what it affects and what it does not |
| Ownership | You raise gaps outside your scope early | Flag one cross-team risk a month to the person who owns it |
| Team awareness | You know who depends on your output and when | Map your deliverables to the functional, test and cutover plans on your current project |

## What AI tools have changed for new consultants

The skills above have not changed. The entry point has.

SAP now ships AI help aimed directly at consulting work. Joule for consultants answers configuration and ABAP questions grounded in SAP documentation, and Joule is available inside the SAP Activate Roadmap Viewer. Joule Studio in SAP Build lets developers build custom Joule skills (generally available July 2025) and Joule agents ([generally available December 2025](https://community.sap.com/t5/artificial-intelligence-blogs-posts/agent-builder-in-joule-studio-is-now-generally-ava/ba-p/14289282)). Similar tools exist in every major ERP.

My view of what that means for an engineer moving into consulting:

1. **Routine drafting is cheaper.** First drafts of configuration notes, code and test cases come faster. The value moves to checking them.
2. **Judgement is worth more.** When a tool produces a plausible answer in seconds, the person who can tell plausible from correct becomes more valuable. Engineers with deep functional knowledge from their previous roles arrive well placed.
3. **Agent design is a new skill.** Designing an agent's steps, guardrails and human hand-off points is close to the state-machine and process thinking engineers already do.
4. **Explaining matters more.** The tool drafts. You explain what it got right, what it missed and what you recommend.

If you are planning the move into SAP or ERP consulting, [SAPopedia](https://www.sapopedia.com/) maps the career paths and courses, and if your CV is what is holding you back, [ERPCV](https://erpcv.com/) rebuilds it around your project delivery.

## Mistakes I see in the transition

I have made some of these myself and watched good colleagues trip over them.

**Arguing after the decision.** If the client chooses an option you think is technically weaker, make sure they understand the trade-off, then support the decision.

**Treating relationships as overhead.** Trust is built between people. The relationship with the client's finance lead is worth as much as any technical output over a project.

**Confusing activity with progress.** Check regularly whether what you are doing is on the critical path or just feels productive.

**Sitting on bad news.** When something is going wrong and you are unsure whether to raise it, raise it. Waiting until you have confirmed the problem is technically sensible and politically wrong.

Consulting can also feel unsteady. Travel, late scope changes and vague requirements test patience, and some engineers miss the depth of owning one product for years. There is no perfect path. I still juggle that tension myself.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Do engineers make good consultants?</summary>

Yes, with a change of mindset. Engineers bring analytical skill, comfort with complexity and disciplined problem solving, which suit ERP and systems consulting well. The hard part is moving from the correct answer to the useful answer, delivered in time and explained so the client can act on it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the most important skill for an engineer moving into consulting?</summary>

Communication, built on understanding what the other person needs to know. Close behind is structured thinking: breaking an ambiguous problem into parts in real time, in front of a client. Both improve with deliberate practice.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long does the move from engineering to consulting take?</summary>

The technical side can take months once you understand the project structure and the client's business. The mindset side, choosing usefulness over correctness and owning outcomes, usually develops over the first two to three years. Engineers with earlier exposure to client-facing or cross-functional work move faster.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can I stay technical in a consulting role?</summary>

Yes. Technical depth is an advantage. The most sought-after ERP consultants can talk to the business in business terms and then do the technical work themselves. The risk is being typecast as a purely technical resource and left out of the conversations where careers are built.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Will AI tools replace junior consultants?</summary>

They change the work rather than remove it. Tools such as Joule for consultants and Joule Studio take on routine drafting and some automation. Checking the output, explaining it to the client and designing agents and controls are the parts that grow.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How important is industry knowledge for an engineer entering consulting?</summary>

More than most engineers expect. System knowledge transfers between industries; business judgement does not. Early in a consulting career, build depth in one or two industries before going broad. That depth is what lets you spot an audit or regulatory problem before the client raises it.

</details>
