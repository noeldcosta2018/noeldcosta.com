An AI strategy for a bank has four stages: build a governed data foundation, add an intelligence layer your regulator can follow, scale AI agents across onboarding, credit, fraud and AML, then embed AI in the bank's key functions. Banks that skip the first two end up with clever pilots that never pass model risk review.

Through my role at DXC I have worked with banks across the Middle East and Africa. The ambition is rarely the problem. The problem is that customer, account and transaction data sit in a core banking system, a card platform, a CRM and a dozen spreadsheets, each with its own definition of a customer.

This roadmap is technology neutral. It works with any core banking platform, any cloud and any model provider your regulator will accept.

Each stage earns its keep. The foundation alone gives you trusted data, faster delivery and stronger regulatory reporting.

The intelligence layer brings the first AI value in engagement, retention and staff productivity. Agents bring value at scale: lower cost to serve, growth and lower risk.

## Now: build the data foundation

Banks already know why data matters, because regulators made them. The BCBS 239 principles on risk data aggregation ask for accurate, complete and timely risk data with clear ownership. A good AI foundation is the same discipline extended to customer and product data.

1. **A modern data platform.** A cloud or in-country lakehouse that brings together core banking, cards, payments, lending, CRM and digital channel data.
2. **Governance, catalogue and lineage.** Every figure in a regulatory return and every feature in a model should trace back to its source.
3. **Self-service reporting.** Product, risk and finance teams work from the same customer and balance figures.

The hardest part is the single customer view. One person can be a retail customer, a card holder, a guarantor on a business loan and a director of a corporate client. Resolve that once, in the foundation, not in every model.

## Year 1: a governed intelligence layer

The core of year one is a semantic layer: one business language that both people and AI agents understand. Customer, account, product, limit, exposure, transaction and complaint each get one definition. An agent asked about "active customers" must use the same definition as the board pack.

AI governance comes in the same year, aligned to your regulator.

In the UAE that means the joint guidelines for financial institutions adopting enabling technologies. In the UK, the PRA's model risk management principles (SS1/23). In the EU, the AI Act treats creditworthiness assessment of individuals as high-risk.

Build one register of AI use cases with owners, risk ratings, testing and human review, and map it to all of them.

Then deliver quick wins that customers and staff notice.

1. **A personal spending advisor.** Customers see where their money goes and get prompts to save, inside the app they already use.
2. **Staff copilots.** Relationship managers and contact-centre staff ask questions of policies, product terms and customer history.
3. **Retention prompts.** Signals of a customer about to leave reach the right person while there is still time to act.

## Year 2: scale AI agents across the bank

Year two moves from assistants to agents that do work within limits you set. In banking, the volume and the value sit in four places.

1. **Onboarding and KYC.** The agent reads identity and company documents, checks them against registers and screening lists, and prepares the file for a person to approve.
2. **Credit.** It gathers statements, bureau data and financials, spreads them and drafts the credit memo. The decision stays with a credit officer.
3. **Fraud.** It scores transactions in near real time and holds the suspicious ones, with an explanation an analyst can check.
4. **AML.** It triages alerts, gathers the evidence and drafts the case narrative, so investigators spend time on the alerts that matter.

The same year adds near real-time customer engagement. Salary planning, cash-flow forecasts for small businesses and the next best action reach customers when they act, not in a monthly campaign.

The operating model changes too. Each agent needs an owner, defined authority, monitoring and a kill switch. Model risk teams should review agents the way they review models.

## Year 3: AI embedded at scale

By year three AI sits inside the key business functions: retail lending, cards, SME banking, treasury, operations and risk. People and agents work together, and every decision path is governed end to end.

Data sharing extends the reach. Open banking APIs and richer ISO 20022 payment data let agents see more of the customer's financial life, with consent.

Partners plug into the same governed layer. The result is sustained value and a customer experience competitors find hard to copy.

## Across every stage

Four commitments run through the whole roadmap.

1. **In-country and sovereign AI options.** Many regulators expect customer data to stay in the country. Choose platforms and models that can run there, including open models you host yourself.
2. **An open, multi-platform ecosystem.** Avoid tying the bank to one AI vendor. Models change every few months.
3. **Governance aligned to your regulator.** One AI register, one model risk process, mapped to every regulation you answer to.
4. **Your people upskilled.** Relationship managers, analysts and investigators need to know how to work with agents and when to overrule them.

## Where bank AI strategies go wrong

The first mistake is starting with a customer-facing chatbot. It is visible, it is risky, and without a semantic layer it gives different answers to the same question. Internal copilots and operations agents are safer places to learn.

The second is building AI outside model risk management. A pilot that never went through validation cannot go into production, and the team has to start again. Bring risk and compliance in from the first use case.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What should a bank's AI strategy include?</summary>

A governed data foundation, a semantic layer and AI governance aligned to the regulator, AI agents for high-volume processes such as onboarding, credit, fraud and AML, and a plan to embed AI in key business functions. In-country options, an open stack and staff training run through all of it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Where should a bank start with AI?</summary>

Start with the data foundation and internal use cases. Staff copilots, complaint summaries and a personal spending advisor deliver value with manageable risk. Credit decisions come later, with the strongest controls and human review.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is AI for credit scoring high-risk under the EU AI Act?</summary>

Yes. The EU AI Act lists AI systems used to assess the creditworthiness of individuals or set their credit score as high-risk, with fraud detection as an exception. High-risk systems need risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight and monitoring.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do AI agents differ from AI assistants in banking?</summary>

An assistant answers questions for a person. An agent takes actions within limits you set: it gathers documents, triages alerts or drafts a credit memo. In banking, agents should prepare decisions and a person should approve them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can banks use AI and keep data in the country?</summary>

Yes. Many cloud providers offer in-country regions, and open models can run on infrastructure the bank controls. Decide which data can leave the country before choosing platforms and models.

</details>
