The best ERP for manufacturing depends on your size and complexity. For large, complex or regulated manufacturers I recommend SAP S/4HANA, and for most mid-sized manufacturers of about 200 to 1,000 staff I recommend Epicor Kinetic.

Small manufacturers should start with Acumatica. Below: a comparison table, recommendations by type and size, and ERP vs MES.

**Short answer:** SAP S/4HANA for large, multi-plant and regulated manufacturers. Epicor Kinetic for mid-sized manufacturers, especially discrete and make-to-order. Acumatica for small manufacturers. Add an MES such as SAP Digital Manufacturing or Siemens Opcenter on top when the shop floor needs real-time control.

I have been in enough ERP rollouts to know what survives past go-live and what does not. I have worked with SAP S/4HANA, Epicor Kinetic, Infor M3, Acumatica, SAP Digital Manufacturing and Siemens Opcenter, seen them hold up, and know where each one fits.

Most manufacturers buy on feature lists. The features that matter are the ones your team will use under shift-work pressure.

## What a manufacturing ERP has to do

Manufacturing ERP is a different animal from ERP for retail or professional services. You are juggling bills of materials, routings, shift schedules, machine calibrations, scrap rates and lot traceability. A generic ERP that "does manufacturing" usually tracks inventory and stops there.

Ask it to trace a defective part back to its production batch and it falls apart. Six capabilities separate a manufacturing ERP from a general one:

1. **Inventory management** for raw materials, work in progress and finished goods in one place
2. **Production planning and scheduling** that lines up labour, machines and materials with demand
3. **Material requirements planning (MRP)** so you neither over-order nor run out
4. **Shop floor control** that records output, downtime and deviations, or passes them in from an MES
5. **Quality management** for inspections, audits and nonconformances
6. **Lot and serial traceability**, required in food, pharma and defence and increasingly in automotive

## The systems in detail

I have split this into two groups. The first four I have worked with. The rest are on most manufacturing shortlists, and I describe them from the vendors' own material, not from my own programmes.

### SAP S/4HANA (via RISE or GROW)

My rule is simple: SAP for manufacturing, public sector and regulated, operations-heavy organisations; Oracle for finance-led organisations and professional services. I have led or rescued 21 SAP programmes, and for large and complex manufacturers S/4HANA is still the benchmark. Production planning, materials, quality and plant maintenance share one data model with finance.

[SAP S/4HANA](https://www.sap.com/products/erp/s4hana.html) comes in two commercial bundles. RISE with SAP runs the private edition and suits large, multi-plant estates. GROW with SAP runs the public edition, is for new implementations only and keeps you on standard processes.

My [SAP PP guide](/sap-pp-production-planning/) explains the production planning module in detail.

It is expensive and complex. A GROW programme takes about 6 to 9 months and a RISE programme 9 to 18 months, and partners routinely quote the short end.

**Who should use it:** large, multi-plant or regulated manufacturers (pharma, defence, aerospace, food at scale), and companies already on SAP ECC planning their move before mainstream maintenance ends in 2027.

### Epicor Kinetic

This is my recommendation for a mid-sized manufacturer of about 200 to 1,000 staff. Reliable on the shop floor. Scheduling and shop floor execution do what they say, and most teams get the hang of it quickly.

Epicor describes [Kinetic](https://www.epicor.com/en/products/enterprise-resource-planning-erp/epicor-kinetic/) as a cloud ERP built for manufacturers and specialised for discrete, make-to-order manufacturing. It is cloud-focused but still offers on-premises and hybrid deployment, which matters for plants with patchy connectivity.

Not the flashiest system. One you can trust on the floor.

**Weakness:** if you run heavy batch or formula-based process manufacturing, check the process functions carefully against Sage X3 or Infor M3.

### Infor

Infor sells two manufacturing ERPs, so make sure you are comparing the right one. Infor CloudSuite Industrial (the product many still call SyteLine) is built for discrete and mixed-mode manufacturers and job shops. [Infor M3](https://www.infor.com/products/m3) is aimed at food and beverage, chemicals, fashion, distribution and equipment companies.

The one I have worked with is Infor M3. I describe CloudSuite Industrial from Infor's own material.

CloudSuite Industrial suits manufacturers that need depth in production scheduling. It handles many concurrent orders on shared equipment, where lighter systems start to show gaps.

**Weakness:** the interface works but looks dated next to newer cloud products.

### Acumatica

Good value for smaller manufacturers that want to grow without re-implementing. Acumatica's [manufacturing edition](https://www.acumatica.com/cloud-erp-software/manufacturing-management/) covers discrete and process production on one platform, and licensing is not per user, so you can put the whole plant on the system.

Acumatica publishes a packaged offer, APEX for Manufacturing, with support and implementation services built into a starting price under US$2,185 a month (acumatica.com, October 2026). It runs as SaaS, and Acumatica also sells private cloud and on-premise licences.

It lacks the depth of Epicor or Infor for complex shop-floor scenarios. For straightforward production without heavy MES needs, it delivers.

### Oracle Fusion Cloud ERP and NetSuite

Oracle sells two ERPs that manufacturers shortlist. Oracle's documentation says [Fusion Cloud Manufacturing](https://docs.oracle.com/en/cloud/saas/supply-chain-and-manufacturing/26a/faumf/overview-of-oracle-manufacturing-cloud.html) supports discrete and process manufacturing, including both in the same plant. Under my rule, Fusion fits a finance-led group that also manufactures, more than a plant-centred business.

NetSuite is Oracle's cloud ERP for small and mid-sized companies, often chosen by multi-entity manufacturers with online sales. Manufacturing depth is adequate rather than deep, and add-on modules raise the price. My [SAP vs Oracle comparison](/sap-vs-oracle-which-erp-is-better-for-your-business/) goes deeper on that choice.

### Microsoft Dynamics 365

Dynamics 365 Supply Chain Management, sold with Dynamics 365 Finance, suits companies standardised on Microsoft 365 and Azure. It is mostly bought as cloud, though Microsoft still documents an on-premises option. Its AI features need clean master data to be useful.

### IFS Cloud

When assets matter as much as inventory, IFS belongs on the list: aerospace, defence, energy and utilities. IFS Cloud brings ERP, enterprise asset management and field service into one product, and IFS lets you [run it as a service or on your own premises](https://www.ifs.com/ifs-cloud/choice) on the same software.

The depth brings implementation effort. Do not plan a six-month rollout without serious resource commitment.

### Sage X3

Sage positions X3 for chemical, food and beverage, distribution, equipment and industrial manufacturing businesses, with production management, BOM planning, shop floor control and quality control. It is available in the cloud or on-premise. Its strength is batch management and lot traceability for mid-sized process manufacturers that do not want the weight of an SAP programme.

## ERP vs MES: what is the difference?

An ERP plans and accounts for production. A manufacturing execution system (MES) runs it on the shop floor, in real time, and reports back.

Siemens, which makes Opcenter, puts it in ISA-95 terms. [ERP is a Level 4 system for business logistics, and MES a Level 3 system](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/opcenter/manufacturing-execution-systems-mes-and-enterprise-resource-planning-erp-systems-how-they-relate) that runs production over seconds to shifts. The ERP sends production demand down; the MES sends material consumption, scrap and completions back so the ERP can cost them.

<explainer-layers title="Where the MES sits between ERP and the machines" caption="The MES sits on top of the ERP's plan and below it in time: the ERP thinks in days and weeks, the MES in seconds and shifts." layers="ERP (ISA-95 Level 4) => Orders, MRP, costing, finance: SAP S/4HANA, Epicor Kinetic, Infor, Acumatica|MES (Level 3) => Work order execution, quality checks, genealogy, OEE: SAP Digital Manufacturing, Siemens Opcenter|Machines and sensors (Levels 0 to 2) => PLCs, SCADA, historians, test rigs" tones="|accent|"></explainer-layers>

The two MES products I have worked with both sit on top of the ERP. SAP's current MES is SAP Digital Manufacturing, which receives master data and production orders from S/4HANA or ECC and sends order confirmations back. [Siemens Opcenter](https://www.siemens.com/global/en/products/automation/topic-areas/opcenter.html) is Siemens' manufacturing operations management portfolio, with Opcenter Execution as its MES.

| Question | ERP | MES |
|---|---|---|
| Main job | Plan, buy, cost and account | Execute, record and control production |
| Time horizon | Days, weeks, months | Seconds to shifts |
| Typical users | Planners, buyers, finance, management | Operators, supervisors, quality engineers |
| Key records | Sales orders, MRP, inventory, costs | Work order steps, machine data, batch genealogy |
| Examples | SAP S/4HANA, Epicor Kinetic, Infor, Acumatica | SAP Digital Manufacturing, Siemens Opcenter |

You need both when one of these is true:

1. You run several plants and need one production standard across them
2. Regulators or customers expect full batch or serial genealogy (pharma, food, aerospace, automotive)
3. You need real-time machine data, OEE or electronic work instructions at the line
4. The ERP's own shop floor module cannot keep up with your volume or speed

A single mid-sized plant on Epicor Kinetic or Acumatica often runs fine on the ERP's shop floor functions alone. Once the plant starts producing sensor and machine data at volume, that data needs a home outside the ERP; my guide on [what Databricks is used for](/what-is-databricks-used-for/) covers that layer.

## Implementation challenges in manufacturing ERP

Whichever system you pick, these problems decide whether it works.

1. **Unclear goals.** Write the success measures before you select a vendor, not after go-live.
2. **Over-customisation.** Every legacy habit gets forced into the new system. Map workflows first and change the process before changing the software.
3. **Dirty master data.** Bills of materials, routings and item masters are always worse than the first estimate. Name data owners before the project starts.
4. **Shop floor left out.** Put supervisors in the demos and the testing. A system the floor rejects gets worked around, and a system being worked around is worse than no system.
5. **ERP and MES scoped separately.** Agree which system owns each record, such as confirmations, scrap and batch genealogy, before either is configured.
6. **Training underestimated.** Untrained users go back to spreadsheets. Budget for shift-based training, not one classroom session.
7. **No plan for after go-live.** Plan stabilisation and hypercare before go-live, with named support for every shift.

Timelines slip in the same places. On SAP, a GROW programme takes about 6 to 9 months and RISE 9 to 18 months, and data migration is the usual reason a plan moves.

## Cloud or on-premise for manufacturing ERP

Cloud is the default for most new manufacturing ERP projects. You do not run infrastructure, and adding a plant is easier. The trade-off is timing: on public cloud you take the release whether or not it suits your production calendar.

Most vendors on this list still let you choose. Epicor Kinetic offers cloud, on-premises and hybrid; Acumatica, IFS Cloud and Sage X3 offer cloud or on-premise; NetSuite and Oracle Fusion are cloud only. For SAP, most new manufacturing programmes go to RISE (private cloud) or GROW (public cloud), and on-premise S/4HANA is still sold.

On-premise still makes sense for high-security environments, plants with unreliable connectivity and data-residency rules. Shop-floor connectivity matters more here than in an office: if the line stops when the internet drops, design for local execution, often in the MES.
