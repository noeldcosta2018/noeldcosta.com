There is no single best ERP for manufacturing. For global, multi-plant or heavily regulated manufacturers, SAP S/4HANA is still the benchmark. For mid-market discrete shops, Epicor Kinetic or Infor CloudSuite Industrial usually fit better. Process manufacturers in the mid-market should look at Sage X3 or Infor M3, asset-heavy industries at IFS Cloud, and small manufacturers at Acumatica or Cetec. This guide is for owners, COOs and CIOs shortlisting manufacturing ERP in 2026. Start with the recommendations table near the end, then read the detail on the two or three systems it points you to.

Manufacturing ERP is a different animal from ERP for retail or professional services. You are juggling bills of materials, routings, shift schedules, machine calibrations, scrap rates and lot traceability. A generic ERP that "does manufacturing" usually tracks inventory and stops there. Ask it to trace a defective part back to its production batch and it falls apart.

I have been in enough ERP rollouts to know what survives past go-live and what does not. I have worked with the systems below, seen them hold up, and know where each one fits. Most manufacturers buy on feature lists. The features that matter are the ones your team will use under shift-work pressure.

## What a manufacturing ERP has to do

Six capabilities separate a manufacturing ERP from a general one:

1. **Inventory management** for raw materials, work in progress and finished goods in one place
2. **Production planning and scheduling** that lines up labour, machines and materials with demand
3. **Material requirements planning (MRP)** so you neither over-order nor run out
4. **Shop floor execution** that records output, downtime and deviations as they happen
5. **Quality management** for inspections, audits and nonconformances
6. **Lot and serial traceability**, required in food, pharma and defence and increasingly in automotive

## The systems in detail

### SAP S/4HANA (via RISE or GROW)

SAP still leads at enterprise scale. When you need MES integration, batch traceability across several plants and a system that handles complexity without blinking, S/4HANA is the benchmark. Production logic sits at its core, and finance and operations share one data model.

It is expensive and complex. Implementation teams are not cheap, and change management is substantial.

The newer option is GROW with SAP, built on S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition. It is for new implementations only, runs SAP's standard processes and allows no modification of the core. In return it brings SAP within reach of mid-market manufacturers in a way RISE never did.

**Who should use it:** global manufacturers with multi-plant complexity or regulatory weight (pharma, defence, aerospace), and companies already on SAP ECC planning their move before mainstream maintenance ends in 2027. Mid-market manufacturers willing to adopt standard processes should look at GROW.

**Weakness:** cost and timeline are routinely underestimated. A partner will quote 12 months. Add buffer. Clean-core discipline now limits the heavy ABAP customisation many ECC manufacturers relied on. Good for cost control, painful if your processes depend on it. My guide to [SAP in manufacturing](/sap-manufacturing-industry-secrets/) covers what makes it work on the floor.

### Oracle NetSuite

Cloud-only ERP that brings finance, supply chain, inventory and production into one system, with a long track record in multi-location mid-market manufacturing.

Manufacturing depth is adequate rather than deep. Work orders, bills of materials, demand planning and basic shop floor control are handled well. Constraint-based scheduling or tight MES integration will stretch it.

**Who should use it:** fast-growing manufacturers that have outgrown entry-level accounting software and are not ready for SAP. Good for multi-entity set-ups with e-commerce or direct sales.

**Weakness:** add-on modules are often needed, and pricing climbs with them. Get a three-year price in writing before you sign, not just year one. Customisation needs SuiteScript skills.

### Microsoft Dynamics 365

Works well in Microsoft-heavy environments. Strong finance, good Power BI integration, and Power Automate cuts integration effort. Mixed-mode manufacturers, running both discrete and process production, often find it a good fit.

The AI features need clean data to be useful. On messy master data, the AI layer adds noise. Fix data quality first.

**Who should use it:** companies standardised on Azure and Microsoft 365.

**Weakness:** manufacturing functions are less mature than Epicor or Infor for pure shop-floor work.

### Infor CloudSuite Industrial

Built for job shops and discrete manufacturers (the product many still call SyteLine). Its advanced planning and scheduling and PLM features handle engineer-to-order and mixed-mode work without becoming heavy.

It suits manufacturers that need depth in production scheduling, especially many concurrent orders on shared equipment, where NetSuite or Dynamics would show gaps. For process industries such as food and chemicals, Infor's M3 product is the better-aligned option.

**Who should use it:** mid-market discrete and mixed-mode manufacturers with complex scheduling.

**Weakness:** the interface works but looks dated next to newer cloud products.

### Epicor Kinetic

Reliable on the shop floor. MES, scheduling and shop floor execution do what they say, and most teams get the hang of it quickly. It is not over-engineered for the job it does.

Not the flashiest system. One you can trust on the floor.

**Who should use it:** discrete manufacturers that want proven shop-floor capability without SAP's complexity or price.

**Weakness:** finance lags the operational side. Some companies run a separate finance system alongside it.

### IFS Cloud

When assets matter as much as inventory (aerospace, defence, energy, utilities), IFS makes sense. It combines ERP, enterprise asset management (EAM) and field service management (FSM) in one product. Few competitors do.

The depth brings implementation effort. Do not plan a six-month rollout without serious resource commitment.

**Who should use it:** asset-intensive manufacturers in regulated industries where equipment lifecycle, maintenance and compliance drive operations.

### Acumatica

Good value for smaller manufacturers that want to grow without re-implementing. Browser-based and modular. Its licensing charges for the applications you use and the resources you consume, with unlimited users, which helps when you want the whole plant on the system.

It lacks the depth of Epicor or Infor for complex shop-floor scenarios. For straightforward production without heavy MES needs, it delivers.

**Who should use it:** small to mid-market manufacturers that want modern cloud ERP without enterprise cost or complexity.

### Sage X3

Aimed at mid-market process manufacturing: pharmaceuticals, food and beverage, chemicals. Batch management, lot traceability and regulatory compliance are built in. Better for process than discrete. For a mid-sized regulated process manufacturer, it can deliver GxP batch traceability without the weight of an SAP programme.

**Who should use it:** mid-market process manufacturers that need traceability and compliance without SAP's overhead.

### DELMIAWorks and Cetec ERP

DELMIAWorks (formerly IQMS, now part of Dassault Systèmes) is built for small discrete manufacturers, with strong shop floor control. Cetec is the low-cost option: cloud, quick to deploy, easy to scale for small shops that do not want to overinvest.

If budget is the main constraint and the operation is simple, Cetec is worth a serious look.

## Common implementation challenges

Whichever system you pick, these are the problems that decide whether it works. Most of them are decided before the project starts.

| Challenge | What goes wrong | How to avoid it |
|---|---|---|
| **Unclear goals** | No KPIs or success criteria at the start | Write the success measures before you select a vendor |
| **Over-customisation** | Every legacy habit forced into the new system | Map workflows first; change the process before changing the software |
| **Change management underestimated** | Users resist; training is rushed | Name a change champion in each team; communicate early |
| **Dirty data** | Bad master data causes go-live errors | Name data owners before the project starts |
| **Scope creep** | Features added mid-project | Define a minimum scope for go-live; phase the rest |
| **Inadequate testing** | Go-live without full process tests | Test end to end with real data, especially finance flows |
| **No post-go-live plan** | Support fades; users drift back to spreadsheets | Plan stabilisation and hypercare before go-live |

Training is always underestimated. Untrained users do not use the system; they work around it, which defeats the point. Data cleanup always runs long too. Build extra time into both.

## Cloud or on-premise for manufacturing

Cloud is the default for most new manufacturing ERP projects. Updates arrive automatically, you do not run infrastructure, and scaling is easier. The trade-off is control over update timing: you get the new release whether or not it suits your production calendar.

For SAP, most new manufacturing programmes go to RISE (private cloud) or GROW (public cloud). On-premise S/4HANA is still sold, and is usually chosen for data-residency, sovereignty or plant-connectivity reasons. If you run SAP Solution Manager today, note that its mainstream maintenance ends at the end of 2027, with limited extended maintenance to 2030. SAP points customers to [SAP Cloud ALM](https://support.sap.com/en/alm/faq.html) as the successor.

On-premise still makes sense for high-security environments, plants with unreliable connectivity, and integration architectures that cloud models do not support. Most vendors in this list offer both, so the decision usually comes down to IT policy rather than ERP capability.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>How do I choose the best ERP for manufacturing?</summary>

Start with your production type (discrete make-to-order or make-to-stock, process with batches and formulas, or mixed-mode) and your biggest operational gaps. Filter systems against those gaps, not against vendor feature lists.

Get shop-floor supervisors into the demos early. What looks smooth in a sales presentation can look very different to a supervisor logging a work order at the start of a shift. Their reaction matters more than the analyst score.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the most widely used ERP in manufacturing?</summary>

SAP is the most widely deployed among large manufacturers. In the mid-market, Epicor, Infor, NetSuite, Dynamics 365 and now GROW with SAP are all common.

Most widely used is not the same as best fit. SAP's position at enterprise level reflects its depth, not that it suits every operation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between MES and MRP?</summary>

A manufacturing execution system (MES) manages real-time shop floor activity: machine data, labour, quality events and work in progress. It answers "what is happening on the floor right now?"

Material requirements planning (MRP) works out what to buy and when, based on demand and lead times. It answers "what do I need to order to meet the schedule?"

Most manufacturing ERPs include MRP. MES is either a separate system integrated with the ERP or part of its shop floor module. The more complex the production, the more likely you need a dedicated MES.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is there a free ERP for manufacturing?</summary>

ERPNext is open source and free to self-host. It covers manufacturing basics: bills of materials, production orders and inventory.

The catch is that self-hosting needs in-house technical skills for set-up, maintenance and upgrades. It works for small manufacturers with technical people and simple processes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is SAP used for in manufacturing?</summary>

SAP S/4HANA covers production planning (PP), plant maintenance (PM), quality management (QM), materials management (MM) and the financial integration of all of them in one system. At scale it handles multi-plant scheduling, lot traceability, integration with MES and SCADA systems, and global compliance.

It is complex and expensive. For large manufacturers, the depth justifies it. My [SAP PP guide](/sap-pp-production-planning/) explains the production planning module in detail.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the biggest mistakes in manufacturing ERP selection?</summary>

Three come up repeatedly: buying features you will never use, underestimating implementation effort (especially data migration and training), and choosing on vendor demos without the people who will use the system every day.

The third is the most costly. A system that is technically right but unacceptable to shop-floor staff gets worked around. A system being worked around is worse than no system at all.

</details>
