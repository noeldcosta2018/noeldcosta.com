Manufacturing ERP is not like ERP for retail or professional services. You are juggling bills of materials, routings, shift schedules, machine calibrations, scrap rates, lot traceability. A generic ERP might claim it "does manufacturing," but that usually means it tracks inventory and stops there. When you need to trace a defective part back to the original production batch, it falls apart.

Across 21 SAP S/4HANA programmes plus advisory engagements on the major non-SAP manufacturing ERPs, I have seen what survives past go-live and what does not. Most manufacturers buy on feature lists. The features that matter are the ones your team will actually use under shift-work pressure.

The 2026 landscape has shifted enough to warrant a fresh look. SAP introduced GROW with SAP (S/4HANA Cloud public edition) as a credible mid-market manufacturing offer through 2023-2024. RISE with SAP became the default for net-new enterprise S/4HANA. Joule shipped across the SAP product family including the manufacturing scope. The comparison below reflects where these products actually sit today.

## What manufacturing ERP needs to do

Core requirements that matter in a production environment:

1. **Inventory management**: raw materials, WIP, finished goods, tracked in one place
2. **Production planning and scheduling**: aligning labour, machines, and materials with demand
3. **Material requirements planning (MRP)**: so you are not ordering parts you do not need or running out of ones you do
4. **Shop floor execution**: tracking output, downtime, and process deviations in real time
5. **Quality control**: inspections, audits, nonconformances. Without it, defects slip through
6. **Lot and serial traceability**: required in food, pharma, defence, and increasingly in automotive

## The ten systems in detail

### SAP S/4HANA (via RISE or GROW)

SAP still leads at enterprise scale. When you need deep MES integration, batch traceability across multiple plants, and a system that handles complexity without blinking, S/4HANA is the benchmark. It is built with production logic at its core: finance and operations actually talk to each other.

The honest qualification: it is expensive and complex. RISE with SAP private edition typically runs $20M to $80M+ for enterprise programmes. Implementation teams are not cheap. Change management is substantial. The new variable in 2026 is GROW with SAP (S/4HANA Cloud public edition) at $2M to $6M for mid-market manufacturers with 50 to 1,000 staff. GROW is greenfield-only and forces a clean-core posture, but it brings SAP within reach for the mid-market in a way RISE never did.

On a GCC food and beverage programme in 2024 the client landed on RISE with SAP private edition for multi-plant batch traceability across three sites. The total programme cost ran inside $30M, which was the upper end of their business case but inside the realistic range.

**Who should use it:** Global manufacturers with multi-plant complexity, regulatory requirements (pharma, defence, aerospace), or operations already on SAP ECC considering migration to RISE. Mid-market manufacturers willing to adopt standard processes should look at GROW.

**Honest weakness:** Cost and implementation timeline are often underestimated. Vendors will quote 12 months. Add buffer. The clean-core posture under RISE and GROW now constrains historical SAP-side customisation overrun, which is a good thing for cost discipline but a constraint for organisations that depended on heavy ABAP customisation in ECC.

### Oracle NetSuite

Cloud-only ERP that unifies finance, supply chain, inventory, and production. 37,000+ installs, with a solid track record in multi-location mid-market manufacturing.

Manufacturing functionality is adequate but not deep. Work orders, BOMs, demand planning, shop floor control: it handles the basics well. For anything requiring constraint-based scheduling or tight MES integration, you will be stretching it.

**Who should use it:** Fast-growing manufacturers who have outgrown QuickBooks and are not ready for SAP complexity. Good for multi-entity setups with e-commerce or direct sales.

**Honest weakness:** Add-on modules are often necessary and pricing escalates. The NetSuite three-year total often runs 40 to 60 percent above the year-one quote. Customisation requires SuiteScript expertise.

### Microsoft Dynamics 365

Works well in Microsoft-heavy environments. Strong finance capabilities, good Power BI integration, and Power Automate reduces integration effort significantly. Mixed-mode manufacturers (those running both discrete and process production) often find it a good fit.

AI features are real but require clean data to be useful. If your master data is messy, the AI layer adds noise. Sort data quality first.

**Who should use it:** Organisations standardised on Azure/M365 who want ERP without switching away from the Microsoft ecosystem.

**Honest weakness:** Manufacturing modules are less mature than Epicor or Infor for pure shop-floor scenarios.

### Infor CloudSuite Industrial

Built for job shops and discrete manufacturers. Its Advanced Planning and Scheduling (APS) and PLM features handle the complexity of engineer-to-order and mixed-mode environments. Flexible without getting heavy.

I have used this for manufacturers who need depth in production scheduling, particularly those running multiple concurrent production orders on shared equipment where NetSuite or Dynamics would show gaps.

**Who should use it:** Mid-market discrete and mixed-mode manufacturers with complex scheduling requirements.

**Honest weakness:** UI is functional but dated compared to newer cloud-native tools.

### Epicor Kinetic

The most reliable shop-floor ERP in the mid-market. It does what it says. MES, scheduling, shop floor execution, and most teams get the hang of it quickly, not over-engineered for the operational context it serves.

A European automotive client in 2023 ran Epicor across two plants and it held up under the reality of shift work, machine downtime, and production exceptions. It is not the flashiest system but it is one you can trust on the floor.

**Who should use it:** Discrete manufacturers who want proven shop-floor capability without SAP complexity or pricing.

**Honest weakness:** Finance module lags the operational side. Some clients run a separate finance system alongside it.

### IFS Cloud

When assets matter as much as inventory (aerospace, defence, energy, utilities), IFS makes sense. It combines ERP, enterprise asset management (EAM), and field service management (FSM) in one system. Few competitors do that.

The feature depth comes with implementation complexity. On a European aerospace engagement in 2024 the IFS rollout ran 16 months end-to-end. This is not a system you deploy in six months without significant resource commitment.

**Who should use it:** Asset-intensive manufacturers in regulated industries where equipment lifecycle, maintenance, and compliance are central to operations.

### Acumatica

The best value option for smaller manufacturers who want to grow without re-implementing. Browser-first, modular, subscription pricing. You can start with core manufacturing and add modules as the business grows.

It does not have the depth of Epicor or Infor for complex shop floor scenarios, but for manufacturers running straightforward production without heavy MES requirements, it delivers.

**Who should use it:** Small to mid-market manufacturers (under 200 users) who want modern cloud ERP without enterprise-level cost or complexity.

### Sage X3

Focused on mid-market process manufacturing: pharmaceuticals, food and beverage, chemicals. Batch management, lot traceability, and regulatory compliance are built in. Better suited to process industries than discrete. A European pharmaceutical client in 2024 used Sage X3 for GxP batch traceability when SAP would have been a heavier commitment than the operation justified.

**Who should use it:** Mid-market process manufacturers who need traceability and compliance without SAP's overhead.

### DELMIAWorks and Cetec ERP

DELMIAWorks (formerly IQMS) is purpose-built for small discrete manufacturers with strong shop floor control. Cetec is the lowest total cost of ownership option: cloud, quick to deploy, and easy to scale for small shops that do not want to overinvest.

If budget is the primary constraint and the operation is straightforward, Cetec is worth a serious look.

## Common implementation challenges

| Challenge | What goes wrong | How to avoid it |
|---|---|---|
| **Unclear goals** | Teams start without defined KPIs or success criteria | Write a success plan before selecting the vendor |
| **Over-customisation** | Every legacy process forced into the new system | Map workflows first; modify processes before customising software |
| **Change management underestimated** | Users resist adoption; training rushed | Assign team-level change champions; communicate early |
| **Dirty data** | Bad master data causes go-live errors | Assign data owners before the project starts |
| **Scope creep** | New features added midstream | Define minimum viable product; phase extras after go-live |
| **Inadequate testing** | Go-live without full process testing | End-to-end testing with real data, especially finance workflows |
| **No post-go-live plan** | Support fades; users revert to old tools | Build a stabilisation and hypercare plan before going live |

Training is always underestimated. If users do not know how to use the system, they will not. They will find workarounds, which defeats the purpose. Data cleanup always takes longer than planned. Build in extra time on both.

## Cloud vs on-premise: which fits manufacturing better in 2026

Cloud ERP is now the default for most new implementations. Updates are automatic, you do not manage infrastructure, and scaling is easier. The trade-off is less control over update timing: you will get the latest version whether or not it is convenient.

For SAP specifically, the 2026 default for manufacturing is RISE (private cloud) or GROW (public cloud). Net-new on-premise S/4HANA deployments are now rare and usually driven by data-residency or sovereignty requirements. SAP Cloud ALM is the bundled application lifecycle management platform; Solution Manager extended support runs through 2027 for existing on-premise landscapes.

On-premise still makes sense in specific scenarios: high-security environments with data sovereignty requirements, plants with unreliable connectivity, or organisations with specific integration architectures that cloud models do not support.

Most manufacturing ERP vendors now offer both. The decision usually comes down to IT infrastructure preference and security policy, not ERP capability.

## Recommendations by manufacturer type

| Manufacturer type | Best ERP options | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **SMB (under 100 users)** | Acumatica, Cetec ERP | Affordable, cloud-native, scalable without re-implementing |
| **Mid-market 50 to 1,000 staff** | GROW with SAP, Epicor Kinetic, Acumatica | GROW brings SAP within reach at $2M to $6M; Epicor for shop-floor focus |
| **Discrete manufacturing** | Epicor Kinetic, DELMIAWorks | Strong MES, scheduling, and real-time shop floor control |
| **Process manufacturing** | Sage X3, Infor CloudSuite Process | Batch traceability, regulatory compliance, process-specific workflows |
| **Mixed-mode** | Infor CloudSuite Industrial, Dynamics 365 | Handles both discrete and process without heavy customisation |
| **Global enterprise** | RISE with SAP S/4HANA, IFS Cloud | Multi-plant, multi-currency, deep compliance and integration capability |
| **Asset-intensive industries** | IFS Cloud, RISE with SAP for very large estates | ERP + EAM + FSM in one system |

A 50-person plant may run more complex processes than a 500-person facility. Do not choose based on headcount alone. Look at your production type, your workflow complexity, and the team you have to support it. That is usually more telling than any feature comparison.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>How do I choose the best ERP for manufacturing?</summary>

Start with your production type: discrete (make-to-order or make-to-stock), process (batches, formulas), or mixed-mode, and your biggest operational gaps. Then filter systems against those gaps, not against vendor feature lists.

Get your shop floor team into demos early. What looks smooth in a sales presentation often looks different when your production supervisor is trying to log a work order at the start of a shift. Their reaction matters more than the analyst score.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the most widely used ERP in manufacturing?</summary>

SAP is the most widely deployed globally, particularly among large enterprises. In the mid-market, Epicor, Infor, NetSuite, Dynamics 365, and now GROW with SAP are all common. "Most widely used" is not the same as "best fit". SAP's dominance at the enterprise level reflects its depth, not that it is right for every operation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between MES and MRP?</summary>

MES (Manufacturing Execution System) manages real-time shop floor activity: machine data, labour tracking, quality events, WIP status. It answers "what is happening on the floor right now?"

MRP (Material Requirements Planning) manages what materials you need to procure and when, based on demand and lead times. It answers "what do I need to order to meet the production schedule?"

Most manufacturing ERPs include MRP as a core module. MES is either a separate system that integrates with ERP, or a capability built into the ERP's shop floor module. The more complex the production environment, the more likely you will need a dedicated MES alongside the ERP.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is there a free ERP for manufacturing?</summary>

ERPNext is open-source and free to self-host. It covers manufacturing basics including BOMs, production orders, and inventory. The trade-off is that self-hosting requires in-house technical skills for setup, maintenance, and upgrades. It works well for small manufacturers with technical resources and straightforward processes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is SAP used for in manufacturing?</summary>

SAP S/4HANA covers production planning (PP module), plant maintenance, quality management, materials management, and financial integration: all in one system. At scale, it handles multi-plant scheduling, lot traceability, integration with MES and SCADA systems, and global compliance requirements. It is complex and expensive, but for large manufacturers the depth justifies it. Under RISE with SAP and GROW with SAP the deployment now includes Joule for AI-assisted configuration and SAP Cloud ALM for application lifecycle management.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the biggest mistakes in manufacturing ERP selection?</summary>

Three I see repeatedly. Buying features you will never use (over-specified for current operations). Underestimating the implementation effort (especially data migration and user training). Selecting based on vendor demos without involving the people who will actually use the system every day.

The second mistake is the most costly. A system that is technically right but operationally unacceptable to shop floor staff gets worked around, and a system being worked around is worse than no system at all.

</details>
