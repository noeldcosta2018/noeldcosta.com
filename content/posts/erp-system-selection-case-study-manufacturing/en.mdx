# ERP system selection: case study for a mid-sized manufacturer

After two decades in ERP advisory, I thought I had seen it all, until a mid-sized manufacturing company in Poland nearly signed off on the wrong system and locked themselves into a $1.2 million mistake. Leadership was overwhelmed by vendor sales tactics, internal politics, and a requirements list that had grown into a wish list.

That's when I was brought in. I simplified the selection process, introduced structured evaluation criteria, asked leadership about their real pain points, and reminded them that ERP selection is a governance and risk management decision, not a feature contest.

The result: a confident ERP choice made in twelve weeks, $1.2 million in unnecessary spend avoided, and a board that went into implementation with clarity rather than hesitation.

## The client and the problem

The company had revenues just under $400 million. Finance, warehouse, and production each ran on separate platforms with very limited integration. Reporting was slow and unreliable. Departments had built manual workarounds just to keep operations running. The board had made their position clear: they could not wait another year.

The hidden pressures that emerged when I spent time with leadership:

- **CIO**: pushing for cloud capability and long-term scalability
- **CFO**: pushing hard on cost control, worried about another budget overrun
- **COO**: insisting operational stability came first, worried about production disruption
- **Vendors**: using quarter-end discount tactics and "limited time" offers to force decisions

Each priority made sense on its own. Together, they created a standstill. Nobody had hard numbers. Every decision was being driven by polished vendor slide decks with no structured cost model behind them.

Staff were exhausted. Some weeks included three or four demos with no evaluation framework. One manager admitted quietly that he no longer remembered which product offered which feature. Decision fatigue had set in, and the temptation to just choose something was real.

| Common problem | Impact | How to address it |
|---|---|---|
| Jumping straight into vendor demos | Teams distracted by features; core process fit gets ignored | Document process flows first; run demos with your real data |
| Unclear requirements from leadership | Conflicting priorities drag decisions and widen scope | Lock must-have and should-have lists; get executive approval |
| No five-year cost model | Hidden costs surface after go-live and strain budgets | Build a TCO view covering licenses, escalators, integrations, and support |
| Integration blind spots | Interfaces discovered late cause delays and cost overruns | Map all systems early; validate with a proof of concept |
| Internal politics and bias | Decisions lean on opinion rather than facts | Use weighted scorecards and role-specific criteria |

## My approach

### Phase 1: Requirements vendors never ask about

Most selection processes focus on modules and functions. I looked deeper. I asked where integration would matter most, because legacy systems never disappear overnight. I raised questions about vendor lock-in: penalties and future migration costs that no sales team puts on the first slide, and I checked organizational readiness.

During workshops, I found that inventory practices were inconsistent across different production sites. Some locations tracked raw materials in spreadsheets. Others used local databases. That gap alone could have turned into a significant cutover crisis. Flagging it early meant leadership understood that system selection had to go hand in hand with process alignment.

### Phase 2: Scripted demo day with real data

Vendors prefer to show what they do best. That hides weaknesses. I built a scripted demo day where each vendor had to walk through the same core workflows (order-to-cash, production planning) using the client's own data and following the same sequence. No cherry-picking scenarios.

Some vendors pushed back. They said it was unusual. I held firm. The client saw side-by-side comparisons that revealed differences polished brochures had completely hidden.

### Phase 3: Surfacing hidden costs

Price tags are misleading. I built a five-year TCO model that covered annual escalation clauses that raise fees quietly year after year, resource backfill costs from pulling staff off their day jobs, and hypercare support that often costs two to three times more than vendors initially quote.

One "mid-tier" option showed a hidden $500,000 cost increase after year three. Without that model, the client would have chosen it based on the headline price. This single exercise justified the entire engagement.

### Phase 4: Neutralizing internal bias

Even with data, bias creeps in. The CIO leaned toward the cloud-first vendor. The CFO leaned toward the lowest upfront price. The COO leaned toward stability. I ran structured voting sessions separately and then compared results, highlighting where personal bias had outweighed the facts.

By grounding decisions in structured scoring, it became harder to say "this one feels better" without evidence.

### Phase 5: Board alignment

I summarized everything into a one-page executive dashboard: a scorecard, cost ranges, and a risk heatmap. No jargon. No long reports.

The board discussion lasted less than an hour. Within 45 minutes, leadership aligned and made the decision. They left the room confident, not because they had more data, but because the data was structured and transparent.

| Step | Purpose | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Define business objectives | Clarify what the ERP should solve: cost control, compliance, scalability | Alignment between leadership and operations on key goals |
| Map current processes | Document workflows, pain points, and dependencies | Baseline for assessing fit against ERP capabilities |
| Shortlist vendors | Narrow to realistic options that meet requirements | Focused evaluation with relevant candidates only |
| Run fit-gap analysis | Compare vendor functionality against business needs | Clear view of customizations, risks, and integration gaps |
| Model total cost of ownership | Include licenses, implementation, training, and five-year support | Financial clarity for budgeting and board approval |
| Conduct reference checks | Talk to peers in similar industries using the same ERP | First-hand insights into vendor performance and challenges |

## The outcomes

- ERP chosen in twelve weeks
- $1.2 million in unnecessary spend avoided, by removing modules with no business value and rejecting inflated premium bundles, not by chasing discounts
- Decision fatigue lifted; leadership aligned and moved into kick-off with agreed expectations
- Vendor quarter-end deadlines neutralized; the client purchased on their terms

## Lessons for executives

The value of the approach came from stripping away noise. By focusing on what vendors avoid discussing, surfacing hidden costs, and forcing decisions into a structured process, the company avoided an expensive mistake and gained alignment they didn't have at the start.

| Lesson | Why it matters | Practical takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| Don't rush the decision | Quick choices often overlook hidden costs and integration risks | Build in extra time for evaluation and peer references |
| Get real numbers, not just slide decks | Vendor marketing hides long-term expenses and support gaps | Develop a TCO model with 5–7 year visibility |
| Balance leadership priorities | Conflicts between cost, scalability, and disruption can stall progress | Facilitate structured workshops to align on trade-offs early |
| Protect staff from demo fatigue | Too many demos without structure drain morale and skew feedback | Standardize evaluation criteria; rotate demo participants |
| Keep the board engaged | Late-stage surprises lead to resistance and delayed approvals | Provide concise updates; involve directors in checkpoint reviews |

<details>
<summary>How should a mid-sized manufacturer start ERP selection without getting lost in demos?</summary>

The best starting point is not vendor demos. Those distract leadership with features that may never be used. Start by mapping core business processes: order-to-cash, production scheduling, inventory management, supplier payments.

Once those are documented and agreed, the evaluation can focus on how each system supports them. I've seen teams spend weeks on polished demos and then realize that half of what they watched had no relevance to their actual operations. Starting with process mapping typically makes the evaluation shorter and less political.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What belongs in a five-year total cost of ownership model for ERP?</summary>

Most teams underestimate because vendors rarely highlight the full picture. A proper TCO should cover: software licensing and user growth, annual maintenance and escalation clauses, integration effort, data migration, testing, hypercare, and training backfill for internal staff pulled off day jobs.

In one case, a team approved a system based only on license fees and found the budget had doubled by year two. The TCO model forces a conversation about contract escalators and service renewals before you're committed to them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do we make vendor demos genuinely comparable?</summary>

Script identical scenarios using your own data, each vendor walks through the same workflows (order-to-cash, production planning, return processing) in the same sequence, with the same time limits. Scoring is weighted by your business priorities, not by vendor presentation quality.

One manufacturing team asked vendors to process a complex return order including credit issuance. That test revealed differences that polished demos had hidden completely. The extra structure creates friction with vendors but makes the final decision defensible at board level.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What risks should a board see before approving an ERP decision?</summary>

ERP touches finance, operations, and compliance, so risks need to be visible at board level, not just discussed in IT. Common risks include data migration failure, integration gaps with existing systems, uneven process adoption across sites, and vendor lock-in that becomes painful when upgrades are due.

A single-page risk heatmap classifying probability and impact often changes the conversation. Without it, executives sometimes assume ERP is only about licenses and timelines. Visible risks force accountability, and some can be mitigated while others just need to be accepted knowingly.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do we prevent scope creep during ERP selection?</summary>

Scope creep usually starts with small exceptions that nobody wants to challenge. The discipline that works: separate must-have requirements from optional items, and document the link between each feature and a specific measurable business outcome.

A formal change process also helps; any new requirement needs to demonstrate either risk reduction or financial return. I've seen teams approve every request until the timeline doubled. Every additional feature competes with core go-live readiness, and leadership needs to reinforce that consistently.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do we handle vendor pressure and quarter-end discounts?</summary>

Vendor discounts tied to short deadlines are pressure tactics, not genuine savings. The real savings come from eliminating unnecessary modules or challenging over-licensing. A 10% discount looks attractive until you realize half the functionality is never used.

In this engagement, delaying the decision did not change the available terms: vendors rarely walk away from a committed buyer. Pressure tactics should be called out directly. If the deal holds after scrutiny, the business makes a cleaner decision without artificial urgency. The $1.2M saving came from scope rationalization, not from any vendor discount.

</details>
