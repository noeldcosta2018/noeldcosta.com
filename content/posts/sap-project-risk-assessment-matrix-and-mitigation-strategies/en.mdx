Most SAP risk assessments sit in a folder. They get built before the Steering Committee, reviewed once, and never touched again.

That's not risk management. That's a document.

I've watched teams ignore risks that were visible in week two and spend the last three months of the project firefighting. What starts as a small integration issue in Materials Management blocks Finance. A workflow that looked fine in design frustrates users who just want to get through their day. A data migration assumption that nobody questioned delays go-live by six weeks.

The risks weren't surprises. They were documented. Nobody acted on them.

## The five risk categories that derail SAP projects

### Scope risks

Your project starts with standard modules. Then someone adds "just one more report." Then a custom dashboard. Then a few enhancements. Clean design breaks under every upgrade. This is where scope management fails first.

Scope risk isn't about changing requirements. It's about scope changes introduced informally during design: in workshops, in emails, in conversations the project manager doesn't hear about until configuration is already done.

### Resource risks

Your SAP architect gets pulled onto a production issue. A key developer leaves. Business users miss test cycles because their day jobs don't stop. Resource availability needs written commitments, not verbal ones. Verbal promises evaporate when headcount pressure hits.

### Technical risks

Data migration fails because field mappings were never validated against the target structure. Interfaces pass unit testing and break under real-world transaction volume. Custom code looks fine until it hits production load. These aren't surprises — they're predictable if you know where to look.

### Timeline risks

A delay in blueprinting means testing gets squeezed. The go-live date stays fixed. UAT, training, and data preparation get rushed. Recovery becomes expensive. The same pattern happens on almost every programme that misses a phase gate and doesn't adjust the downstream schedule.

### User adoption risks

People reject what they don't understand. If training is weak or starts too late, users resist the change. Workarounds develop. Data quality degrades. The system gets blamed for a change management problem.

## Real-world failures where risk assessment didn't happen

These are public cases. They're worth knowing because the patterns repeat.

**Lidl, €500M, seven years.** Lidl's inventory system used purchase prices. SAP was designed around retail prices. Nobody flagged this as a structural data risk early. The result was a fundamental mismatch that no amount of customisation could fix. The implementation was scrapped entirely.

Lesson: if your data structures don't align with SAP's model, that's a risk to identify in week one, not month thirty-six.

**HP, $160M revenue loss.** HP migrated tens of thousands of open orders mid-transition with no adequate backup plan. Orders were lost. Inventory mismatches compounded. $160M in revenue walked out the door.

Lesson: if your business runs on accurate transactions, you need a tested rollback plan before cutover begins.

**Nike, $100M in lost sales.** Nike implemented new forecasting software without properly testing the integration with SAP. The system overproduced some lines and underproduced others. Stock price dropped. Nike disclosed the failure publicly.

Lesson: never assume a system integration works without testing it under production conditions.

**UK NHS, £10 billion.** The NHS didn't assess how hospitals actually used their existing systems before the project started. Customisation spiralled. Vendor disputes followed. The system never launched.

Lesson: end-user behaviour is a risk input, not an assumption.

## What a useful risk assessment requires as inputs

Risk assessments built on assumptions are worse than no risk assessment. They create false confidence while actual risks accumulate.

The six inputs that matter:

**Scope documentation.** Project charter, approved scope, signed-off business requirements, stakeholder agreements. If these don't exist, the scope risk is already high.

**Resource availability.** Written commitments from department heads, not verbal ones. Skills matrix for critical roles. A named backup for every key position.

**Budget and timeline.** Approved budget with contingency. Timeline feasibility validated against comparable projects. If leadership is expecting a full rollout in six months with minimal funding, that's a risk to call out now.

**Vendor and third-party commitments.** Contracts with SLAs. Documented penalties for missed deadlines. Past performance data if available.

**Technical landscape.** Legacy system compatibility assessment. Data migration complexity estimate. Cloud vs on-premise risk analysis.

**Past project logs.** Risk registers from previous SAP or ERP implementations. Issue logs. Post-mortem reports. Most risks aren't new — they repeat if nobody reads the history.

## The risk matrix: scoring and prioritisation

A risk matrix scores each risk on two dimensions: probability (how likely) and impact (how severe). The product determines priority.

| Risk | Probability (1–5) | Impact (1–5) | Score | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Data migration failure | 4 | 5 | 20 | High |
| Integration delays | 4 | 4 | 16 | High |
| Resource constraints | 4 | 4 | 16 | High |
| Budget overrun | 3 | 5 | 15 | High |
| Scope creep | 4 | 3 | 12 | Medium |
| Test coverage gaps | 3 | 4 | 12 | Medium |
| Stakeholder misalignment | 3 | 4 | 12 | Medium |
| Performance degradation under load | 3 | 4 | 12 | Medium |
| User resistance / low adoption | 3 | 3 | 9 | Medium |
| Compliance gaps | 2 | 5 | 10 | Medium |
| Licence cost exposure | 2 | 4 | 8 | Medium |
| KPIs not tracked | 3 | 2 | 6 | Low |

High scores (16+) need immediate attention and named owners. Medium scores (8–15) need monitoring with defined escalation triggers. Low scores (under 8) go on the register but don't consume priority bandwidth.

Scores are starting points, not verdicts. A compliance gap scoring 10 can become a 25 in a regulated industry. Context matters.

## Building a risk assessment that gets used

### Step 1: Identify risks across all categories

Run structured workshops with IT, business, and vendors. Each group sees different risks. IT sees technical failure modes. Business sees process and adoption risks. Vendors see delivery and dependency risks.

The risks most teams miss: mismatched expectations between IT and business, poorly defined integrations with external systems, UAT owners who aren't available or ready, scope changes introduced informally during design.

### Step 2: Score each risk

Assign probability and impact. Use actual numbers when possible. "High risk" is vague. "A one-week delay in UAT costs approximately $150K in resource time and may push go-live by three weeks" gets attention.

### Step 3: Assign owners

Every risk on the register must have a named owner. Not a team. A person. If no one owns a risk, no one tracks it. If no one tracks it, it doesn't get resolved.

### Step 4: Define response plans

For each high-priority risk, define the response before it materialises. There are four options: avoid (change scope or approach to eliminate the risk), mitigate (reduce probability or impact), transfer (vendor SLA, insurance), or accept (conscious decision to live with it).

Avoid "monitor and respond." That's not a response plan, it's deferred decision-making.

### Step 5: Review weekly

Not at Steering Committees. Not monthly. Weekly. SAP projects move fast. A month between risk reviews is long enough for a medium risk to become a programme-stopping issue.

At each review: check status of open risks, identify new risks, update scores if conditions changed, escalate anything approaching a trigger threshold.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is a risk assessment in SAP projects and why does it matter?</summary>

A risk assessment is the process of identifying potential problems before they happen, scoring them by probability and impact, assigning ownership, and building response plans.

In SAP projects, the stakes are high enough that informal risk management doesn't hold. You have multiple modules, integration dependencies, data migration complexity, a business change programme, and a fixed go-live date all running simultaneously. Any of these can fail. The question is whether you've identified which ones are most likely to fail and planned accordingly.

PMI research shows high-performing teams are 21% more likely to use formal risk management techniques. The correlation makes sense. Formal risk management creates visibility, accountability, and response capacity. Informal risk management creates false confidence and reactive firefighting.

The risk assessment doesn't need to be long. It needs to be used. A short list of real risks with owners and response plans beats a comprehensive document nobody reads.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you build a risk assessment matrix for an SAP project?</summary>

A risk matrix maps risks on two axes: probability (likelihood of occurrence) and impact (severity of consequences if it occurs). Multiplying the two gives a risk score.

The practical process:

Start by identifying risks in five categories: scope, resource, technical, timeline, and user adoption. Run workshops with IT, business, and vendors — each group sees different risks.

Score each risk on a 1–5 scale for probability and impact. Multiply the scores. A risk scoring 16 or above is high priority. 8–15 is medium. Below 8 is low.

Assign an owner to every risk that's medium or higher. Define what action that owner will take if the risk escalates. Set a threshold trigger ("if UAT completion is below 80% by end of week 16, go-live date goes to review").

Update the matrix weekly or at phase gates, whichever comes first. Risks that were low-probability in Explore can become high-probability in Realize. A static risk matrix becomes irrelevant within weeks.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the most common risks in SAP implementation projects?</summary>

Based on what consistently appears across programmes, the highest-frequency risks are:

Data migration failure: legacy data is almost always messier than initial estimates. Field mappings that look clean in extract fail in load. Historical data volumes take longer to process than planned. Data migration is the single most consistent source of go-live delay.

Integration delays: custom interfaces between SAP and external systems take longer to build and test than estimated. This is especially true when legacy systems have undocumented API behaviour or when third-party vendors are involved.

Scope creep: informal scope additions during design, requirements that weren't captured in the original scope, and "small" enhancements that turn into structural changes. Scope creep compresses testing and training at the end when there's no buffer left.

Resource constraints: key people pulled onto other work, business users unavailable during UAT because month-end close happens to coincide, consultants rolling off before knowledge transfer is complete.

User adoption failure: training that demonstrates the system rather than simulating the actual job. Rollout materials designed for the original implementation, not adapted for the new location or user group. Resistance that was predictable but not planned for.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When should a risk assessment be done during an SAP project?</summary>

Four formal trigger points, plus continuous informal review.

Before the project starts. Before scope is locked, before contracts are signed, before the team is assembled. This is when structural risks need to be called out. Data architecture mismatches, unrealistic timelines, budget assumptions that don't hold. Discovering these in month six is much more expensive than discovering them in week two.

Before each SAP Activate phase gate. Blueprint to Realise. Realise to Deploy. Each transition resets the risk profile. What was a manageable risk in Explore may need immediate action in Realise. Phase gate risk reviews are not optional.

When scope, budget, or resources change. Every material change creates new risks and may resolve others. A risk assessment snapshot taken before the change is the baseline. After the change, rescore.

When a risk becomes a problem. When a high-priority risk materialises: reassess what else it affects (most serious risks have cascading effects), update the response plan, and escalate to the Steering Committee with a clear proposal. Not just "it happened," but "here's what we're doing about it."

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the real-world consequences of skipping risk assessment on ERP projects?</summary>

Three well-documented public examples show what happens at scale.

Lidl: €500M and seven years invested in an SAP implementation that was scrapped. The data model conflict between Lidl's purchase-price inventory system and SAP's retail-price model was never identified as a fundamental risk. The mismatch couldn't be customised away.

HP: $160M in revenue lost when tens of thousands of open orders were migrated mid-transition without adequate backup or validation. Orders disappeared. Inventory positions became unreliable. The financial impact was direct and material.

Nike: $100M in lost sales from a supply chain planning system implemented without proper integration testing against SAP. The system produced systematically wrong forecasts. Inventory was misallocated. Nike disclosed the failure publicly.

At smaller scale, the pattern is identical: data architecture mismatches discovered late, integration failures under production volume, user adoption failures from inadequate training. The difference from enterprise disasters is magnitude, not mechanism. The risks were identifiable before they became problems.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How should risk ownership be structured in an SAP programme?</summary>

Every risk needs a named individual owner. Not a team, not a workstream. One person who is accountable for the risk status, the mitigation action, and escalating if the risk moves.

Risk ownership should align with the area that can actually do something about the risk. Data migration risk should be owned by the data lead, not the programme manager. Integration risk should be owned by the technical architect. User adoption risk should be owned by the OCM lead.

The programme manager tracks risk register health. They don't own every risk.

Escalation paths matter. If an owner identifies that a risk is materialising, who do they tell? What authority does that person have to authorise a response? These questions need answers before the risk register is finalised, not when a risk is already escalating.

Risk reviews in Steering Committee should show risk status, owner, last action, and next action. Not just a RAG status. A RAG status with no action commentary is a compliance exercise, not risk management.

</details>
