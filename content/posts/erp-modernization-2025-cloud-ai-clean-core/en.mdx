In 2024, most leaders were still buying time. Analyzing ECC deprecation timelines. Debating hybrid versus full-stack migrations. Running sandbox tests without committing to direction.

By mid-2025, that window has largely closed. The technical debt from SAP ECC and Oracle EBS is no longer theoretical. It shows up in failed regression tests after quarterly updates. It shows up in audit findings on custom code that nobody owns. And in data that takes 12 hours to reflect across planning and execution systems.

This is execution time.

## Four forces driving action in 2025

**Deadlines.** SAP ECC mainstream support ends 2027 with extended options to 2033, but only 35% of SAP ECC customers had started their S/4HANA migrations as of mid-2025. Of those, many are still in sandbox. Oracle EBS customers face a similar story. 40% are exploring Fusion Cloud, fewer than 1 in 5 are live. These aren't aggressive timelines. They're already tight.

**Budget model shift.** Cloud ERP moves spend from CAPEX-heavy licensing to OPEX subscription models. That sounds cleaner. In practice, CFOs are discovering that cloud billing is less predictable across modules. Usage-based pricing, licensing sprawl, and integration overhead add costs that weren't in the original business case.

**Finance and supply chain fragmentation.** Finance teams run AI on Accounts Payable and Receivable but audit processes haven't caught up; most internal audit teams still use templates built for a different era. Supply chains face partial modernization: SAP IBP for planning, legacy LE-WM for execution, Ariba for procurement but manual GRN. The bottleneck isn't technology; it's fragmentation between systems that don't talk to each other cleanly.

**ERP is one layer, not the whole stack.** ServiceNow handles process orchestration. Salesforce drives customer workflows. Workday owns HR lifecycle. 70% of S/4HANA customers also run ServiceNow for workflows. 55% integrate Salesforce directly with their ERP layer. ERP has become the financial backbone, not the end-to-end process hub it was designed to be in the 1990s.

## The architecture shift: backbone plus satellites

The mental model most enterprise architects are now working with is a financial backbone (SAP S/4HANA or Oracle Fusion) surrounded by specialists:

| Layer | What sits here | What doesn't |
|---|---|---|
| Financial backbone | General ledger, cost controlling, procurement, inventory, manufacturing execution, statutory close | Workflow approvals, CRM, workforce management, analytics |
| Workflow | ServiceNow for IT and ops processes, approvals, change management | Transactional record-keeping |
| Customer engagement | Salesforce or SAP CRM | Financial processing |
| Workforce | Workday or SuccessFactors | Operational transactions |
| Analytics | SAP Analytics Cloud, Power BI, Snowflake | Core ERP data |

This separation is intentional. Forcing everything back into SAP adds friction, slows updates, and creates ownership confusion. Trying to cram CRM, workflow, analytics, and EHS into one system is what made previous modernization programmes so painful.

The question worth asking: "What should ERP no longer be responsible for?" Skip that question and you rebuild the same monolith with a newer licence.

## Clean core: required, not optional

Clean core isn't a choice anymore once you're on cloud ERP with quarterly updates. Heavily customized systems can't absorb quarterly releases without breaking. Regression testing becomes a recurring crisis. The upgrade path closes.

What clean core requires:
1. Remove custom code from the core. Every Z-program maintained is a quarterly liability.
2. Extensions via SAP BTP or low-code tools, not core modification
3. Standard processes wherever SAP ships them; customization only where regulation or genuine competitive differentiation requires it

The resistance to this is cultural, not technical. Business leads who've relied on custom code for 15 years still expect it to "just be re-implemented." That was 2012. Quarterly cloud releases make that model untenable.

The typical ECC landscape carries 1,500–3,000 custom objects based on assessments I've run across manufacturing and services. Only about 25% show active business usage. The rest is historical weight that inflates migration cost and creates audit exposure: audit flags on custom code grow roughly 15% per year because change traces lack owners.

The practical move: run a usage scan now. Deprecate what's inactive. Start a formal cut list before design begins. Teams that plan clean core from week one have dramatically better upgrade experiences than those that treat it as a constraint to work around.

## The governance gap nobody talks about

With ERP as one layer among many, ownership questions become critical. Who owns:

1. The API between Salesforce and the ERP?
2. A workflow that spans SAP and ServiceNow?
3. Conflicting update priorities when both systems need changes simultaneously?

I've seen this delay go-lives by months. Teams don't realize they're stuck until integration testing reveals the overlap.

On one project, five systems were touching the same supplier master record. No master ownership document existed. Nobody had designed the authority matrix for who could change what, in which system. That's not a technology failure. It's a governance failure that technology exposed.

35% of modernization programmes in recent reviews cited unclear data ownership, not technical blockers, as their biggest cause of delay.

## AI readiness: clean data first

AI value in ERP is real, but it depends on data quality and clean architecture. Joule, SAP's generative AI assistant, is growing rapidly. Agentic use cases on BTP are shipping, not slidware, but they rely on clean, consistent, structured data.

If your data is duplicated, inconsistently coded, or sitting in custom tables that S/4HANA doesn't recognize, AI has nothing reliable to work with. Finance teams running AI on AP automation discover this quickly when invoices match to the wrong vendor because vendor master was never cleaned.

The sequence that works: clean core first, data governance second, analytics third, AI fourth. Skipping steps doesn't accelerate the timeline; it just delays the problem.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is clean core in SAP S/4HANA?</summary>

Clean core means keeping SAP S/4HANA configuration and code as close to the standard as possible: no custom Z-programs embedded in core processes. Extensions go through SAP BTP or side-by-side tools; they don't modify the core.

The business reason is upgrade protection. On cloud S/4HANA with quarterly updates, any custom code in the core creates regression risk every release. Teams that maintain clean core absorb quarterly updates in days. Teams with heavy customization treat each update as a mini-project.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the backbone plus satellites architecture for ERP?</summary>

It's the design approach where ERP (SAP S/4HANA or Oracle Fusion) handles what it's built for: financial record-keeping, inventory, procurement, manufacturing transactions, and statutory reporting, while specialist platforms handle adjacent functions: ServiceNow for workflow and approvals, Salesforce for customer engagement, Workday for HR, analytics tools for insights.

The alternative, trying to force all of these into core ERP, creates a monolith that updates slowly, customizes heavily, and frustrates everyone. 70% of S/4HANA customers now also run ServiceNow. The separation is already the standard pattern.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How should companies approach the 2027 SAP ECC deadline?</summary>

Start with the Readiness Check. It surfaces custom code volumes, add-on dependencies, and data sizing: the three factors that determine whether your migration approach is brownfield, greenfield, or selective. Without it, timeline estimates are guesswork.

Realistically, complex enterprise migrations run 18–24 months from assessment to go-live. If you haven't locked an approach and started partner conversations by late 2025, a clean 2027 go-live is already constrained. Extended support to 2033 is available but costs more and limits innovation access.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When is AI valuable in ERP modernization and when isn't it?</summary>

AI is valuable when the underlying data is clean, consistent, and structured, and when the process it's automating has well-defined rules. AP automation, demand forecasting, and anomaly detection in financial transactions are practical use cases with proven results.

AI is not a shortcut past poor data quality or governance gaps. If vendor master records are duplicated, if custom tables contain data that S/4HANA doesn't recognize, or if process ownership is unclear, AI will make decisions based on bad inputs, and those decisions are harder to catch than manual errors.

The sequence: clean core, data governance, stable processes, then AI, not the other way around.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the biggest ERP modernization mistakes companies make in 2025?</summary>

Treating it as a technology project rather than a business transformation. The clean core debate is lost before it starts when business leads aren't involved in the decision. Custom code decisions made by IT teams without business buy-in get reversed in workshops later.

Starting data governance after design begins. Data ownership and master data decisions made in week one of design save months in testing. The same decisions made in UAT cost months of rework.

Not defining what ERP should stop doing. Without an explicit decision about which functions stay in core ERP and which move to specialist platforms, the scope of core ERP expands by default: which is exactly how the previous monolith was built.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does ERP modernization cost in 2025?</summary>

Costs vary significantly by approach and scale. A brownfield ECC-to-S/4HANA conversion for a mid-market company typically runs $2M–$8M in implementation costs, plus ongoing cloud subscription and OPEX. Enterprise programmes with global scope, significant customization, and multiple entities can run $20M–$100M+.

The shift to cloud OPEX changes the financial model: lower upfront cost, predictable quarterly fees, but integration and governance overhead that wasn't in the old on-premise TCO. CFOs who modelled the switch based on licence comparison alone typically find the real TCO is 20–30% higher once integration, testing, and operational support are included.

</details>
