In 2024, most leaders were still buying time. Analysing ECC deprecation timelines. Debating hybrid versus full-stack migrations. Running sandbox tests without committing to direction.

By 2025, that window had largely closed. By mid-2026, the SAP ECC mainstream support end date in 2027 is one year away and the technical debt from SAP ECC and Oracle EBS is no longer theoretical. It shows up in failed regression tests after quarterly updates. It shows up in audit findings on custom code that nobody owns. And in data that takes 12 hours to reflect across planning and execution systems.

This is execution time.

## Four forces driving action in 2026

**Deadlines.** SAP ECC mainstream support ends 2027 with the standard extended support window to 2030 (and additional fee-based options through 2033 for some customers), but only about 40 percent of SAP ECC customers had started their S/4HANA migrations through 2025-2026. Of those, many were still in sandbox. Oracle EBS customers face a similar story. 40 percent were exploring Fusion Cloud, fewer than 1 in 5 are live. These are not aggressive timelines. They are already tight.

**Budget model shift.** Cloud ERP moves spend from CAPEX-heavy licensing to OPEX subscription models. That sounds cleaner. In practice, CFOs are discovering that cloud billing is less predictable across modules. Under RISE with SAP and GROW with SAP, FUE (Full User Equivalent) growth, capability tier uplifts, and integration overhead add costs that were not in the original business case.

**Finance and supply chain fragmentation.** Finance teams run AI on Accounts Payable and Receivable but audit processes have not caught up; most internal audit teams still use templates built for a different era. Supply chains face partial modernization: SAP IBP for planning, legacy LE-WM for execution, Ariba for procurement but manual GRN. The bottleneck is not technology; it is fragmentation between systems that do not talk to each other cleanly.

**ERP is one layer, not the whole stack.** ServiceNow handles process orchestration. Salesforce drives customer workflows. Workday owns HR lifecycle. 70 percent of S/4HANA customers also run ServiceNow for workflows. 55 percent integrate Salesforce directly with their ERP layer. ERP has become the financial backbone, not the end-to-end process hub it was designed to be in the 1990s.

## The architecture shift: backbone plus satellites

The mental model most enterprise architects are now working with is a financial backbone (SAP S/4HANA or Oracle Fusion) surrounded by specialists:

| Layer | What sits here | What does not |
|---|---|---|
| Financial backbone | General ledger, cost controlling, procurement, inventory, manufacturing execution, statutory close | Workflow approvals, CRM, workforce management, analytics |
| Workflow | ServiceNow for IT and ops processes, approvals, change management | Transactional record-keeping |
| Customer engagement | Salesforce or SAP CRM | Financial processing |
| Workforce | Workday or SuccessFactors | Operational transactions |
| Analytics | SAP Analytics Cloud, Power BI, Snowflake | Core ERP data |

This separation is intentional. Forcing everything back into SAP adds friction, slows updates, and creates ownership confusion. Trying to cram CRM, workflow, analytics, and EHS into one system is what made previous modernization programmes so painful.

The question worth asking: "What should ERP no longer be responsible for?" Skip that question and you rebuild the same monolith with a newer licence.

## Clean-core: contractual, not optional

Clean-core is not a choice anymore once you are on cloud ERP with quarterly updates. Under RISE with SAP and GROW with SAP, clean-core compliance is contractual rather than a design preference. Heavily customised systems cannot absorb quarterly releases without breaking. Regression testing becomes a recurring crisis. The upgrade path closes.

What clean-core requires:
1. Remove custom code from the core. Every Z-program maintained is a quarterly liability and a Joule integration blocker
2. Extensions via SAP BTP (Business Technology Platform), SAP Build apps, or side-by-side services, not core modification
3. Standard processes wherever SAP ships them; customisation only where regulation or genuine competitive differentiation requires it

The resistance to this is cultural, not technical. Business leads who relied on custom code for 15 years still expect it to "just be re-implemented." That was 2012. Quarterly cloud releases under RISE and GROW make that model untenable.

The typical ECC landscape carries 1,500 to 3,000 custom objects based on assessments I have run across manufacturing and services. Only about 25 percent show active business usage. The rest is historical weight that inflates migration cost and creates audit exposure. On a European financial services engagement in 2024 the inherited Z-program inventory triggered audit findings that took six weeks of remediation before the migration could proceed.

The practical move: run a usage scan now. Deprecate what is inactive. Start a formal cut list before design begins. Teams that plan clean-core from week one have dramatically better upgrade experiences than those that treat it as a constraint to work around. Joule for SAP Activate now drafts the fit-gap classification (standard vs BTP vs cut) 30 to 50 percent faster on programmes that adopt it end-to-end.

## The governance gap nobody talks about

With ERP as one layer among many, ownership questions become critical. Who owns:

1. The API between Salesforce and the ERP?
2. A workflow that spans SAP and ServiceNow?
3. Conflicting update priorities when both systems need changes simultaneously?

I have seen this delay go-lives by months. Teams do not realise they are stuck until integration testing reveals the overlap.

On a European manufacturing programme in 2024 five systems were touching the same supplier master record. No master ownership document existed. Nobody had designed the authority matrix for who could change what, in which system. That is not a technology failure. It is a governance failure that technology exposed.

35 percent of modernization programmes in recent reviews cited unclear data ownership, not technical blockers, as their biggest cause of delay.

## AI readiness: clean data first

AI value in ERP is real, but it depends on data quality and clean architecture. Joule, SAP's generative AI assistant, now spans S/4HANA, SuccessFactors, Ariba, and SAP Build, with the Activate-aligned Joule for SAP Activate compressing fit-gap and configuration cycles 30 to 50 percent on programmes that adopt it end-to-end. Agentic use cases on BTP are shipping, not slideware, but they rely on clean, consistent, structured data.

If your data is duplicated, inconsistently coded, or sitting in custom tables that S/4HANA does not recognise, AI has nothing reliable to work with. Finance teams running AI on AP automation discover this quickly when invoices match to the wrong vendor because vendor master was never cleaned.

The sequence that works: clean-core first, data governance second, analytics third, AI fourth. Skipping steps does not accelerate the timeline. It just delays the problem.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is clean core in SAP S/4HANA?</summary>

Clean-core means keeping SAP S/4HANA configuration and code as close to the standard as possible: no custom Z-programs embedded in core processes. Extensions go through SAP BTP, SAP Build, or side-by-side tools; they do not modify the core. Under RISE with SAP and GROW with SAP, clean-core compliance is contractual.

The business reason is upgrade protection. On cloud S/4HANA with quarterly updates, any custom code in the core creates regression risk every release. Teams that maintain clean-core absorb quarterly updates in days. Teams with heavy customisation treat each update as a mini-project.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the backbone plus satellites architecture for ERP?</summary>

It is the design approach where ERP (SAP S/4HANA or Oracle Fusion) handles what it is built for: financial record-keeping, inventory, procurement, manufacturing transactions, and statutory reporting, while specialist platforms handle adjacent functions. ServiceNow for workflow and approvals, Salesforce for customer engagement, Workday for HR, analytics tools for insights.

The alternative, trying to force all of these into core ERP, creates a monolith that updates slowly, customises heavily, and frustrates everyone. 70 percent of S/4HANA customers now also run ServiceNow. The separation is already the standard pattern.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How should companies approach the 2027 SAP ECC deadline?</summary>

Start with the Readiness Check. It surfaces custom code volumes, add-on dependencies, and data sizing: the three factors that determine whether your migration approach is brownfield, greenfield, or selective. Without it, timeline estimates are guesswork.

Realistically, complex enterprise migrations run 18 to 24 months from assessment to go-live. If you have not locked an approach and started partner conversations by mid-2026, a clean 2027 go-live is already constrained. The standard extended support window runs to 2030 with additional fee-based options through 2033, but both cost more and limit innovation access. RISE with SAP is the practical default for new commitments at this stage.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When is AI valuable in ERP modernization and when is it not?</summary>

AI is valuable when the underlying data is clean, consistent, and structured, and when the process it is automating has well-defined rules. AP automation, demand forecasting, and anomaly detection in financial transactions are practical use cases with proven results. Joule for SAP Activate now drafts fit-gap analyses 30 to 50 percent faster on programmes that adopt it end-to-end.

AI is not a shortcut past poor data quality or governance gaps. If vendor master records are duplicated, if custom tables contain data that S/4HANA does not recognise, or if process ownership is unclear, AI will make decisions based on bad inputs, and those decisions are harder to catch than manual errors.

The sequence: clean core, data governance, stable processes, then AI, not the other way around.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the biggest ERP modernization mistakes companies make in 2025?</summary>

Treating it as a technology project rather than a business transformation. The clean-core debate is lost before it starts when business leads are not involved in the decision. Custom code decisions made by IT teams without business buy-in get reversed in workshops later.

Starting data governance after design begins. Data ownership and master data decisions made in week one of design save months in testing. The same decisions made in UAT cost months of rework.

Not defining what ERP should stop doing. Without an explicit decision about which functions stay in core ERP and which move to specialist platforms, the scope of core ERP expands by default: which is exactly how the previous monolith was built.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does ERP modernization cost in 2025?</summary>

Costs vary significantly by approach and scale. A brownfield ECC-to-S/4HANA conversion for a mid-market company typically runs $2M–$8M in implementation costs, plus ongoing cloud subscription and OPEX. Enterprise programmes with global scope, significant customization, and multiple entities can run $20M–$100M+.

The shift to cloud OPEX changes the financial model: lower upfront cost, predictable quarterly fees, but integration and governance overhead that was not in the old on-premise TCO. CFOs who modelled the switch based on licence comparison alone typically find the real TCO is 20 to 30 percent higher once integration, testing, and operational support are included. Under RISE and GROW, FUE growth across the contract term is the line that most often surprises.

</details>
