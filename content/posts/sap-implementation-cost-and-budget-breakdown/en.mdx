An SAP implementation costs $80,000 to $500,000 for a small business under 200 staff, $500,000 to $2 million for a mid-market company of 200 to 1,000 staff, and $2 million to $10 million or more for a large enterprise. Global groups with more than 10,000 staff should plan for $10 million to $50 million or more.

Short answer: SAP implementation cost depends mostly on company size, scope and partner fees, not on the software. Partner services take 35 to 50 percent of the total, and you should hold another 10 to 20 percent on top as contingency.

The initial quote usually covers licences and the partner's build. It rarely covers data cleanup, extra test cycles, proper training or the months after go-live. That gap is what catches CFOs off guard.

## SAP implementation cost by company size

These are total programme ranges I use across the 21 SAP programmes I have led or rescued. They cover licences or subscription, partner services, data, testing, training and hypercare.

| Company size | Total cost range (USD) | Typical SAP product | What pushes it up |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small (under 200 staff) | $80K to $500K | SAP Business One, S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition | Industry add-ons, legacy data volume |
| Mid-market (200 to 1,000) | $500K to $2M | S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition under GROW, or Private Edition | Integrations, data quality, country localisation |
| Large (1,000 to 10,000) | $2M to $10M+ | S/4HANA Private Edition under RISE, or on-premise | Multi-system integration, custom code, test scope |
| Global group (10,000+) | $10M to $50M+ | S/4HANA Private Edition or on-premise, SAP BTP | Multi-country rollouts, regulation, change at scale |

Public partner benchmarks sit in the same bands. LeverX, an SAP partner, gives UK planning ranges of £500K to £2M for mid-sized businesses, £2M to £10M for large enterprises and £10M+ for global programmes ([LeverX UK cost guide, 2026](https://leverx.com/en-gb/blog/sap-s4hana-implementation-cost-guide-uk)). Its US guide puts a large-enterprise greenfield implementation at $4M to $10M+ and a large global transformation at $10M to $25M+ ([LeverX US cost guide, 2026](https://leverx.com/en-us/blog/sap-s4hana-migration-cost-us)).

The lower bound needs tight scope control, a clean core and a sensible day-rate blend. The upper bound is what happens when those drift. If you want a first number for your own case, try the [SAP implementation cost calculator](/sap-implementation-cost-calculator/).

## Where the money goes

Implementation services, not software, take the biggest share. This is the split I use as a starting point.

| Cost component | Typical share of total | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Implementation services | 35 to 50 percent | Consulting, configuration, testing, project management |
| Licences or subscription | 10 to 20 percent | Core S/4HANA plus add-ons such as Ariba, SuccessFactors, BTP |
| Custom development and extensions | 5 to 15 percent | ABAP, Fiori apps, BTP extensions, third-party interfaces |
| Data migration | 5 to 10 percent | Extraction, cleansing, conversion, validation |
| Training and change management | 5 to 10 percent | End-user training, adoption tools, change agents |
| Infrastructure and hosting | 5 to 10 percent | Hosting, network, middleware |
| Hypercare | 3 to 5 percent | Post-go-live support and stabilisation |

Contingency is not padding. I remember a telecom company that added a 15 percent buffer to its implementation budget. It used part of it when legacy data threw up integration work nobody had planned.

Without the buffer, the programme would have stalled. Hold it on your side of the table, not inside the partner's price.

## A budget formula you can use before scoping

Use this before detailed scoping. It is crude on purpose, and it gives a CFO a defensible first number.

### On-premise programmes

1. Start with the base software licence cost.
2. Multiply it by 1.5 to 2.5 for implementation services. The multiplier reflects complexity.
3. Add hardware or infrastructure.
4. Add 10 to 20 percent contingency on the total.

A manufacturing client had 200 users and $400K in licences. High customisation pushed the multiplier to 2.2, so implementation came to $880K. Plus $150K hardware and $200K contingency, a total of $1.63M before the project started.

Standard processes, clean data and experienced internal teams push the multiplier toward 1.5. Heavy customisation, messy data and thin internal capability push it to 2.5 or beyond.

### Subscription programmes (RISE or GROW)

1. Take the annual subscription, based on your full user equivalent (FUE) count and tier.
2. Multiply by the years of your term, usually five, for the full view.
3. Add implementation services, priced from the scope you confirm after Explore: the WRICEF count, integrations and countries.
4. Add 10 to 20 percent contingency.

## The six drivers that move the number

### 1. Deployment model: RISE, GROW or on-premise

The deployment choice sets the shape of the cost before anyone configures a transaction.

RISE with SAP bundles S/4HANA Cloud Private Edition, hyperscaler hosting, technical managed services and SAP BTP credits into one subscription priced on FUEs and the committed term. You get one contract. You also lose the ability to negotiate each component on its own.

GROW with SAP packages S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition for mid-sized companies. You can only extend through released interfaces, so the system enforces a clean core. In return, GROW programmes often take about 6 to 9 months, against 9 to 18 months for RISE.

On-premise S/4HANA means a perpetual licence plus annual support. Enterprise Support runs at about 22 percent of the licence value each year, as [Computerworld reported in 2009](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1553083/sap-agrees-to-delay-full-price-hike-on-enterprise-support-switch.html). Over seven to ten years, a company with its own infrastructure team can still come out cheaper on-premise.

### 2. Partner fees and the day-rate blend

The partner, and the mix of locations they staff from, moves the budget more than any other vendor decision. These are indicative senior day rates for 2026.

| Region | Senior day rate (USD) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| US onshore | $1,800 to $3,500 | Lead architect, programme director, US-regulated workstreams |
| GCC (Riyadh, Abu Dhabi, Dubai) | $1,200 to $2,000 | Programme lead, finance architect on regional rollouts |
| Nearshore (Eastern Europe, Mexico) | $700 to $1,400 | Functional lead, integration developer |
| Offshore (India, Philippines) | $300 to $700 | ABAP development, Fiori extensions, support |

Too much onshore seniority can add millions to a large programme. Too little, with no finance lead on site, tends to surface as month-end close problems six months after go-live. That is the more expensive mistake.

Tier 1 firms (Deloitte, Accenture, PwC, EY, IBM, DXC) bring structure and higher rates. Tier 2 and 3 firms cost less per day but often need more direction. Either way, vague goals at the start get paid for in hours later, because rework is billable.

### 3. Customisation and clean core

Every change to SAP standard adds code, documentation and test effort, and it has to be retested at every upgrade. In my experience, companies that customise everything spend 30 to 50 percent more on the implementation.

On one global implementation across six countries, tax logic was built entirely in ABAP. Technically it worked. When one country changed a tax rate, the business had to raise a development request, wait for the build, then retest everything.

Standard tax configuration would have handled most of it. In Public Edition the system enforces a clean core; in Private Edition and on-premise it is a governance decision. My [clean core guide](/sap-clean-core-strategy-what-it-means-for-your-business/) explains SAP's A to D extension levels.

### 4. Data migration

Data is rarely as clean as teams expect. Legacy systems collect duplicates, blank fields and records nobody can explain.

I remember a client who delayed go-live by several weeks simply because of missing entries in their inventory tables. Profiling had been scheduled late. By the time the gaps surfaced, the plan had no slack left.

Regulated industries add another layer: financial records may need to be kept for seven to ten years, in a form the new system can read. My [data migration article](/why-sap-data-migration-fails-and-how-to-fix-it/) covers how to plan this.

### 5. Training and change management

Training is the line most teams try to cut. It is also the one that comes back as extended hypercare.

I once attended a training session that left users overwhelmed. Go-live came and they went back to spreadsheets. Another company I worked with had to redo two months of work because users skipped proper training.

Digital adoption tools can reduce classroom hours. SAP completed its acquisition of WalkMe in September 2024, so WalkMe now sits next to SAP Enable Now in SAP's portfolio. None of them replaces practice on the processes people will run every day.

### 6. Running costs after go-live

Go-live is not the finish line. On-premise support is the 22 percent above. Under RISE and GROW, support is inside the subscription, but extra managed services cost more.

If you are still on ECC, the clock matters. SAP ERP 6.0 on enhancement packages 6 to 8 leaves mainstream maintenance on 31 December 2027, with optional extended maintenance to the end of 2030 at a two-point premium ([SAP News](https://news.sap.com/2020/02/sap-s4hana-maintenance-2040-clarity-choice-sap-business-suite-7/)). SAP Solution Manager 7.2 mainstream maintenance also ends in 2027, and SAP recommends moving to SAP Cloud ALM before then.

AI tooling does not change the budget yet. Joule for consultants and SAP Build Code can shorten some design and build tasks, but SAP has not published a saving I would put into a budget. Ask your partner whether their use of these tools shows up in the effort estimate.

## Costs that show up after the contract is signed

The biggest one is scope. Companies try to keep the cost low at the start, then get hit in the Explore phase of SAP Activate, when the fit-to-standard workshops show the real scope. I cover that pattern, and how to stop it, in [why SAP projects go over budget](/sap-implementation-cost-breakdown-why-budgets-explode-50/).

The rest are smaller but predictable. They are simply missing from most initial quotes.

1. **Data cleanup.** Profiling almost always finds more problems than assumed. Schedule it in the first phase, not before cutover.
2. **Training cut to recover schedule.** One-off workshops do not embed new ways of working. Users need practice on the transactions they will run daily.
3. **Siloed integration testing.** One firm I worked with had to extend user acceptance testing (UAT) by almost a month because it tested workflows in silos. Sales orders feed inventory, billing and finance, so test them together.
4. **Change requests compounding.** I saw one ticket, "add a required field", turn into two days of adjustments across forms, reports and authorisations. Review change requests against budget weekly.
5. **Thin hypercare.** Test environments do not reproduce real business pressure. Without enough trained support on day one, response times slip and users lose faith fast.
6. **Audit and compliance validation.** One public sector rollout I advised needed a month of validation after go-live just to settle audit queries. The effort had never been put in the plan.

## Fixed price, time and material, or both

Fixed price works when scope is well defined and unlikely to change. The partner carries the delivery risk, so the partner prices that risk in. Every change outside the agreed scope becomes a negotiation.

Time and material works when requirements will evolve, which is most large SAP programmes. You pay for actual work, so the exposure sits in your budget.

On complex programmes I prefer a hybrid: fixed price for well-defined phases such as design and initial configuration, time and material for integration, testing and post-go-live work. Whichever model you choose, the scope control has to come from your side.

## Budget checklist before you sign

The biggest cost mistake I see before signing is letting the vendor control the scope. Run through this with your programme director before the statement of work (SoW) is signed, and give each check a client-side owner.

1. **Programme director:** scope is written and owned by you, not drafted by the system integrator.
2. **Data lead:** data profiling is scheduled in the first phase, not just before cutover.
3. **Change lead:** training is priced per user group and includes practice time.
4. **Test manager:** cross-module test cycles are named in the SoW, such as order to cash, procure to pay and record to report.
5. **Financial controller:** audit and validation effort is scoped for regulated processes.
6. **CFO:** contingency of 10 to 20 percent is held by you, not inside the partner's price.
7. **Solution architect:** existing custom code is classified before the scope is agreed.
8. **Procurement:** the partner explains how AI tooling affects its estimate.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>How much does SAP cost for a small business?</summary>

For a company under 200 staff, plan for $80,000 to $500,000 in total. Most land on SAP Business One or S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition. Industry add-ons and the volume of legacy data are what push the number up.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the average cost of an SAP implementation for a mid-sized company?</summary>

For 200 to 1,000 staff, plan for $500,000 to $2 million. Where you land depends on module scope, data quality, integrations and how far you move from standard processes. GROW with SAP programmes often take about 6 to 9 months; RISE with SAP programmes 9 to 18 months.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How much does SAP S/4HANA cost to implement for a large enterprise?</summary>

For 1,000 to 10,000 staff, plan for $2 million to $10 million or more. Global groups with more than 10,000 staff should plan for $10 million to $50 million or more. Multi-country rollouts, integrations and custom code move you up the range.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is included in SAP implementation cost?</summary>

More than the licence. Implementation services are usually the largest component at 35 to 50 percent of the total, and licences or subscription take 10 to 20 percent. Custom development, data migration, training, infrastructure and hypercare make up the rest, with 10 to 20 percent contingency on top.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How much contingency should I add to an SAP budget?</summary>

Hold 10 to 20 percent on top of the budget. Keep it on your side, not inside the partner's price, so it is spent on your priorities. Release it against named risks, not to cover missing scope.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is SAP cloud cheaper than on-premise?</summary>

Not always. Cloud costs less upfront: no hardware, faster start, upgrades included. On-premise costs more upfront, then about 22 percent of the licence value a year in support, so compare total cost over the seven to ten years you plan to run it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is SAP more expensive than Oracle or Microsoft Dynamics 365?</summary>

Usually yes. SAP tends to cost the most, then Oracle Cloud ERP, then Dynamics 365. SAP and Oracle do not publish cloud ERP list prices, while Dynamics 365 Finance lists at $210 per user per month, paid yearly ([Microsoft pricing](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics-365/products/finance/pricing)).

</details>
