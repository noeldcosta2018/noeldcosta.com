# The $50 billion ERP industry and why CFOs still reach for Excel

The logistics supervisor at a manufacturing client I worked with exports his ERP data to Excel every week for inventory planning. They had paid a significant sum for SAP planning modules. The dashboards were first-rate. He still uses the spreadsheet.

When I asked why, he said: "The ERP shows me what the system thinks. The spreadsheet shows me what I think."

That sentence is the actual ERP failure story. Not the go-live. Not the configuration. The gap between what the system produces and what the decision-maker needs.

The ERP market generates approximately $50 billion annually. Companies spend millions on systems that, in every business case I have ever read, promise to eliminate manual spreadsheet work. 88% of organisations still rely on Excel for core business functions. Not because they failed to implement. Because the system, as implemented, did not replace what the spreadsheet was doing.

## Why Excel persists

Excel has four properties that most ERP systems have historically failed to replicate:

**Familiarity.** Most finance and operations professionals have been using Excel for twenty years. The interface is intuitive. The learning curve for a new analysis is minutes, not days. When a manager needs an answer before tomorrow's board meeting, they open Excel because they know it will produce an answer.

**Flexibility.** Excel lets users construct the analysis they need rather than the analysis the system provides. A CFO who wants to model three scenarios for the next quarter's cash position can build that in Excel in an afternoon. The same analysis in an ERP requires a configuration request, a development sprint, and a testing cycle.

**Speed.** Changing a formula takes seconds. Changing an ERP report requires someone who knows how to change it, approval to change it, and time to test the change. For decision-making with tight timelines, Excel always wins on speed.

**Control.** Decision-makers trust what they can see and manipulate. An ERP is, from most users' perspective, a black box that produces outputs they cannot fully explain. Excel is transparent. They can trace every number to its source.

These are not irrational preferences. They are rational responses to systems that were not designed for the decisions the people using them need to make.

## The ERP UX problem

Traditional ERP interfaces were designed to ensure data completeness and process integrity, not to support the decisions that come from that data. The transaction that posts a goods receipt is designed to capture every required field. The report that shows a user what their inventory position means for next month's production plan requires a separate tool, separate configuration, and often a separate specialist.

SAP Fiori was a meaningful step toward addressing this. Role-based launchpads, streamlined transaction screens, mobile access. The direction is right. The implementation, in most companies I have worked with, has not kept pace with the intent. Users have Fiori access but the analytical tiles have not been configured to show the decisions they actually need to make. They use the transactions, then export to Excel for the analysis.

SAP Analytics Cloud and embedded BW analytics represent a more complete answer: bringing the data and the analysis into the same environment as the transaction. For organisations that have invested in configuring these tools properly, Excel dependency is genuinely lower. For most, the analytical investment trails the transactional investment significantly.

## The risks of spreadsheet dependency

The costs of running critical processes in Excel are real and accumulate silently.

**Version control failures.** When five people are updating their own copies of the same spreadsheet and reconciling weekly, the risk of working from the wrong version is constant. I worked with a company that ran its quarterly close process on a shared Excel workbook. Two people edited simultaneously without realising it. The version reconciliation took a week and delayed the board pack.

**Audit trail gaps.** ERP systems maintain a complete audit trail by design. Spreadsheets do not. When an auditor asks why a number changed between the first and final version of a financial statement, the spreadsheet cannot answer that question reliably. The ERP can.

**Data quality divergence.** When the ERP and the spreadsheet hold different versions of the same data, the organisation's decision-making splits. Operations trusts the spreadsheet. Finance trusts the ERP. Discrepancies in the monthly reconciliation consume time and create mistrust between functions.

**Manual error compounding.** Every manual step in a process is a potential error point. A copy-paste that misses a row, a formula that does not extend to new data, a filter applied and forgotten. The more complex the spreadsheet and the more manual steps it contains, the higher the error rate over time.

## Can ERP replace Excel?

Yes, but only if the implementation addresses the reasons Excel was reached for in the first place.

The finance close that runs on Excel pivot tables is not a training problem. It is a reporting configuration problem. Someone designed a close process that requires outputs the ERP does not produce in a usable format. The solution is to configure the ERP to produce those outputs, not to train users to stop using Excel.

The inventory planner who exports to Excel is not resisting the system. The ERP's planning reports do not show him what he needs to see for the decision he is trying to make. The solution is to design the planning reports around the decision, not around the system's data model.

This requires understanding what users are actually doing in Excel before designing the ERP outputs to replace it. In every post-go-live review where spreadsheet dependency is high, the pattern is the same: the ERP reporting was designed by IT and the SAP consultant, based on what the system could produce, not on what the business needed to see.

The practical approach: before go-live, ask every key user what their most important spreadsheet is and what decision it supports. Design the ERP report to replace that spreadsheet. Test with the user that the ERP output meets the decision need before go-live. The spreadsheet does not disappear on its own. It disappears when the system provides something better.

## Where to start

The most tractable targets for Excel replacement are the processes where data accuracy and audit trail matter most: financial close, inventory reconciliation, procurement analysis, and management reporting.

For each of these, the approach is the same: identify the Excel workbook that runs the process, understand what decision it supports, assess whether the ERP can produce that output with configuration effort, and if it can, invest in the configuration rather than accepting the spreadsheet as permanent.

For the 20% of cases where the ERP genuinely cannot produce what the business needs at the speed the business needs it, a structured integration with a tool like SAP Analytics Cloud or Power BI is more appropriate than a manual export and rebuild. The data model stays in the ERP. The visualisation layer meets the decision need.

What does not work: training users to use the ERP reports that were not designed for their decisions. They will return to Excel. Not because they are resistant to change. Because the ERP report does not do the job.

<summary>What are the risks of running business processes in Excel spreadsheets?</summary>

Four categories of risk account for most of the cost of spreadsheet dependency.

Version control failures. When multiple people maintain their own copies of a shared model and reconcile periodically, the risk of working from the wrong version is constant. Duplicate edits, missed updates, and reconciliation delays are common in finance processes run on shared workbooks.

Error accumulation. Each manual step in a spreadsheet process is a potential error point. Copy-paste errors, formula range failures, filter application errors. The more complex the spreadsheet and the more manual steps it contains, the higher the cumulative error rate. In a financial process, these errors surface during audit or in management reports, not in real time.

<details>
<summary>How can ERP systems reduce Excel dependency?</summary>

ERP systems reduce Excel dependency when they are configured to support the decisions users are actually trying to make, not just to capture the data the system needs.

The practical approach: before go-live, identify the most important spreadsheets used by key roles and understand what decision each one supports. Design ERP reports and analytical outputs to replace those specific spreadsheets. Test with the users that the ERP output meets the decision need before go-live.

For inventory planning: configure planning reports to show the same view of supply, demand, and coverage that users were constructing manually in Excel. The planning module is often capable of this. It is frequently not configured to produce it.

For financial close: configure the close process to produce the output format that feeds the management pack directly, rather than requiring a manual export and reformatting step. SAP Analytics Cloud or embedded BW analytics can produce management report formats without an Excel intermediary.

For operational reporting: Fiori analytical tiles designed around role-specific decisions, not around the system's data model, give operational managers what they need without exporting.

The spreadsheet does not disappear because users are told to stop using it. It disappears when the system provides something better for the specific decision it was supporting.

<details>
<summary>What is the ERP UX problem and how is SAP addressing it?</summary>

Traditional ERP interfaces were designed for data completeness and process integrity, not for usability or decision support. Transaction screens require users to navigate complex field structures to complete a process. Reports show data in the format it is stored, not in the format that supports a business decision.

SAP Fiori, introduced as part of the S/4HANA platform, represents the primary response to this problem. Fiori provides role-based launchpads, simplified transaction screens, and mobile-accessible interfaces designed around user roles rather than system modules. The intent is to reduce the cognitive load of using the system by surfacing only what the user needs for their specific role.

SAP Analytics Cloud extends this further by providing embedded analytics and planning capabilities within the SAP environment, reducing the need to export data for analysis.

In practice, the UX improvement is real but unevenly realised. Companies that invest in Fiori configuration, including role-specific analytical tiles and personalised launchpads, see meaningful adoption improvements. Companies that deploy Fiori as a pass-through to the same underlying transactions see limited benefit.

The gap between what SAP provides and what users experience is primarily a configuration investment gap, not a product gap. The tools exist to support decision-making effectively. The configuration to connect those tools to the decisions users actually make requires investment that many implementations defer.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why do inventory and supply chain teams still use Excel after ERP implementation?</summary>

Supply chain and inventory teams persist with Excel for two specific reasons.

First, the planning reports in the ERP were not configured to support the planner's actual decision. A planner who needs to see a rolling twelve-week view of supply and demand coverage by product category, with exception flags for items approaching a coverage threshold, requires specific report configuration. If that report was not designed and configured as part of the implementation, the planner builds it in Excel from an ERP export.

Second, the ERP planning output is trusted for data but not for judgment. The MRP run produces planned orders. The planner uses those as inputs but applies their own judgment about lead times, supplier reliability, and customer priority before making the final call. That judgment process happens in Excel because the ERP does not provide a layer for it.

SAP IBP (Integrated Business Planning) addresses the second problem by providing a planning environment that combines ERP data with analytical and simulation tools. For organisations where supply chain planning is a strategic function, IBP's capability to model scenarios and run simulations in the same environment as the underlying data is meaningful.

For most organisations, the immediate intervention is reporting configuration: design the planning report around the planner's decision, not around the system's data model, and the Excel dependency reduces significantly.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the real cost of ERP failure?</summary>

ERP failure cost calculations typically focus on implementation overruns, but the ongoing cost of a system that does not replace manual processes is usually larger than the implementation cost over a five-year horizon.

The quantifiable costs: manual data reconciliation time across the finance and operations teams that maintain parallel ERP and spreadsheet processes; audit remediation costs when spreadsheet-based reporting produces errors that require correction; the cost of the duplicate infrastructure (ERP licences plus the human time maintaining manual equivalents); and the productivity loss from decision-making delayed by manual data assembly.

The less quantifiable but real costs: the senior finance or operations talent spending time on data assembly that the ERP should have automated; the errors in management information that result from manual reconciliation failures; and the strategic opportunity cost of analytics and AI capabilities that cannot be built on top of a data foundation that is split between an ERP and a collection of spreadsheets.

The most honest framing: an ERP that has been implemented but has not replaced manual processes is an ERP that has been purchased but not delivered. The implementation cost was spent. The business case benefit was not realised. Closing that gap requires understanding what the manual processes are doing and configuring the ERP to do it instead.

</details>
