A mid-sized media company in Qatar was rolling out SAP across multiple regions. Finance and procurement were going live in phase one. They also wanted to introduce AI forecasting models into their reporting stack.

By week six, delays were creeping in. Business users said they had signed off on requirements but still felt confused about what they were getting. AI use cases had been proposed and nobody could explain who owned the data or how the output would be used. Stakeholders were showing up to meetings late or not at all.

It was that in-between phase. Too early to call it a failure. Too late to pretend it would fix itself.

Over the next four weeks, seven frameworks applied in sequence changed the trajectory. The project landed eight weeks later than planned. Not perfect. But it landed. And the second phase started on significantly better ground.

These are the seven frameworks. I have used versions of all of them across 23 years of delivery on SAP, Oracle, and AI programmes in the Gulf, Europe, and beyond. None of them are exotic. All of them work if you apply them with discipline rather than as documentation exercises.

## Framework 1: map the current state before planning the fix

The most common mistake at the start of an SAP or AI programme is jumping to the solution before documenting what actually exists. Process documentation is usually out of date. People describe how things should work, not how they do work. The gap between the two is where implementation problems live.

A useful current state map takes three to five days of workshops with the people who actually do the work, not the people who manage them. It covers the process steps in sequence, the systems used at each step, the manual interventions and workarounds, and the data flows between departments.

What you are looking for: the manual steps that nobody mentions because they have become invisible, the workarounds that have been running for years and are now considered standard process, and the data quality issues that everyone knows about and nobody has fixed.

In Qatar, the current state workshops were the first time Finance and Procurement had sat in the same room and walked through the same process. They had different names for the same steps and different assumptions about who owned each one. That conversation alone resolved three weeks of scope ambiguity.

A current state map done properly takes days. A current state map treated as a document-collection exercise takes months and produces something nobody trusts.

## Framework 2: establish who decides what before the first delay

Decision rights are the most consistently missing structural element in SAP and AI projects. Everyone has a view. Nobody knows who has the authority to make the final call. Decisions get deferred to the next meeting. The next meeting defers to the steering committee. The steering committee defers back to the working group.

A RACI matrix (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) assigns a role to every decision and deliverable. One person is Accountable. That person can be Responsible themselves or delegate responsibility. Consulted parties provide input. Informed parties receive the outcome.

The practical version: identify the twenty to thirty most critical decisions in the current phase and run a RACI workshop with the decision-makers in the room. Where the RACI assignment is contested, that tells you something important: either the governance structure is not clear or there is a political conflict about authority. Either way, you want to surface it in week two, not week fourteen.

RACI without enforcement is decoration. The names on the matrix need to match the people with real decision authority. A matrix that assigns accountability to a role title rather than a named individual is a way of avoiding the conversation about who actually owns the risk.

## Framework 3: tie SAP and AI to business capabilities

SAP and AI implementations generate a lot of technical activity that the business cannot see and does not understand. The connection between the system being built and the business outcome it is supposed to deliver becomes abstract. Business sponsors disengage. "IT projects" get blamed for delays that are actually business decision delays.

Capability mapping connects system functionality to business capabilities: the things the organisation needs to be able to do. Instead of tracking whether the purchase order workflow is configured, you track whether the organisation can process supplier invoices within three days of receipt. The system configuration is the mechanism. The capability is the outcome.

In SAP Activate terms, this maps to the fit-to-standard workshops in the Explore phase. Each business process in scope is a capability. Each gap between the standard SAP process and the required capability is a decision: accept the standard, configure a variant, or customise.

Keeping the conversation at the capability level, not the transaction level, maintains business engagement through the build phase. It also surfaces the cases where a capability was assumed to be in scope but was never explicitly confirmed.

## Framework 4: find the root cause before the next patch

When the same type of problem keeps surfacing across sprints or phases, patching individual instances does not help. The root cause is somewhere upstream, and until it is addressed, the symptoms will continue.

The five-whys approach is the simplest root cause tool: ask why the problem occurred, then ask why that cause occurred, and repeat until you reach the factor you can actually change. Five iterations typically reach the real cause. Two iterations typically reach the symptom.

In a SAP data migration context: the data quality issue in the trial load is usually a symptom. The first why is that the source data extract had incorrect field mappings. The second why is that the mapping specification was not reviewed by the business data owner. The third why is that the data owner was not assigned until after the mapping was complete. The fourth why is that the project plan did not include data owner engagement as a dependency for mapping development. That is the root cause. The fix is a project governance decision, not a data fix.

Root cause analysis done in a blame-free environment produces honest answers. Done in a blame-attributing environment, it produces defensiveness and partial information. The facilitator's job is to make the conversation about process, not people.

## Framework 5: rank priorities before the build begins

In every SAP and AI scope discussion, there is a consensus trap: the group does not want to make trade-offs, so everything ends up labelled high priority. When everything is high priority, nothing is high priority. The build team tries to do everything. Nothing is done well.

MoSCoW (Must Have, Should Have, Could Have, Won't Have) forces prioritisation by requiring the group to choose. Must Have items are the absolute minimum required for go-live. Should Have items are important but not blockers. Could Have items are desirable if time and budget allow. Won't Have items are explicitly deferred.

The discipline is in the Must Have category. In my experience, the first pass of a MoSCoW exercise puts 80% of items in Must Have. The correct number for a realistic programme is closer to 30 to 40% of scope in Must Have. The difference is usually assumptions: items that are in Must Have because nobody has challenged whether they are actually essential.

Running MoSCoW with business and technology in the same room, rather than separately, prevents the version where IT's Must Have list and business's Must Have list are incompatible and nobody reconciles them until configuration is already underway.

## Framework 6: rank risks by what can actually stop the project

Risk registers are often long lists of items scored by probability and impact, reviewed monthly, with RAG statuses that rarely move. They capture the existence of risk without driving action.

The more useful approach: separate risks that can stop the programme from risks that make the programme harder. The first category needs owners, defined response plans, and weekly visibility. The second category needs monitoring.

In the Qatar project, the risk log looked fine on paper, but the team felt on edge. When we ran a structured risk ranking session with the delivery leads, three risks that were rated as medium in the formal register were immediately re-rated as high by the people who owned them. They had been soft-scored because nobody wanted to escalate.

Risk registers that look fine but feel tense are almost always concealing issues. The register is not the truth. The conversations around it are. A weekly risk review that asks "what kept you up last night?" rather than "what is the status of item 14?" surfaces different information.

## Framework 7: do the post-mortem before you forget

Post-mortem reviews after a phase or go-live are the mechanism that prevents the same mistakes from recurring in the next phase. They are also the first thing cut when schedule pressure mounts.

A useful post-mortem covers: what did we plan to achieve, what did we actually achieve, what went well and should be repeated, what went badly and what caused it, and what would we do differently with hindsight. It should produce three to five specific action items with owners, not a document summarising what happened.

The most common failure: the post-mortem session turns into a justification exercise where teams defend their decisions rather than examining them. The facilitator's job is to keep the conversation forward-looking. The question is not who is to blame for week-six delays. The question is what structural change prevents week-six delays in the next phase.

In Qatar, the post-mortem session after the first phase go-live was the first time the delivery team had been candid about what had actually happened. It produced four specific changes to the governance model for phase two. Phase two started faster and had fewer repeated issues.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What are consulting frameworks and why do SAP projects use them?</summary>

Consulting frameworks are structured approaches to analysis, decision-making, and problem-solving. In SAP and AI programme delivery, they provide a shared language and method for the diverse group of people involved in a complex implementation: business leads, IT architects, project managers, system integrators, and executive sponsors.

Without structure, each group defaults to its own mental model of the project. Business leads think about process outcomes. IT architects think about technical architecture. Project managers think about timeline and resource. These perspectives are all valid, but without a shared framework, they do not produce aligned decisions.

Frameworks like current state mapping, RACI, and MoSCoW provide a common structure for conversations that would otherwise be circular. They do not eliminate disagreement, but they make disagreement explicit and resolvable rather than implicit and persistent.

In SAP specifically, the SAP Activate methodology is itself a framework: it structures the delivery phases, the key decisions at each phase gate, and the artefacts that must be produced. The frameworks described here operate within and alongside Activate, addressing the structural and interpersonal dynamics that methodology alone does not resolve.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does current state mapping work in an SAP implementation?</summary>

Current state mapping documents how business processes actually work before the SAP configuration begins. It is distinct from the as-is process documentation that organisations often already have, because that documentation typically describes how processes are supposed to work, not how they do work.

The most useful current state maps are built in workshops with the people who execute the processes, not the people who manage them. Workshop participants walk through the process step by step, identifying the systems used, the handoffs between teams, the manual interventions, and the workarounds that have become standard practice.

What the map reveals: gaps between documented process and actual practice; workarounds that exist because the current system cannot support the process properly; data quality issues that are managed manually; and ownership ambiguities where two teams think the other is responsible for the same step.

In SAP Activate, current state mapping contributes to the fit-gap analysis in the Explore phase. The current state becomes the baseline against which SAP's standard processes are compared. Gaps identified in this analysis drive the customisation and configuration decisions that follow.

The map should be completed before fit-to-standard workshops begin. Attempting to assess fit to SAP standard processes without a clear picture of current state produces a gap analysis built on assumptions rather than evidence.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is MoSCoW prioritisation and when is it used in SAP programmes?</summary>

MoSCoW is a prioritisation technique that categorises requirements into Must Have, Should Have, Could Have, and Won't Have. It forces explicit trade-offs rather than allowing all requirements to be treated as equally important.

Must Have: the minimum required for the delivery to meet its purpose. Without these items, the go-live cannot proceed.

Should Have: important requirements that add significant value but where the delivery can proceed without them. These are typically included if time and budget allow.

Could Have: desirable requirements that would improve the outcome but are not critical. These are the first to be deferred when pressure increases.

Won't Have: items explicitly excluded from the current scope. Documenting this category prevents requirements from re-entering scope informally.

MoSCoW is most valuable at two points in an SAP programme: during the Explore phase when fit-gap findings are driving customisation decisions (to prevent scope creep before build begins), and during the Realize phase when defects and enhancements are competing for build time (to prevent the most critical items from being displaced by lower-priority work).

The typical challenge: the initial pass of a MoSCoW exercise places too many items in Must Have. Running the exercise with a facilitator who challenges Must Have classifications, and with business and technology in the same session to prevent disconnected prioritisation, produces a more realistic output.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you run an effective risk review on an SAP project?</summary>

An effective risk review is a conversation, not a status update. The goal is to surface what people are actually worried about, not to confirm that items in the risk register are still rated the same as last week.

The structure: start with open questions ("What is your biggest concern this week that is not in the risk register?") before moving to the formal register. Open questions produce information that status updates do not.

For each high-priority risk: what has changed since the last review, what action has been taken, is the risk trajectory improving or worsening, and is the response plan still appropriate. A risk that was medium probability three weeks ago may be high probability now if conditions have changed. The scoring should reflect current reality, not the original assessment.

Risks that consistently stay at the same rating without action or resolution are often understated. Ask the owner directly: is this rating accurate, or have we got comfortable with a risk that should be escalating? Some of the most damaging project risks are the ones that have been on the register since week two, rated medium, with no action taken.

Weekly risk reviews for the current phase, shifting to biweekly during stable periods. The steering committee should see the top five risks with current status and next action at every session, not a full register.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What makes a useful post-mortem after an SAP go-live?</summary>

A useful post-mortem produces specific, actionable changes to how the next phase or project is run. A documentation exercise that summarises what happened is not a post-mortem. It is a record.

The post-mortem should cover four questions: what did we set out to achieve, what did we achieve, what worked and should be carried forward, and what did not work and what should change.

The "what did not work" section is where most value is created and where most sessions fail. Under schedule pressure or in political environments, teams default to surface-level explanations: "we were under-resourced," "the timeline was too tight." These are not root causes. They are symptoms.

A good facilitator pushes below the surface: why was the team under-resourced? Was the resource plan wrong, was the scope wrong, or was the governance structure unable to escalate resource gaps effectively? Each answer leads to a specific, actionable change for the next phase.

Post-mortem output should be: three to five specific changes with named owners and implementation timelines. Not a document. Not a presentation. Actions. If the session produces only observations and no commitments, the session was a debrief, not a post-mortem.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do consulting frameworks help with AI implementation in SAP environments?</summary>

AI implementation in SAP environments fails for most of the same structural reasons that SAP itself fails, plus a few that are specific to AI: unclear ownership of the data the AI needs, undefined success metrics, and no agreed process for acting on AI output.

Current state mapping applied to an AI use case identifies who owns the data the model needs to run, how that data currently flows through the organisation, and what quality issues exist. A forecasting model built on data that nobody owns and that is inconsistently captured will produce forecasts that nobody trusts.

RACI applied to an AI use case answers: who owns the data, who validates the model output, who decides when to act on the model's recommendation, and who is accountable when the recommendation is wrong. These questions are rarely answered before AI is implemented. They should be answered before the model is built.

MoSCoW applied to AI use cases distinguishes between use cases that are genuinely essential to the business case and use cases that are interesting but not critical. AI programmes that try to implement fifteen use cases simultaneously typically deliver none of them well. Starting with two or three genuinely essential use cases, with clear ownership and defined success metrics, produces better outcomes than broad ambition without focus.

</details>
