An SAP implementation partner delivers your programme: it supplies the team that designs, builds, tests and deploys the system, and it is accountable for that delivery. An SAP independent consultant works on your side of the table, with no stake in the partner's revenue, and checks the contract, the design and the partner's work.

Short answer: most large programmes need a partner to deliver. Many also need an independent advisor during contracting, design and the review of the partner's deliverables, while the partner does the building.

This article is one part of my guide on [how to choose an SAP implementation partner](/how-to-choose-sap-implementation-partner/). Here I only cover the split between the two roles, and when each one earns its fee.

## What an SAP implementation partner brings

A partner is the firm that runs the delivery. It brings functional consultants by module, technical and integration people, data migration specialists, testers, change and training leads, and the project management that holds them together. It also brings a bench, so when someone leaves, someone else can step in.

Most partners deliver with SAP Activate, SAP's implementation methodology. It runs in six phases: [Discover, Prepare, Explore, Realize, Deploy and Run](https://www.sap.com/products/erp/activate-methodology.html). Explore is where fit-to-standard analysis confirms the design, and Realize is where the system is built and tested in iterations.

SAP also grades partners. Its partner programme, SAP PartnerEdge, uses a competency framework with [seven competencies and three tiers each: essential, advanced and expert](https://news.sap.com/2022/09/sap-partner-visibility-new-recognition-system/). Partners earn them on expert knowledge, customer success and proficiency, and you can search the designations in [SAP Partner Finder](https://www.sap.com/partners/find.html).

For RISE with SAP there is a further recognition. SAP's [July 2025 description of RISE with SAP Validated Partners](https://news.sap.com/2025/07/transform-sap-implementations-meet-evolving-customer-expectations/) asks for the RISE with SAP Methodology and updated skills in key roles.

Partner projects must also document clean core quality gates in SAP Cloud ALM. My guide to [choosing an S/4HANA migration partner](/choose-s4hana-migration-partner/) covers what those badges prove and what they do not.

No independent consultant replaces any of this. One person, or a handful of people, cannot staff a programme, carry delivery risk under a contract or cover for a lead who resigns in the middle of testing.

### Where the partner model has structural limits

The limits are in how the contract is built, not in anyone's intent. A partner's commercial incentive is to win the work, start it and add to it when it finds more. Your incentive is to finish within the number you gave the board.

That creates three blind spots when the partner is the only expert in the room:

1. **The partner writes the scope.** The same firm then raises the change requests against it.
2. **The partner checks its own work.** Design documents, test results and cutover plans are signed off on the partner's word unless someone on your side can read them.
3. **The proposal team is not always the delivery team.** The people who presented the bid may not be the people who turn up after signature.

None of this makes partners bad. It means the client needs someone who can read the partner's work with the same depth, and that person cannot be on the partner's payroll.

## What an independent SAP consultant does

"Independent SAP consultant" covers two different jobs. Mixing them up is where a lot of buying mistakes start.

- **An independent contractor inside the delivery team.** A freelance specialist hired to fill a role, such as a finance lead or a data migration lead. They add capacity and work to the delivery plan, often under the partner's direction.
- **An independent advisor on the client side.** They report to your sponsor, CIO or CFO. They do not build the system; they check the contract, the design and the deliverables, and they help you make the decisions the partner should not make for you.

This article is mostly about the second kind. The value of a client-side advisor comes from one thing: they have nothing to sell you after the review. My notes on [SAP negotiation advisors](/sap-negotiation-advisors-reduce-cost/) set out the independence checks in more detail.

An advisor has limits too. They have no bench, they do not carry delivery accountability, and an advisor who starts directing the partner's consultants blurs who is responsible for what. Keep the role narrow and it works.

## Partner or independent consultant: when you need which

This is how the two roles compare on the questions that matter to a buyer:

| Question | SAP implementation partner | Independent advisor |
|---|---|---|
| Who they work for | Their firm, under contract to you | You only |
| What they deliver | Design, build, test, data migration, cutover, hypercare | Reviews, challenge, decisions you can defend |
| Delivery accountability | Yes, under the statement of work | No |
| Commercial interest | Revenue grows with scope and duration | None in the scope or duration of the delivery |
| Main strength | Capacity, bench, methodology, product depth | A second reader with no stake in the answer |
| Main limit | Checks its own work unless you add a check | Cannot staff or deliver a programme |
| When to involve them | From contract signature through hypercare | Selection and contracting, design and deliverable sign-off |

The table points to three situations:

1. **Partner alone.** Your own team has run an SAP programme before and can challenge the partner's architecture, contract and deliverables without outside help. The scope is small and well defined.
2. **Independent consultant alone.** There is nothing to build yet, or the build is small. A programme health check, a business case, an RFP, a contract review, or a single specialist role filled by a contractor.
3. **Both.** A programme of any real size where your side does not have senior SAP experience in-house. The partner delivers, the advisor checks, and you decide.

Most of the CIOs and CFOs reading this are in the third group. They have run large projects, but not an SAP programme, and the partner knows more about SAP than anyone on the client side.

## When both: the three points where I bring in an independent advisor

I bring in an independent consultant, rather than rely on the partner alone, at three stages. They are contracting, design and the assessment of the partner's documents. Between those points, the advisor's involvement can be light.

### Contracting

Selection and contracting is where the cost of the whole programme is set. I would shortlist at least three partners, and selection takes about 12 weeks when it is done properly. My guide to [writing an SAP partner RFP](/sap-partner-rfp/) covers the scoring.

What I weigh most when choosing a partner for a client is client references. On the reference calls, I ask about the partner's experience on projects similar to the buyer's. A reference from a different kind of project tells you very little.

Two things make me walk away from a bid. The first is weak quality in the proposed resources. The second is a partner that cannot explain the architecture and the plan for your programme.

Generic answers that could apply to any company are the warning sign for both.

Check the people as well as the firm. SAP Certification badges are [issued on Credly](https://learning.sap.com/helpcenter/digital-badges), so ask each proposed lead for the badge link, not a certificate PDF.

On the commercial side, the model I recommend is time and materials with the effort quantified. The estimated effort by phase and role goes into the contract, so you can track actual effort against it.

The part of a statement of work that clients most often leave out is the detailed assumptions and exclusions. Every vague assumption is a future change request. My guide to the [SAP statement of work](/sap-statement-of-work/) sets out the categories, and my notes on [ERP contract negotiation](/erp-implementation-contract-negotiation-cost-review-cfo/) cover named resources and milestones.

### Design

Budgets get hit in the Explore phase, as the WRICEF list (workflows, reports, interfaces, conversions, enhancements and forms) grows. The partner runs the fit-to-standard workshops and proposes the design. Your business team often agrees to it without knowing which choices cost money later.

An independent advisor reads the design with three questions in mind:

1. **Does it stay close to SAP standard?** Every deviation needs a business reason, not a habit carried over from the old system.
2. **Is every WRICEF object justified?** Count the objects at the start of Explore and at the end, and ask why each new one exists.
3. **Does the design match what was priced?** If Explore produces a bigger design than the contract assumed, you need to know before Realize starts.

The partner still owns the design. The advisor gives your design authority enough depth to approve or reject it with confidence.

### Assessing the partner's deliverables

Every phase ends with documents: design documents, functional specifications, test plans and results, data migration reconciliations, a cutover plan. If acceptance is tied to payment, signing them off is a commercial decision as well as a technical one.

An advisor reviews each deliverable against its acceptance criteria before your team signs. The question is simple: is this complete enough to build, test or go live on, or is it a placeholder that will be finished on your budget later? My guide to [SAP quality gates](/sap-quality-gates-implementation/) shows how to make those sign-offs stick.

## Keeping the two roles clean

An advisor is not there to compete with the partner or to second-guess every decision. When the roles blur, the partner loses accountability and the client pays twice for the same opinion.

These rules keep the arrangement working:

1. **Put independence in writing.** No revenue from the partners being assessed, no referral fees and no reseller arrangement.
2. **Rule out the follow-on work.** The person who assessed the bids should not later bid for the delivery.
3. **Write the advisor's scope.** Which documents they review, at which stages, and to whom they report.
4. **Keep one line of accountability.** The advisor reviews, the client decides, the partner delivers. The advisor does not direct the partner's team.
5. **Tell the partners at RFP stage.** A partner that knows its contract, design and deliverables will be reviewed writes them more carefully. A partner confident in its plan has little to lose from a second reader.

Sometimes the review finds a problem the partner cannot fix. I have changed a partner during a programme, and the trigger was that the partner could not provide quality resources. My guide to [changing your SAP partner mid-project](/change-sap-partner-mid-project/) covers what to secure before that conversation.

## Your next step

Before the RFP goes out, list the decisions your own team must be able to challenge. They are the architecture and plan, the contract, the design in Explore and each deliverable sign-off.

Next to each one, write the name of the person on your side who can do it. The blanks are where an independent advisor belongs.

Across the 21 SAP programmes I have led or rescued, the biggest mistake I see is not planning properly at the start. Deciding who checks the partner is part of that planning.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between an SAP partner and an independent SAP consultant?</summary>

An SAP implementation partner is a firm that delivers the programme and is accountable for it under a statement of work. An independent SAP consultant is either a freelance specialist in the delivery team or a client-side advisor.

The advisor checks the contract, the design and the partner's deliverables. The advisor has no stake in the partner's revenue.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When should I hire an independent SAP consultant?</summary>

Bring one in when your own team does not have senior SAP experience to challenge the partner. The points that matter most are the contracting stage, the design in the Explore phase and the sign-off of the partner's deliverables. An independent consultant also suits work with nothing to build yet, such as a health check, a business case or an RFP.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can an independent consultant replace an SAP implementation partner?</summary>

Not on a full programme. An implementation needs a team across modules, technology, data, testing and change, plus a bench to cover people who leave, and a firm that carries delivery accountability. An independent consultant can fill a specialist role or advise the client, but cannot do the partner's job.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I check that an SAP consultant is really independent?</summary>

Ask in writing whether they receive any revenue, referral fees or reseller income from SAP partners, and from the partners you are assessing in particular. Confirm they will not bid for the delivery work they reviewed. Then make sure they are paid by you and report to your sponsor, not to the partner.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Will my SAP partner object to an independent advisor?</summary>

Some partners see it as friction, so set it up openly. Tell partners at RFP stage that the contract, design and deliverables will be independently reviewed, and keep the advisor's role to review, not direction of the partner's team. A partner confident in its plan has little to lose from a second reader.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I verify an SAP consultant's certification?</summary>

SAP Certification badges are issued on Credly. Ask each proposed consultant for the link to their badge rather than a certificate PDF, and check the credential name, holder and date on the badge page. A certification confirms exam knowledge; references on similar projects tell you whether the person can deliver.

</details>
