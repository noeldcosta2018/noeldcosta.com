Teams dive into SAP kickoff meetings with confidence, only to discover that key roles were vaguely defined, scope boundaries weren't agreed on, and nobody can answer a basic question: what are we actually trying to achieve? A project charter should prevent all of that. Most don't, because they're written to satisfy a governance requirement rather than to anchor the work.

I've worked on programmes where the charter looked clean but failed to connect to anything real. Objectives listed without links to measurable outcomes. Scope defined without explicit exclusions. Stakeholders named by title without accountability. Those documents created false confidence and then caused expensive confusion when pressure arrived.

The version that works is the one people argue about during drafting. That's how you know it's honest.

## Charter versus proposal versus plan

Three documents that get confused in every organization doing a large SAP programme. They're not interchangeable.

| Document | Purpose | Created by | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Project proposal | Justify why the project should happen | Business sponsor | Before approval |
| Project charter | Authorize the project; define scope, objectives, stakeholders, and governance | Sponsor + PM | At initiation |
| Project plan | Define how the work gets done: tasks, resources, dependencies | Project manager | After charter is approved |

The charter is the governance document. It draws lines. If your S/4HANA implementation spans multiple modules, geographies, and vendors, the charter is where you define what's included and what isn't: before vendor SOWs get signed and team members start making assumptions.

## What a charter must cover

**Vision tied to a business outcome.** Not "system modernization." A manufacturing client nearly scrapped their SAP programme midstream because the charter offered no financial justification. Nobody could defend the spend because nothing had been linked to tangible outcomes. The vision should state what operations will improve and by how much.

**Scope with explicit exclusions.** This is the most overlooked section. I've seen teams write detailed in-scope lists and leave out the exclusions entirely. The exclusions are where arguments happen. A healthcare client I worked with kept their rollout focused because the charter explicitly named what was out of scope, when the marketing head tried to add analytics during UAT, the steering committee blocked it immediately. That one decision saved $850,000 and six weeks.

**Named stakeholders with accountability.** Listing departments isn't enough. List names, roles, and what each person owns, when something breaks or timelines slip, accountability needs to be individual, not departmental. A good stakeholder section looks like: "Finance Lead: defines reporting requirements, signs off on FI/CO configuration, approves data migration readiness."

**Measurable success criteria.** I've worked on SAP programmes where everyone celebrated at go-live, and two months later nobody could answer whether it delivered the promised value. One manufacturing client spent over $4 million and couldn't produce metrics for the CFO. Nobody had defined success at the charter stage.

A retail client who did it right had defined KPIs from day one: 32% drop in order processing costs, 90% user adoption within 90 days of go-live. Phase 2 was approved within weeks of delivery. Metrics in the charter make post-project decisions straightforward.

**Timeline with committed milestones.** Not estimates. Commitments. A retail client delayed their go-live by four months because a three-week slip in requirements gathering was never added to the schedule. The team assumed they'd catch up downstream. They spent $1.8 million in overages. On a manufacturing engagement, when the data migration team flagged a delay, the steering committee authorized staffing support immediately because the charter made the milestone commitment visible. That decision saved time and $600,000.

**Budget and risk.** High-level estimates and the three or four risks that could derail the project. Naming them early builds credibility and forces decisions before they become crises.

## Five steps to create it

**Step 1: Ask the people who know the project.** Before writing anything, sit with sponsors, functional leads, and end users. Ask what's broken that the project is meant to fix. What are the real constraints? What was tried before that didn't work?

I worked with a manufacturing client who assumed they knew what the shop floor needed. They never asked. Six months into implementation, the real workflow issues weren't being addressed. $1.8 million in and they were rebuilding from scratch.

On a finance transformation, IT had planned to roll out SAP assuming it would solve efficiency problems. Stakeholder conversations revealed finance was struggling with data quality, not system inefficiency. If nobody had asked, the project would have spent millions solving the wrong problem.

**Step 2: Separate must-have from nice-to-have before writing scope.** When you collect stakeholder input, immediately categorize it. What's critical to go-live? What can go to phase 2? What doesn't belong in this project at all? A professional services client went 40% over budget because requirements from three separate stakeholder conversations weren't organized before they were committed to. The same requirements kept getting "rediscovered" months into the project.

**Step 3: Use a template, then adapt it.** A generic project charter template covers enough to start. An SAP-specific one is better. The additions that matter: integration points and dependencies, data migration scope and ownership, module-specific assumptions, and regulatory or compliance requirements. One team started with a generic template that didn't mention data cleanup. Six months in, they discovered their legacy data was unusable. It added $750K and three months to the project.

**Step 4: Be specific enough to settle arguments.** Vague objectives cause expensive disagreements. A retail client in Singapore had "modernize inventory management" in their charter. Half the team thought that meant faster processing. The other half was focused on forecasting. $1.8 million spent and nobody agreed on what success looked like. Every objective in the charter should pass the test: "Can I prove this was completed?"

**Step 5: Get real sign-off, not ceremonial approval.** The charter isn't done when it's signed; it's done when the people signing it have read it, challenged it, and accepted the commitments it contains. Walk sponsors and senior leadership through objectives, scope boundaries, timeline commitments, and risks. If someone questions a decision during that walkthrough, the charter is doing its job. Better now than six months in.

## Common charter mistakes

| Mistake | What it causes | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Vague objectives ("improve efficiency") | Team works in different directions; success is undefined | Attach a number to every objective |
| No exclusions listed | Scope expands quietly; everyone adds their requirements | Document what's out of scope as carefully as what's in |
| Stakeholders listed by title only | No accountability; decisions deferred indefinitely | Name individuals, not departments |
| No link to business value | Sponsor support weakens; funding at risk mid-programme | Connect every objective to a financial or operational outcome |
| Success criteria missing | Go-live celebrated; value never measured | Define KPIs before kickoff |
| Parallel programmes not mapped | Resource conflicts, budget collisions, delivery failure | Document dependencies and shared resources explicitly |

One retail client spent $3.2 million on an SAP implementation that never went live. Their finance redesign and SAP project were scheduled in parallel with the same team and the same budget window. No coordination. No reference to the dependency. The charter hadn't addressed portfolio overlap, and leadership caught it too late.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is a project charter in an SAP implementation?</summary>

A project charter formally authorizes the SAP project and sets the foundation for everything that follows. It defines scope boundaries, names the project sponsor with their authority, identifies key stakeholders and their accountabilities, establishes high-level timelines and milestones, documents the business case and measurable success criteria, and flags the major risks.

What separates a useful charter from a bureaucratic one: the useful version has specifics that settle arguments. It names who owns what. It says what's out of scope, not just what's in. It links objectives to numbers. Without that level of honesty, it authorizes the project without guiding it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What's the difference between a project charter and a project plan?</summary>

The charter authorizes and defines. The plan executes and controls. The charter draws scope lines, names sponsors, establishes governance, and sets high-level milestones. The plan breaks all of that into hundreds of tasks with dependencies, resource assignments, and sequencing.

Both are necessary. A project plan built without a solid charter tends to drift because the scope boundaries were never agreed on. A charter without a plan gives teams a governance document but no execution roadmap. In SAP implementations, both need to exist and both need to be connected; the plan should trace back to the charter commitments.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Who should create the project charter?</summary>

The project sponsor owns the charter and the decisions it captures. The project manager drafts it and facilitates the conversations that produce the content. Finance, IT, and operations leads contribute the specifics: objectives that come from the business side, constraints from IT, and process scope from operations.

What I recommend against: letting the implementation partner write the charter. Their incentive is to start the project, not to protect the client from scope they don't need. The charter should represent the client's position before vendor commitments are made, not after.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How detailed should a project charter be?</summary>

Detailed enough to settle arguments, not so detailed that nobody reads it. The test: if two people on the steering committee have a disagreement about scope, timeline, or accountability, the charter should resolve it. If it can't, it's too vague.

Practically: objectives with numbers, scope with explicit exclusions, stakeholders with names and defined accountability, milestones with dates, and risks that are specific enough to be actionable. The charter isn't the project plan; it doesn't need individual task breakdowns. It needs enough structure to anchor the plan and governance that follows.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What happens when there's no project charter on an SAP programme?</summary>

The same pattern. Teams make different assumptions about scope. Stakeholders add requirements informally. Timelines drift without anyone having the authority to hold them. Roles are unclear, so decisions get deferred. Budget burn accelerates without visibility.

One client started their SAP implementation without a proper charter: just a statement of work from the implementation partner and a kickoff presentation. Two million dollars later, they realized key business processes hadn't been scoped at all. The engagement had to restart. The charter-equivalent document they eventually produced took three weeks to write and settled six months of ambiguity.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can the project charter be updated during implementation?</summary>

Yes, but through a controlled process, not informally. The charter should be treated as a baseline, when something materially changes (scope, sponsor, major timeline shift, budget adjustment), the charter gets updated with a version number, a record of what changed and why, and renewed sign-off from the relevant stakeholders.

What the charter shouldn't be: a living document that gets quietly revised whenever the project drifts. That defeats its purpose as a governance anchor, when someone proposes a change that affects the charter, that's a scope change request; it goes through change control, gets impact-assessed, and results in an official charter update or a formal deferral to a later phase.

</details>
