I do not normally write about political decisions. This is an exception, because the 2025 tariff changes are not just a policy story. They are an operations problem. Companies that have not updated their procurement configurations, pricing models, and landed cost calculations are running on assumptions that no longer reflect the actual cost structure.

That gap will show up in margin compression, supplier invoice discrepancies, and financial reporting errors that compound quietly over months before they become visible in the board numbers.

## What the tariffs actually are

The 2025 tariff structure as of April:

1. **10% universal tariff** on all US imports regardless of origin
2. **145% tariff on Chinese goods**, covering electronics, clothing, industrial components, and most manufactured products
3. **20-25% tariffs on EU, Canadian, and Mexican goods**, covering a broad range of agricultural and industrial categories
4. **25% tariff on imported vehicles and automotive parts** across all origins

The retaliatory responses:
1. China: 125% tariffs on US agricultural products and technology exports
2. EU: tariffs on US cars, spirits, and selected manufactured goods
3. Canada: tariffs on US steel, agricultural products, and selected consumer goods

The immediate supply chain effect: importers face sharply higher costs on incoming goods. Some of that cost can be absorbed in margin. Most of it cannot.

## How tariff costs reach consumer prices

The mechanism is straightforward. Importers pay the tariff at the border. They had two choices: absorb it or pass it through. Most cannot absorb a 25% or 145% cost increase on a product category without becoming unprofitable. They pass it through to their customers. Those customers, if they are also in a supply chain, pass it through again. By the time it reaches the end consumer, the cost increase is fully loaded.

The 2019 tariff cycle, which included 25% on Chinese goods, produced well-documented analysis from the Federal Reserve showing that consumers paid over 90% of tariff costs. The 2025 tariffs are structurally larger and affect more categories.

The companies most exposed: retailers with high China sourcing proportions for consumer electronics and clothing, construction material distributors (steel and aluminum price exposure), automotive manufacturers and dealers, and any business with complex cross-border supply chains across US-China-EU corridors.

## Three sectors showing early impact

**Consumer electronics.** The 145% China tariff is severe enough that most electronics retailers cannot maintain current pricing without unsustainable margin compression. Products in the $50-$500 range, where China manufacturing represents 60-80% of the supply base, are seeing wholesale cost increases of 20-40% depending on how much origin diversification the supplier has already done.

**Construction and industrial materials.** Steel and aluminum tariffs have been a recurring feature of US trade policy since 2018, but the 2025 structure is broader. Builders, manufacturers, and infrastructure contractors are seeing input cost increases that are difficult to pass through on fixed-price contracts already signed.

**Automotive.** The 25% tariff on imported vehicles affects not just fully assembled imports but components. Modern vehicles contain components sourced from dozens of countries. Even US-assembled vehicles are exposed to tariff costs on their imported parts content.

## What supply chain diversification looks like in practice

The theory: shift sourcing from China and tariff-exposed origins to Vietnam, Mexico, India, or other lower-tariff countries. The reality: supplier qualification takes 12 to 24 months minimum. Factory audits, quality validation, capacity assessment, and contract negotiation do not happen in weeks. Companies that started this in 2023 are seeing benefit now. Companies starting in April 2025 are looking at 2026 at the earliest for material impact on their cost structure.

Mexico and Canada are complicated by the 20-25% tariffs that now apply to those origins as well. The tariff advantage over China remains significant on most categories, but the free-trade assumptions that governed sourcing decisions under USMCA are no longer fully intact.

## The ERP configuration gap

For organisations running SAP or any major ERP, the tariff changes create a configuration review requirement. The areas most at risk:

**Pricing conditions.** Pricing structures built on pre-tariff landed costs need to be updated. Selling prices that were set assuming a 0% or 7.5% tariff on a product category are now structurally wrong at 25% or 145%. SAP's condition technique in SD allows systematic price updates by material group and origin, but someone has to initiate the review.

**Vendor master and purchasing info records.** Suppliers that are being replaced or supplemented due to tariff exposure need to be set up and validated before they can be used in purchasing documents. Procurement teams that have been managing this in spreadsheets outside the ERP are creating reconciliation problems.

**Material cost estimates.** Standard cost rolls that were run in January or February are based on pre-tariff purchase prices. New cost estimates should be run against current purchasing conditions to give accurate margin visibility.

**Landed cost calculations.** For companies using SAP MM and logistics invoice verification, the landed cost model needs to reflect the new tariff rates by origin. This affects inventory valuation and the margin calculation on imported goods.

The businesses with real-time procurement analytics, properly configured product costing, and live supplier analytics in Ariba or an equivalent system can model the impact quickly and make sourcing decisions with current data. Businesses running on ERP systems that have been partially configured or maintained primarily in spreadsheets are in a worse position.

## The inflation and wage mismatch

Tariff-driven price increases compound with existing inflationary pressure. The 2021-2023 inflation cycle raised wage expectations. A second wave of price increases in 2025, driven by tariffs rather than demand, creates a situation where purchasing power continues to erode without a corresponding wage adjustment.

For businesses, the challenge is that their customers are also experiencing margin pressure. The ability to pass through tariff cost increases depends on whether competitors are equally exposed, whether substitute products are available, and whether demand is price-elastic enough to absorb the increase without volume loss.

Industries with limited substitution and inelastic demand (construction materials, certain industrial components, healthcare products) can pass through costs more easily than discretionary consumer categories where demand will simply shift or decline.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Is a tariff a tax?</summary>

Yes. A tariff is a tax collected by the importing country's government on goods crossing the border. It is charged as a percentage of the goods' value (an ad valorem tariff) or as a fixed amount per unit (a specific tariff).

The paying party is technically the importer, who pays the tax to customs when the goods enter the country. In practice, the cost is almost always passed forward through the supply chain and ultimately reaches the consumer in the form of higher prices. The government collects revenue. The end buyer pays more.

Tariffs are distinct from other trade barriers like quotas (which limit the quantity of imports) or embargoes (which prohibit imports entirely), but they are similar in effect: they raise the cost of imported goods relative to domestically produced alternatives.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why do businesses not just absorb tariff costs?</summary>

Because the margins do not support it in most cases. A 25% tariff on a product category where a business operates at 15% gross margin means absorbing the full tariff would make the business loss-making. The tariff increase has to come from somewhere.

The options: raise prices (passes the cost to customers), reduce costs elsewhere (compresses margins in other areas), source from lower-tariff origins (viable long-term, takes 12-24 months to implement), or exit the affected product category entirely.

Most businesses use a combination. Short-term: partial price increases. Medium-term: supplier diversification. Long-term: product mix adjustment to reduce exposure to high-tariff categories.

The businesses that absorb tariff costs entirely are usually in highly competitive markets where price increases would cause immediate demand loss. They end up with compressed margins, reduced investment capacity, and often workforce pressure as they try to cut costs elsewhere to compensate.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How should companies update their SAP systems for the 2025 tariffs?</summary>

Four areas require review.

Pricing conditions in SD. If selling prices are calculated from standard cost plus a markup, and the standard cost was set before the tariff changes, current selling prices may be unprofitable. Run a margin review by material group and origin country to identify where prices need to be updated.

Purchasing info records and material master data. Suppliers being replaced or added due to tariff exposure need to be created and maintained in the system before they can appear in purchase orders. Doing this outside the ERP in spreadsheets creates reconciliation problems that compound over time.

Standard cost rolls. The current standard costs for imported materials should reflect post-tariff purchase prices. If the last cost roll used pre-tariff rates, rerunning the cost estimate against current purchasing conditions will show the true margin impact.

Landed cost configuration. For businesses using SAP Global Trade Management or the landed cost functionality in MM, tariff rates by Harmonised System code and origin country need to be updated to reflect the 2025 schedule. Incorrect landed costs flow through to inventory valuation and distort margin calculations.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What industries are most affected by the 2025 tariffs?</summary>

Consumer electronics face the sharpest impact due to the 145% China tariff. Most consumer electronics manufacturing is concentrated in China, and meaningful supply chain diversification takes years to execute. Price increases in this category will be significant and relatively sustained.

Automotive is exposed through both fully assembled vehicle imports and component sourcing. Even US-assembled vehicles use imported parts that now carry tariff costs. Dealerships are facing inventory pricing decisions with imperfect information about their actual cost exposure.

Construction and industrial. Steel and aluminum tariffs have been elevated across multiple administrations. The 2025 structure extends and raises these, affecting everything from residential construction to industrial equipment manufacturing.

Agriculture is affected indirectly through retaliatory tariffs. US farm exports to China and the EU face higher barriers, which reduces demand and pressures farm incomes. The knock-on effect reaches farm equipment, fertiliser, and logistics sectors.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do tariffs affect companies with global supply chains?</summary>

Global supply chains are affected at multiple points simultaneously.

Inbound costs rise wherever tariff-exposed origins are part of the supply chain. A US manufacturer buying components from China, Germany, and Mexico is now facing tariff exposure on all three origins.

Outbound access is reduced where trading partners have implemented retaliatory tariffs on US exports. US exporters in agriculture, aerospace, and manufacturing are seeing market access costs increase.

Supply chain reconfiguration is underway. The response is to shift procurement toward lower-tariff origins and to qualify new suppliers in Vietnam, India, and domestically. This process is expensive and takes time.

For companies using SAP, the operational impact shows up in purchasing, logistics, and finance simultaneously. Purchase order values change as landed costs increase. Inventory valuation shifts as standard costs are updated. Customer pricing needs to be adjusted to reflect new margins. The organisations that have clean, well-maintained ERP data have a significant advantage in modelling and responding to these changes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can supply chains be quickly restructured to avoid tariffs?</summary>

No. Supply chain restructuring is a 12-to-24-month process at minimum. Supplier qualification requires factory audits, quality validation, capacity assessment, and contractual negotiation. New suppliers need to demonstrate consistent quality and delivery performance before they can take significant volume.

The practical constraint is also capacity. The manufacturers in Vietnam, India, and other lower-tariff origins that are positioned to absorb diverted China production are already being approached by multiple buyers simultaneously. Lead times have extended. Prices from these origins have already increased as demand has shifted toward them.

Mexico and Canada represent a partial exception for supply chains that were already integrated across North America under USMCA. The 20-25% tariffs on these origins are significant, but below the 145% China rate for most categories. Qualified North American suppliers are valuable, and companies that already have them face lower short-term disruption.

The companies best positioned to restructure quickly are those that had already started supplier diversification before April 2025, have established supplier relationships outside China, and have the ERP and procurement infrastructure to onboard and manage new supply chain partners efficiently.

</details>
