I worked with a sales team that was losing deals and did not know why. Their CRM was Salesforce. Their ERP was SAP. The integration was live and running. They thought they had real-time inventory visibility in Salesforce.

They did not. The integration ran batch sync every four hours. By mid-afternoon, the inventory numbers in Salesforce were from before lunch. Sales reps were quoting customers off outdated stock positions. Competitors were winning deals on availability commitments the sales team could not make because they did not know what was actually available.

The integration was technically functioning. It was commercially broken. That distinction is the starting point for understanding why these integrations fail.

## Why most SAP-Salesforce integrations break down

### Wrong sync frequency for the use case

Batch sync works for some data. It does not work for inventory availability, pricing, or order status in a business where customers expect real-time answers.

The failure pattern: the integration team builds what is technically feasible given the data volumes and system architecture. They set batch intervals based on technical parameters. Nobody on the business side validates whether that frequency supports the actual decision being made by the end user.

Before any integration is specified, the question is: what is the maximum age of data that makes this integration useful? For customer master updates, daily sync may be fine. For available-to-promise inventory in a B2B sales context, anything more than fifteen minutes is a problem.

### Data quality mismatches

An integration connects two systems. If the customer record in Salesforce has a different format for the company name than the customer master in SAP, every sync produces mismatches that require manual intervention.

I have seen integration projects where the first six weeks of operation were consumed entirely by customer record reconciliation. The integration was live. The data teams were spending three to four hours per day manually resolving sync failures. The source systems had been maintained independently for years and had diverged in ways that nobody had mapped before go-live.

Clean the data before you integrate. This sounds obvious. It is the most consistently skipped step in ERP-CRM integration projects.

### Wrong integration architecture for the scale

Point-to-point integration works for simple cases: two systems, a small number of data flows, stable business processes. It is the cheapest way to connect two systems.

It does not scale. When the ERP adds a new module, point-to-point connections multiply. The same happens when Salesforce is extended for a new business unit, or a third system needs to participate in the same data flows. They become unmanageable. Each additional connection is another interface to maintain, test, and troubleshoot independently.

Middleware-based integration, using SAP CPI, MuleSoft, Dell Boomi, or an equivalent, creates a managed integration layer. Data flows are governed through one platform. New connections are added within the same governance framework. Monitoring and error handling are centralised.

The trade-off: middleware requires upfront investment in architecture and governance. Companies that deploy middleware but treat it as infrastructure with no named ownership end up with the same problems as point-to-point, plus a middleware platform they do not understand.

### No integration governance

Every Salesforce seasonal release and every SAP support package is a potential integration break. API changes, new validation rules, changed field mappings, updated authentication requirements: any of these can break a flow that was working last month.

Integrations that are built and forgotten fail on the first system upgrade that no one thought to regression test against the integration layer. This is the most common post-go-live failure mode I encounter.

Integration governance means a few things. A named owner for each integration flow. A regression test suite that runs before any system upgrade. A monitoring setup that alerts on failures rather than reporting them after users have complained. A documented understanding of what happens when a sync fails.

## The SAP-Salesforce integration options

### SAP CPI (Cloud Platform Integration)

For SAP-centric landscapes, SAP CPI on BTP is the natural choice. It has pre-built connectors for Salesforce and for the main SAP modules (SD, MM, FI). It runs in the SAP cloud, which simplifies the authentication and security model for SAP-to-SAP and SAP-to-Salesforce flows.

SAP CPI is not plug-and-play for complex scenarios. The standard connectors handle the common flows, customer master synchronisation, order status updates, quote-to-order hand-off. Custom business processes require custom iFlows, which need integration development skills.

### MuleSoft

Salesforce acquired MuleSoft in 2018. It has the broadest ecosystem of pre-built connectors and a mature platform for complex integration architectures. For organisations that already own MuleSoft, adding SAP connectors is straightforward.

The disadvantage for SAP-heavy organisations: MuleSoft expertise is a separate skillset from SAP expertise. The team that runs your SAP implementation is probably not the team that should be designing your MuleSoft architecture.

### Dell Boomi

Dell Boomi is a cloud-native iPaaS platform with SAP and Salesforce connectors and a lower entry-level configuration barrier than MuleSoft. It is a reasonable choice for mid-market organisations that want middleware governance without MuleSoft's licensing cost or architectural complexity.

### Point-to-point via REST/SOAP APIs

For organisations with specific, stable integration requirements and the in-house technical capability to manage it, a direct API integration between Salesforce and SAP avoids middleware overhead. It requires disciplined API versioning, regression testing on every release, and a team that understands both systems' API models.

This is appropriate for simple, stable integrations. It is a technical debt accumulator for anything complex.

## What a reliable integration needs

**A clear specification of every data flow.** What fields sync. In which direction. At what frequency. What the primary key mapping is between systems. What happens when the data in one system conflicts with the data in the other.

**An explicit failure design.** Most integrations are designed for the success path. When a sync fails, what happens to the in-flight data? Does it retry? How many times? Does it alert? Who receives the alert? What is the manual recovery process? The failure path is where most integrations are underdesigned.

**Regression testing before every upgrade.** Every Salesforce release (three per year) and every SAP support package should trigger an integration regression test run. This does not need to be manual. An automated test suite that validates the critical flows can run in hours.

**Monitoring with alerts.** An integration that fails silently is worse than one that fails visibly. Errors that accumulate over days before someone notices them are harder to resolve than errors caught within minutes of occurrence.

**Ownership.** Someone is accountable for the integration layer. They know what it does, they know when it breaks, and they have the access and the authority to fix it.

## Common failure scenarios and what to do

**Customer records don't match.** Root cause: Salesforce and SAP customer masters were not aligned before integration. Fix: run a data reconciliation exercise before go-live, establish a master system of record for customer data, and configure the integration to enforce the mapping.

**Order status not updating in Salesforce.** Root cause: the integration covers quote-to-order hand-off but not order status callbacks. Fix: extend the integration to include status updates from SAP SD back to Salesforce at each order milestone.

**Pricing differences between Salesforce quotes and SAP invoices.** Root cause: pricing in Salesforce is maintained manually and diverges from the pricing conditions in SAP. Fix: make SAP the master for pricing and pull prices into Salesforce via the integration rather than maintaining them separately.

**Integration breaks after SAP upgrade.** Root cause: no regression testing on the integration layer during upgrade planning. Fix: include integration regression testing in every SAP upgrade project scope.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Can Salesforce integrate with SAP?</summary>

Yes. SAP and Salesforce can be integrated using several methods: SAP CPI (Cloud Platform Integration) on SAP BTP, MuleSoft, Dell Boomi, direct REST or SOAP API connections, and other iPaaS platforms.

The integration typically covers the following data flows: customer master synchronisation (keeping customer records aligned between the two systems), quote-to-order hand-off (moving a won opportunity in Salesforce to a sales order in SAP SD), order status updates (feeding SAP order and delivery status back to Salesforce), pricing synchronisation (pulling SAP pricing conditions into Salesforce for quote generation), and invoice and payment status updates.

The complexity of the integration depends on the business processes, the number of entities and company codes in scope, the data volume, and the real-time requirements. A simple customer master sync is a standard configuration task. A full quote-to-cash integration with complex pricing logic, multi-entity accounting, and real-time inventory availability is a significant integration project.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is Salesforce an ERP or a CRM?</summary>

Salesforce is a CRM. It manages customer relationships: sales pipeline, opportunity tracking, customer interactions, marketing automation, and service management. It is not a financial system, does not manage inventory, and does not post accounting entries.

SAP is an ERP. It manages the back-office processes: financial accounting, procurement, inventory, production, human capital management. It is not designed for managing sales pipelines or customer relationship workflows.

The integration between the two systems connects the front-office (Salesforce, customer-facing) with the back-office (SAP, operations and finance). Done well, a sales rep can see stock availability and customer payment status in Salesforce. A finance team can see order value and deal stage in SAP. Done poorly, the two systems hold different versions of the same customer information and nobody trusts either.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the main reason ERP-CRM integrations fail?</summary>

The most consistent failure cause is misaligned expectations about what the integration actually needs to do, discovered after it has been built.

The integration team specifies technical requirements: which fields sync, in which direction, at what frequency. The business team reviews the specification at a conceptual level. Nobody maps the specification against the actual decisions being made by end users. Go-live happens. Users start working with the integration. They discover that the inventory numbers are four hours old, or that the pricing in Salesforce does not match the invoice, or that order status updates are delayed.

These are not technical failures. They are requirement definition failures. The integration was built correctly to the specification. The specification was wrong.

The fix: before writing a technical specification, document the specific decisions each user group is trying to make using the integrated data, and what data currency (freshness) those decisions require. Build the specification from the user's decision, not from the technical capability of the systems.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you maintain an ERP-Salesforce integration over time?</summary>

Three practices account for most of the maintenance work.

Regression testing before system upgrades. Salesforce releases three major updates per year. SAP releases support packages and updates on a varying schedule. Each update is a potential integration break. An automated regression test suite that validates the critical integration flows should run before any upgrade in either system. Finding breaks in testing is cheap. Finding them in production is expensive.

Monitoring and alerting. Integration errors that are caught immediately can be resolved before they affect users. Integration errors that accumulate silently for days create data reconciliation problems that require significant effort to untangle. Set up automated monitoring on all critical integration flows with alerts that fire on failure, not on end-of-day reporting.

Ownership. Assign a named owner for the integration layer. That person understands what the integration does, has access to the monitoring platform, knows the escalation path when something breaks, and is included in change management planning for both Salesforce and SAP upgrades.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is SAP CPI and when should you use it for Salesforce integration?</summary>

SAP CPI (Cloud Platform Integration) is SAP's integration middleware platform, part of SAP BTP. It provides pre-built connectors for Salesforce and for SAP modules, a graphical interface for building integration flows (iFlows), and monitoring tools for managing the integration landscape.

SAP CPI makes the most sense when SAP is the dominant system in the landscape, when the organisation has SAP BTP access (which comes with most RISE with SAP subscriptions), and when the integration requirements include SAP-specific content like IDoc processing, BAPI calls, or SAP-format message structures.

It is not the right choice when the organisation has already invested heavily in a non-SAP middleware platform like MuleSoft or Boomi, when the team does not have SAP integration developer skills, or when the integration requirements are primarily non-SAP to non-SAP and SAP is a peripheral participant.

For new RISE with SAP implementations where Salesforce integration is in scope, SAP CPI is the natural starting point. It is included in the BTP entitlement and reduces the number of platforms to maintain.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long does an ERP-Salesforce integration project take?</summary>

Timeline varies significantly by scope and complexity.

A standard integration covering customer master sync, quote-to-order hand-off, and basic order status updates, using a pre-built connector like SAP CPI's Salesforce integration, typically takes 8 to 16 weeks from scoping to go-live. This assumes clean source data and a dedicated integration developer.

A full quote-to-cash integration including complex pricing, multi-entity accounting, credit management, and real-time inventory availability typically takes 4 to 9 months, depending on the complexity of the SAP configuration and the Salesforce customisation.

The most consistent cause of overruns: data quality issues discovered mid-project that were not identified in the initial assessment, and scope additions that come in during build when business users see what the integration can do and want to add more flows.

Budget a data quality assessment before the integration build begins. It is the most reliable way to avoid the reconciliation delays that consume the first weeks of every integration project.

</details>
