On a Singapore consumer goods rollout in 2023, the team rushed through testing to hit deadlines. User acceptance testing barely happened. Executives pushed for the launch anyway.

Within days of go-live: missing setups, broken workflows, data completely wrong.

The problems were not surprises. They were visible before go-live. Nobody stopped to check. The weeks they saved cost three months of recovery.

That is what happens without quality gates. Across 21 SAP programmes that I have led, rescued, or watched go sideways, the programmes that held their schedules and budgets all enforced quality gates as hard stops, not negotiable status updates.

## What quality gates actually are

A quality gate is a formal checkpoint between project phases. Before the team progresses, they must demonstrate that specific criteria have been met. Pass the gate, move forward. Fail the gate, fix the problems first.

In SAP Activate, the framework defines six phases: Discover, Prepare, Explore, Realize, Deploy, and Run. Each phase transition is a natural gate point. The entry criteria define what must be true before the phase begins. The exit criteria define what must be proven before the phase closes.

Quality gates are not progress reviews. They are not status meetings. They are decision points with defined standards, documented sign-off, and real authority to delay the project when standards are not met.

Without them, reviews become rubber stamps. And rubber-stamp quality gates are worse than no quality gates. They create false confidence while real problems accumulate.

## The SAP Activate quality gate framework

| Phase | Quality focus | Key deliverables | Approval criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discover | Business case validation, executive alignment | Business case document, high-level roadmap | Signed-off business case, executive buy-in confirmed |
| Prepare | Governance setup, resource allocation, risk assessment | Project charter, governance framework, risk register | Project charter approved, risk mitigation plan agreed, team onboarded |
| Explore | Fit-gap analysis, process design, backlog prioritisation | Fit-gap report, process diagrams, integration architecture | Approved fit-gap report, validated integration approach, process owners signed off |
| Realize | Configuration validation, integration testing | Configured system, test cases, defect resolution reports | Unit and integration tests passed, critical defects resolved, UAT sign-off |
| Deploy | Data migration validation, cutover readiness | Data migration reports, training completion logs, cutover plan | Migration testing successful, training validated, go-live checklist completed |
| Run | Post-go-live monitoring, system stabilisation | Performance reports, issue logs, improvement roadmap | Stable performance, minimal high-priority incidents, operational handover complete |

Entry and exit criteria for every gate should be agreed and documented in the project charter before the project starts. Defining criteria under deadline pressure, mid-project, produces criteria shaped by what the team can currently demonstrate, not by what the project actually requires.

## What a quality gate needs to work

### Specific, measurable criteria

"Testing complete" is not a quality gate criterion. It generates arguments.

"95% of test cases executed, all Priority 1 defects resolved, no open Priority 2 defects older than five days" is a criterion. It generates answers.

On a European manufacturing programme in 2024, I worked with a client whose quality gate criteria were too vague. Nobody knew whether they had actually passed or not. Every phase transition turned into a negotiation. When we replaced the criteria with measurable thresholds, the arguments stopped. The gates worked.

### Named ownership

Every gate needs a named owner with the authority to delay the project. Not a committee. One person.

On a GCC retail programme in 2023, I watched a project crash because nobody had the power to say "we are not ready". The project manager wanted to delay. The programme director was under pressure from the business. Nobody had been given a formal mandate to hold the gate. The project moved forward unprepared.

The gate owner's job is not to slow things down. It is to protect the project from expensive mistakes. That job requires real authority, documented in the project charter.

### Executive backing before the pressure hits

Executives support quality gates right up until they create a deadline conflict. Then the instinct is to override.

On a European retail programme in 2024, the CIO overruled a failed quality gate to meet a quarterly target. The resulting go-live issues cost twice what the delay would have. I have seen this pattern enough times that I now make executive sign-off on the gate framework a condition of engagement. If leadership is not committed to honouring the gates under pressure, the gates will not hold when it matters.

### Documented evidence

Gate decisions need supporting documentation. Test execution reports, defect logs, process sign-off sheets, data migration validation results.

On a GCC retail programme in 2024, the client's Solution Manager reporting showed that 40% of their "completed" test cases had not actually been executed. They discovered this before the quality gate, not after. The documentation discipline caught the gap. Without it, they would have proceeded on a false assumption.

## How RISE, clean-core, and AI change quality gates in 2026

The SAP Activate gate framework still works. RISE with SAP and the 2024-2025 platform changes added three considerations that gate design needs to absorb.

### Clean-core compliance becomes a gate criterion

Clean-core compliance is mandatory across SAP Cloud editions since 2024-2025. Every customisation must live on SAP BTP, not as in-system modification. The implication for quality gates: clean-core compliance is now a measurable gate criterion that did not exist on pre-2024 programmes.

Add to the Explore gate: every identified customisation has a documented decision on whether it can be configured (no extension), built as a BTP extension (clean-core compliant), or rejected. Add to the Realize gate: 100% of customisations live on SAP BTP under clean-core principles, with no in-system modifications introduced during configuration.

For on-premise programmes where clean-core is recommended but not enforced, the gate criterion should still apply as a voluntary discipline. The technical debt that builds when clean-core is bypassed surfaces at the first major upgrade.

### RISE adds an SAP customer success engagement gate

Under RISE with SAP, SAP holds infrastructure responsibility and runs a customer success cadence. The Prepare phase gate should include confirmation that the SAP customer success contact is named, the escalation path into SAP is documented, and the customer success engagement cadence is agreed. Without this, platform issues that should escalate to SAP get held inside the project team for too long.

### Joule for SAP Activate accelerates gate evidence preparation

The 2024-2025 AI rollout reached gate preparation work. Joule for SAP Activate drafts test execution summaries, defect resolution reports, and fit-gap status updates from source SAP Cloud ALM data. Microsoft Copilot drafts steering committee gate review packs from prepared evidence.

The honest assessment: AI accelerates the production of gate evidence by 30-50% on programmes with clean source data. It does not change the standard required to pass the gate. The evidence still has to meet the criteria; AI just makes producing the evidence faster.

### SAP Cloud ALM is the 2026 default tool

For new programmes in 2026 on RISE with SAP, GROW with SAP, or any S/4HANA Cloud variant, SAP Cloud ALM is the default gate management tool. It is included free with most SAP cloud subscriptions and provides phase checklists, quality metrics, and approval workflows natively.

SAP Solution Manager remains functional for on-premise programmes through 2027 (end-of-mainstream-maintenance was 2025, extended support to 2027). For programmes already on Solution Manager, continuing through delivery is appropriate; new programmes should plan around Cloud ALM.

## Step-by-step setup

### 1. Align gates to SAP Activate phases

Map your quality gates to the natural phase transitions in your implementation methodology. For SAP Activate: after Prepare, after Explore, after Realize, and after Deploy.

Do not create arbitrary checkpoints between phases. Gates at natural transition points carry more weight. They align with the project's momentum and give teams a clear target to build toward.

### 2. Define entry and exit criteria before the project starts

Work with business leads, technical leads, and executive sponsors to define the criteria before configuration begins. What test coverage percentage is required? What defect thresholds are acceptable? Which business process owners must sign off? For RISE programmes, what clean-core compliance threshold applies?

Write these down. Include them in the project charter. Have them signed by sponsors. When deadline pressure hits, you need documented standards, not verbal agreements made under stress.

### 3. Build gates into the project schedule with buffer

Each quality gate needs time in the project plan, not just as a milestone but as an activity. Include buffer before the gate for issue resolution. On a European retail programme in 2024, the client allocated a full week before each gate purely for cleanup. That buffer saved them from three compressed test cycles.

### 4. Assign reviewers with decision authority

Gate reviews need the right people. Business leads who can sign off on process. Technical leads who can sign off on configuration and integration. And a gate owner with authority to delay.

Do not let IT sign off on business process acceptance. Do not let consultants sign off for the business. The sign-off must come from the people who will live with the outcome.

### 5. Store evidence centrally

Whether you use SAP Cloud ALM, SAP Solution Manager, Jira, or another tool, all gate evidence should live in one place. When an auditor asks six months later who signed off on the data migration validation, the answer should be retrievable in under five minutes.

## Common failures

### Skipping gates under schedule pressure

Every time a gate gets skipped to hit a deadline, the problems that gate would have caught get deferred to go-live. At go-live, fixing them costs three to five times more. The time saved is borrowed at a high interest rate.

The pattern repeats so consistently that I treat it as a go-live risk on sight. When a project accelerates into the Deploy phase without evidence of a proper Realize gate, that risk applies regardless of what the status report says.

### Vague criteria

Ambiguous statements like "UAT completed" or "configuration done" create arguments. Half the team thinks "UAT completed" means all tests executed. The other half thinks it means all defects fixed. When the gate review happens, no one agrees on whether it has been passed.

Define criteria in language that produces a binary answer. Either the threshold has been met or it has not.

### Resource constraints

When key reviewers are spread across multiple projects, gate reviews become superficial. I have seen gate reviews conducted in thirty minutes with three people who had also attended four other meetings that day. They were checking boxes, not reviewing work.

Quality gate reviews are not quick. A Realize gate review for a mid-size SAP implementation should take at least half a day, with prepared evidence reviewed in advance. Build that time into the project plan.

### Cultural resistance

Teams used to rushing through milestones resent quality gates. The instinct is to find ways around them rather than through them.

Changing this is harder than implementing the technical framework. It starts with executive tone at kickoff: quality gates are mandatory, they protect the project, and bypassing them is not an option. When an executive communicates this at the start and demonstrates it by refusing to override the first gate that causes a delay, the culture shifts.

## Tooling and measurement

### Tooling for quality gate management

| Tool | Role in gate management | SAP integration | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| SAP Cloud ALM | Phase checklists, task management, quality metrics | Native | RISE and cloud S/4HANA programmes (2026 default) |
| SAP Solution Manager | Native gate tracking, KPI dashboards, defect workflows | Native | On-premise programmes still running it (extended support through 2027) |
| Jira and Confluence (with Atlassian Intelligence) | Issue tracking and gate evidence documentation | API-based | Agile-delivery teams with existing Atlassian tooling |
| Azure DevOps | Pipeline approvals, traceability, gate workflows | Custom via APIs | Microsoft-stack shops with DevOps-led delivery |
| Tricentis Tosca / NeoLoad | Test coverage enforcement for gate criteria | Certified SAP support | Programmes with high automation and test coverage requirements |
| ServiceNow | Approval workflows, audit-ready sign-off | Via adapters | Organisations already running ServiceNow for ITSM |
| Micro Focus ALM | Requirements traceability, defect governance | Via middleware | Larger programmes in regulated industries needing full audit trails |

The tool matters less than the discipline. I have seen quality gates work on SharePoint with a well-structured approval list. I have seen them fail with full Solution Manager implementation because nobody had been trained to use it and the team kept parallel spreadsheets.

Whatever tool is chosen, it needs to be the single source of truth. Parallel tracking in spreadsheets undermines the gate, because the spreadsheet always shows the version the team wants to show.

On a European consumer goods programme in 2024, the client used Jira with Atlassian Intelligence overlays for a global rollout and maintained clear gate status dashboards across all workstreams. The AI overlay automated summary generation for steering committee gate reviews, which freed up the project office to focus on issue resolution rather than report production.

### Measuring whether gates are working

Three metrics indicate gate health.

**Defect leakage rate.** The percentage of defects found after a gate that should have been caught before it. A high leakage rate means the gate criteria were too loose or the reviews were too superficial.

**Quality gate pass rate on first attempt.** If every gate passes on the first attempt, the criteria are probably not strict enough. Some first-attempt failures are healthy; they show the gates are catching real problems. A pass rate of 100% should prompt a review of whether the criteria have any teeth.

**Go-live incident rate.** The volume of high-priority incidents in the first thirty days post-go-live is a retrospective measure of gate effectiveness. A clean go-live is the result of gates that worked. A chaotic go-live is evidence that problems cleared gates they should not have.

These three numbers tell you whether quality gates are protecting the project or providing the appearance of protection.

<summary>What should quality gate criteria include?</summary>

Quality gate criteria must be specific enough to produce a binary answer: met or not met. Criteria that require interpretation are not criteria. They are conversation starters.

For the Realize gate, criteria might include: test case execution coverage above a defined threshold, maximum number of open defects by severity, all critical business processes signed off by named process owners, integration tests completed with documented results, performance test results against defined SLA thresholds, and clean-core compliance rate for any customisations built.

For the Deploy gate: data migration dry run completed with reconciliation sign-off, training completion rates by role, cutover plan approved, go-live checklist completed, hypercare resource plan confirmed.

Every criterion should have an owner responsible for producing evidence, a reviewer responsible for verifying it, and a gate owner with authority to rule on whether the criterion has been met. Criteria without ownership do not get measured.

Document all gate criteria in the project charter before configuration starts. Criteria defined mid-project are often shaped by what the team can demonstrate at that moment rather than by what the project genuinely requires.

<details>
<summary>Why do quality gates fail in SAP implementations?</summary>

Four failure patterns account for most quality gate problems.

Vague criteria. When exit criteria are ambiguous, gate reviews become negotiations. Teams argue over what "done" means rather than presenting evidence against a defined standard. Specific, measurable criteria eliminate the argument.

Rubber-stamp reviews. When gate reviews are scheduled for thirty minutes with no prepared evidence, they produce approvals without examination. A Realize gate review for a mid-size implementation should take at least half a day, with supporting documentation reviewed in advance.

Executive override. Executives support quality gates until the first one conflicts with a deadline. At that point, the instinct is to override. Every override communicates that gates are optional when inconvenient. The project pays for this at go-live.

Resource constraints. When key reviewers are split across multiple projects, they attend gate reviews without adequate preparation time. Reviews become superficial and sign-offs are given on incomplete evidence.

The root cause across all four patterns is that quality gates are treated as process overhead rather than project protection. Teams that understand what a failed go-live costs are much more disciplined about gate enforcement than teams that only see the time cost of doing the review properly.

<details>
<summary>How should go-live readiness be assessed at the final quality gate?</summary>

The Deploy phase gate is the final checkpoint before cutover begins. It should answer one question: is the team ready to run production on the new system, and are the contingency plans in place if something goes wrong?

The evidence required at this gate covers several areas. Data migration: dry run completed, reconciliation reports reviewed and signed off by the data lead, exception handling confirmed for open items. Training: completion rates by role at or above the defined threshold, knowledge assessment scores for critical roles, training materials signed off. Cutover: documented cutover plan with step-by-step sequence, timing, and decision points; rehearsal completed; rollback plan documented and tested. Hypercare: named support team confirmed and available for the first thirty days; escalation paths documented; incident severity classification agreed.

For RISE programmes, add: SAP customer success engagement confirmed for the hypercare period; clean-core compliance verified across all customisations.

The gate owner should be someone with authority to delay go-live. That authority must be established before the gate, not asserted for the first time at it. If the gate reveals that data migration reconciliation has unresolved exceptions or that a critical user population has not completed training, the decision to proceed anyway must be a conscious, documented business decision with named sign-off, not a default because nobody wants to delay.

Go-live readiness is not a feeling. It is a checklist with evidence against each item.

The best SAP quality gates in 2026 are not the most elaborate framework or the most automated tool. They are the gates whose exit criteria were defined in the project charter before configuration started, whose owners had documented authority to delay the project, whose evidence lived in a single source of truth (SAP Cloud ALM for new cloud programmes), and whose first failed gate was actually allowed to fail. Most quality gate frameworks collapse at the first deadline conflict because the executive override expectation was set during a calm moment and broken during a pressure moment. Build for the pressure moment from the start.
