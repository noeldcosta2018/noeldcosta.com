Citizens had started expecting public services to work like online banking. Log in once, see your request, and get updates without chasing multiple offices. The government agency we worked with heard this consistently in feedback, not just complaints about speed, but about not knowing anything at all.

Their legacy systems could not deliver transparency. Different departments held separate records. Every counter asked the same questions. Citizens gave up chasing and staff spent their days re-entering data they'd already received somewhere else.

SAP CX gave us the chance to redesign engagement from the ground up.

## The four modules and what each one actually solved

We chose different parts of the SAP CX portfolio, but with specific jobs for each. This was not a platform decision: it was four targeted answers to four real problems.

| Module | Problem it solved | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| Service Cloud | Cases scattered across departments, no shared view | One case view for staff; trackable status for citizens |
| Customer Data Cloud | Multiple logins per service, consent invisible | Single login; citizens control their own data settings |
| Marketing Cloud (Emarsys) | Citizens chased services; government never reached out | Proactive reminders for renewals, fees, deadlines |
| Commerce Cloud | In-person payments only; reconciliation done by hand | Online payments; instant receipts; ERP updated in real time |

### SAP Service Cloud

Service Cloud became the workhorse. Every channel (web, phone, in-person counter) fed into one case view. A case worker could finally see that the same citizen who just applied for a parking permit had also called last week about a tax question. Before, those details sat in different systems and nobody connected them.

We set service level agreements inside the system. That forced accountability. Citizens started noticing when timelines were visible and realistic: that visibility alone reduced repeat calls.

AI chatbots played a small role, but we were deliberate about scope. One citizen told us it was a relief to get quick answers about opening hours without holding. Then she said: "If I have a dispute, I want a person." We agreed. Chatbots for the basics; people for everything else.

### SAP Customer Data Cloud

Multiple logins were a persistent complaint. People had one account for permits, another for taxes, another for business licensing. CDC replaced all of that with one secure digital identity.

Citizens stopped juggling credentials (and, just as importantly) could finally see their own consent settings. During a feedback session, a local business owner said: "At least now I know where my data goes. Before, it felt like I was signing blind." That kind of trust does not come from features. It comes from giving people control.

### SAP Marketing Cloud (Emarsys)

This was as much a culture change as a technology one. We moved from waiting for citizens to chase us, to reaching out proactively. Renewal reminders, tax notices, deadline alerts sent directly.

The key was restraint. Too many messages and people stop reading. We focused on relevance: childcare fee deadlines for families, pension notices for retirees. A local business owner said he'd stopped worrying about permit renewals because Emarsys reminded him before he forgot. That is the shift.

### SAP Commerce Cloud

Payments used to require in-person visits. People queued, paid cash, and hoped the receipt matched their record. Commerce Cloud moved everything online: applications, fees, confirmations in a single session.

For finance staff, it meant no more reconciling by hand at the end of the week. Receipts went out instantly. Citizens trusted the process because the paper trail appeared immediately.

## The test run moment

Early in the rollout, we ran a pilot on business permit applications. A citizen submitted a request and saw "in progress" on their portal, but the staff dashboard showed "waiting for approval." That small mismatch (the same request, two different statuses) created new inbound calls instead of fewer.

We fixed it by making sure both sides saw exactly the same data, pulling from the same Service Cloud record. That fix, more than anything else, is where we saw the first drop in call volume.

Citizens value clarity above speed. When you give them the same view as your own staff, you stop generating the calls that trust deficits create.

## Back-office integration via SAP BTP

The front-end work was only half the story. Citizens see the portal. They do not see the handoffs between Service Cloud and ERP, but they feel it when those handoffs break.

Before integration, a citizen could apply for a building permit online and staff would still push the request into ERP by hand. Payments came in digitally but were reconciled against finance records at week's end. Social services ran their own case management with no visibility to other departments.

SAP BTP became the glue. Once we connected Service Cloud to S/4HANA and the older platforms:

1. Permit requests triggered workflows directly inside ERP
2. Payments through Commerce Cloud synced in real time with the finance ledger
3. Social service case statuses became visible across departments, within privacy guardrails

One case worker told me: "For the first time, I can see the full picture of a citizen's case without digging through four systems." That shift from data entry to resolving exceptions changed the quality of service.

## The 360 citizen view

"360 citizen view" sounded like jargon at the start. To staff, it meant one thing: stop asking people to tell their story five times.

We pulled IDs, transactions, service requests, and communication logs into one profile. When someone applied for a housing grant, the case worker could also see their ongoing medical claim and a recent conversation with the tax office. The agency started acting like one organisation instead of scattered departments.

Data cleaning was slow and at times boring, but we'd seen what happens when migration skips it: duplicate records, mismatched histories, a 360 view that lies. The investment paid off. When the errors dropped, trust went up.

## Outcomes

| Area | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Permit processing | 6 weeks | 10 days |
| Overall processing times | Baseline | ~50% reduction |
| Online requests | Minimal | 65% in year 1 |
| Staff data entry | Cross-system manual re-entry | Unified workflow; exception handling only |
| Payment reconciliation | Weekly manual spreadsheet | Real-time ERP sync |
| Citizen call volume | High: status chasers | Fell after shared-status fix |

The impact was clear almost immediately. Citizens stopped asking "Where is my request?" because they could see it themselves. Staff stopped re-entering data and started solving actual cases. Confidence grew on both sides.

## Lessons for other public sector programmes

**Omnichannel does not mean all channels at once.** Older residents preferred web or phone over mobile. We designed continuity: start online, switch to a call, staff pick up where the citizen left off. That option mattered more than forcing everyone onto the app.

**Automate the simple cases first.** Staff were cautious about losing control on complex cases. We started with renewals: rules were clear, risk was low. That phased approach built internal confidence before we touched anything difficult.

**Data governance before integration.** If your records are duplicated or inconsistent, a 360 view will surface those problems in front of citizens and staff simultaneously. Clean first, integrate second.

**Relevance beats volume in communications.** Emarsys was most effective when messages were narrow and timely. Blasting every channel with every update reversed the trust gains quickly. Restraint built credibility.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Which SAP CX modules work best for public sector citizen engagement?</summary>

The combination that delivered the most value here was Service Cloud for case management, Customer Data Cloud for unified identity and consent, Emarsys for proactive outreach, and Commerce Cloud for payments.

Service Cloud is usually the starting point: it gives staff a single case view and citizens a trackable status. CDC matters if citizens deal with multiple departments and have fragmented logins. The sequence matters: start with the case layer, then add identity, then proactive comms and payments.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long does it take to implement SAP CX for a government agency?</summary>

For this programme, the initial Service Cloud rollout took around six months from scoping to go-live. Adding CDC, Emarsys, and Commerce Cloud extended the total to roughly 18 months for all four modules.

The biggest variable is data readiness. If citizen records are fragmented or duplicated across departments, data cleaning adds weeks before integration can begin. Programmes that skip this step usually pay for it post-go-live in mismatched statuses and reconciliation failures.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between SAP Service Cloud and a standard CRM for public sector use?</summary>

A standard CRM is built around commercial relationships: leads, pipelines, customers. Service Cloud is built around case management and service resolution. For public sector use, the relevant features are SLA tracking, channel unification (web, phone, counter), and case visibility across departments.

The key difference in a government context is the integration with back-office ERP and compliance systems. Service Cloud connects to SAP BTP and S/4HANA, which means permit requests can trigger ERP workflows without manual handoffs. A generic CRM does not have that integration path.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you measure success in a citizen engagement programme?</summary>

The KPIs that mattered most on this programme: time to resolution per case type, first contact resolution rate, digital adoption percentage by service, CSAT or NPS from citizen surveys, cost-to-serve per transaction, and support ticket volume month-on-month.

The vanity metric to avoid: number of citizens registered on the portal. Registration does not mean engagement. Track completed transactions and whether call volume actually dropped. If people register but still call to check status, the transparency problem has not been solved.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What role does SAP BTP play in citizen-facing services?</summary>

SAP BTP is the integration layer. Citizens do not see it, but they feel it when it is missing. Without BTP, the front-end portal and the back-office ERP operate in silos: staff receive a digital request and then re-enter it manually into finance or case management systems.

With BTP connecting Service Cloud to S/4HANA, permit requests trigger ERP workflows automatically, payments sync in real time, and case data flows between departments within privacy rules. The experience works end-to-end for both citizens and staff because the data moves without anyone carrying it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you handle privacy and consent in a public sector SAP CX rollout?</summary>

Customer Data Cloud handles this directly. It provides a unified digital identity with consent settings that citizens can view and change themselves. Every data use is tied back to explicit consent rather than buried in terms and conditions.

In practice, this meant citizens could see what information each department accessed and adjust their preferences. The local business owner who said "I finally know where my data goes" was not unusual. That sense of control was a recurring theme in feedback sessions. Making consent transparent is also a compliance requirement in most EU jurisdictions, so CDC serves both the citizen experience and the legal obligation simultaneously.

</details>
