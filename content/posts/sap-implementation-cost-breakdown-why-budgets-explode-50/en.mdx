SAP implementations go over budget mainly because of four things: services that were under-scoped, change requests that compound, data migration that runs long, and internal teams stretched past what they can absorb. In the programmes I have reviewed, overruns of 40-60% are common. I have seen mid-sized companies go from a $4 million plan to over $7 million by go-live.

This breakdown is for CFOs approving the numbers and programme leads expected to deliver against them. It covers where the money goes, cost ranges by size and region, a budget formula you can use before detailed scoping, what you can capitalise, and the four patterns behind most large overruns.

For context, survey averages look calmer. Panorama Consulting's [2026 ERP Report](https://www.panorama-consulting.com/panorama-consulting-group-releases-latest-study-of-erp-implementation-outcomes-across-the-globe/) found that more than a quarter of organisations exceeded their ERP budgets. Survey averages and the programmes that end up in front of an adviser are different populations. Plan for the second.

## Where the money actually goes

Licensing is usually the starting point and rarely the final story.

| Cost category | What it covers | Typical share of budget |
|---|---|---|
| Software licence or subscription | Core modules and users (on-premise) or RISE / SAP GROW subscription | 15-25% |
| Implementation services | Consultants, partners, configuration, project management | 30-45% |
| Internal project team | IT, subject matter experts, business leads (their time) | 10-20% |
| Data migration | Mapping, validation, conversion | 5-10% |
| Infrastructure | Servers, hosting, network (smaller under RISE) | 5-10% |
| Training and change management | Workshops, materials, onboarding | 3-7% |
| Testing and QA | Integration testing, UAT, defect resolution | 2-5% |
| Post-go-live support | Hypercare, fixes, stabilisation | 2-5% |
| Contingency | Reserve for unplanned issues | 5-10% |

The table hides the biggest invisible cost. Your best people will be half or more committed to the programme for months, and the business slows around go-live while they learn the new system. Neither appears in a vendor quote.

## Cost by business size

These ranges are the ones I use for S/4HANA programmes, cloud or on-premise, and they match the figures in my other cost articles. Scope, industry and partner move them a lot. Use them as anchors, not quotes. If you want a first estimate for your own scope, the [SAP implementation cost calculator](/sap-implementation-cost-calculator/) uses the same logic.

| Segment | Typical total cost (USD) | Primary cost drivers |
|---|---|---|
| Small business with SAP Business One | $250K-$1.5M | Basic modules, fewer users, limited integrations |
| Mid-market S/4HANA (200-1,000 employees) | $2M-$10M | Multiple modules, some custom development, data migration |
| Enterprise S/4HANA (1,000+ employees) | $20M-$80M+ | Multi-entity, complex integrations, multi-language |
| Global multi-region S/4HANA | $80M-$300M+ | Cross-region scope, finance harmonisation |
| Manufacturing (discrete/process) | $10M-$50M | Shop floor integration, MRP, plant-level logic |
| Retail and consumer goods | $5M-$30M | POS integration, pricing, omnichannel |
| Life sciences / pharma | $15M-$60M | GxP compliance, serialisation, batch traceability |
| Public sector | $8M-$40M | Tender-based scoping, grants, audit trails, data residency |

What that looks like in practice. A manufacturing client of mine with 70-80 employees paid $332,000 all-in for SAP Business One, about $4,700 per user. A distribution company I worked with (380 employees) spent $2.9 million on its implementation, about $7,600 per user. An enterprise client with 3,500 employees spent $18.2 million, which works out to $5,200 per user. Enterprise rollouts get expensive, but they cost less per user: the economies of scale kick in.

### Regional day rates

Geography moves the budget more than most CFOs expect.

| Region | Senior practitioner day rate (USD) | What drives it |
|---|---|---|
| North America | $1,800-$3,500 | Labour rates, regulatory depth, demand for senior people |
| Western Europe (Germany, UK, France) | $1,500-$3,000 | Cross-border rollouts, tax localisation, mature market |
| Eastern Europe / nearshore | $700-$1,400 | Lower costs, nearshore delivery |
| Asia-Pacific (Singapore, Australia) | $1,200-$2,500 | Mixed market, mature capability |
| GCC (UAE, KSA) | $1,200-$2,000 | Visa costs, Arabic-language needs, oil and gas demand |
| India (offshore) | $300-$700 | Offshore execution |
| Latin America (Brazil, Mexico) | $700-$1,400 | Tax engine integration, nearshore for US |

These are partner rates billed to the client for senior specialists, not salaries. Blended programme rates usually come out 30-50% below the senior onshore rate because most programmes mix onshore architects with offshore delivery.

Cheaper is not automatically better. A client who chose the lowest-cost implementation option ended up spending 30% more fixing issues a more experienced team would have avoided. Day rate is the smallest variable in total programme cost.

## How RISE, SAP GROW and clean core change the cost structure

The on-premise licence-plus-maintenance model is no longer the default for new SAP programmes.

### RISE with SAP moves spend from capex to subscription

RISE packages SAP Cloud ERP Private (the private edition of S/4HANA) with SAP-run infrastructure and operations and a set of transformation tools. The shape of the business case changes:

1. No upfront perpetual licence, so year one costs less than on-premise
2. No infrastructure capex; SAP holds the hyperscaler relationship
3. The annual subscription replaces annual maintenance
4. The commitment is multi-year, so the full term has to be visible in the model

The subscription is usually treated as an operating expense, which changes how the spend hits the P&L. More on that in the capitalisation section below.

### SAP GROW narrows the cost envelope

SAP GROW, the package for mid-sized companies on SAP Cloud ERP (the public edition), runs on standardised scope. Total cost typically runs $2M-$6M including subscription and delivery services. Because the public edition only allows extensions through released interfaces, the "scope creep adds 30%" pattern that hits RISE and on-premise programmes is less of a risk.

### Clean core makes customisation cost visible

SAP's clean core guidance now grades every extension from A (released APIs only) to D (not clean). Extensions built side by side on SAP BTP or in-system with ABAP Cloud show up as an explicit cost line, rather than disappearing into the implementation services estimate and resurfacing at the next upgrade.

That visibility is useful. The discipline it forces (is this extension really needed?) usually saves more than the extension work costs.

### AI tooling

Some Joule capabilities come with SAP cloud subscriptions, and RISE now includes activation services for a small number of Joule assistants. Premium AI capabilities are priced separately. Budget for them explicitly and ask for the productivity assumption behind any partner rate card that claims AI savings.

### On-premise compared with cloud

| Cost category | SAP ECC (legacy) | S/4HANA on-premise | S/4HANA cloud (RISE / SAP GROW) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Licence fees | $500K-$2M (perpetual) | $1M-$5M (perpetual) | Replaced by subscription |
| Annual subscription | n/a | n/a | $500K-$5M/year (scales with FUE) |
| Infrastructure | $300K-$1M | $400K-$2M | Included in subscription |
| Implementation services | $5M-$20M | $5M-$20M | $4M-$15M |
| Customisation | $1M-$5M | $1M-$4M | $500K-$2M |
| Annual maintenance | 20-22% of licence | 20-22% of licence | Replaced by subscription |
| 5-year TCO (enterprise) | $25M-$70M | $25M-$70M | $30M-$80M+ |

Migration from ECC to S/4HANA typically costs 40-60% of the original implementation. A company that spent $20M on ECC should plan $8M-$12M for the move, covering data restructuring, custom code remediation and new functionality. Remember the deadline driving it: [ECC mainstream maintenance ends on 31 December 2027](https://news.sap.com/2020/02/sap-s4hana-maintenance-2040-clarity-choice-sap-business-suite-7/), with paid extended maintenance to the end of 2030.

Timelines: small companies on SAP GROW 4-6 months, mid-market 9-14 months, enterprise on RISE 12-24+ months.

## The budget formula

Use this before detailed scoping. It is crude on purpose.

### On-premise programmes

1. Base software licence cost
2. Multiply by 1.5-2.5 for implementation services (the multiplier reflects complexity)
3. Add hardware or infrastructure
4. Add 15-25% contingency on the total

A manufacturing client had 200 users and $400K in licences. High customisation pushed the multiplier to 2.2, so implementation came to $880K. Plus $150K hardware and $200K contingency. Total: $1.63M before the project started.

Standard processes, clean data and experienced internal teams push the multiplier toward 1.5. Heavy customisation, messy data and thin internal capability push it to 2.5 or beyond.

### Subscription programmes (RISE / SAP GROW)

1. Annual subscription (FUE count and tier)
2. Multiply by five for the five-year view
3. Add implementation services (typically 1.5-2x the year-one subscription)
4. Add 15-25% contingency

Implementation services are usually 20-30% lower than on-premise because SAP holds the infrastructure work.

## Five ways to bring the number down

1. **Adopt standard processes.** Every customisation costs twice: to build, then at every upgrade. One manufacturing client saved over $800K by adapting its processes to SAP standards rather than customising. It was painful for some departments, but the financial difference was massive.
2. **Phase the rollout.** A retail client saved $1.2M by splitting its rollout into three phases over 18 months instead of doing everything at once. Initial investment dropped 40%, and lessons from phase 1 made the later phases faster.
3. **Pay for expertise, not a low day rate.** A chemical company client switched from a general SAP partner to an industry specialist and spent 20% less overall despite higher hourly rates. Specialists worked faster and made fewer mistakes.
4. **Use global delivery carefully.** Mixing offshore and onshore saves money when the offshore team has strong SAP experience and workstream boundaries are clear. It costs money when they are not. US programmes typically run 30-60% onshore by FTE; GCC programmes 60-70%.
5. **Automate regression testing.** Tools such as Tricentis Tosca or SAP's own test automation pay back on any programme that runs more than two test cycles, and every upgrade after go-live is another cycle.

## What you can capitalise, and what you can't

This matters for how a large programme hits the income statement. The general principle under IFRS and US GAAP: costs of building an asset you control can be capitalised; costs of operating, training and preparing can't. Cloud changes the answer for a big slice of the budget.

| Cost element | On-premise licence | Cloud subscription (RISE / SAP GROW) |
|---|---|---|
| Licence or subscription fees | Capitalise as intangible asset | Expense over the term (service contract) |
| Configuration and build services | Capitalise if directly attributable | IFRS: often expensed, unless they create a separate asset you control. US GAAP: capitalise implementation costs and amortise over the hosting term |
| Internal labour on build | Capitalise if directly attributable | Same split as above |
| Training and change management | Expense | Expense |
| Data cleansing | Expense | Expense |
| Data conversion tools you keep | Capitalise if reusable | Assess case by case |
| Hypercare and support | Expense | Expense |

The IFRS position comes from the IFRS Interpretations Committee's [2021 agenda decision on configuration and customisation costs in cloud computing arrangements](https://www.ifrs.org/news-and-events/updates/ifric/2021/ifric-update-march-2021/). The US GAAP position comes from ASU 2018-15. Under IFRS especially, a RISE business case that assumes on-premise capitalisation can overstate year-one profit materially.

Documentation decides how much you keep. A manufacturing client saved $2.3M in current-year expenses by capitalising properly, because it tracked consultant activities meticulously enough to defend the approach. Another client faced a painful restatement because it couldn't substantiate its capitalisation decisions. Auditors look hard at large capitalised projects. Agree the policy with them before the programme starts, not at year end.

## What actually causes 50% overruns

Four patterns account for most of the large overruns I have reviewed.

### Scope changes without cost control

One retail chain budgeted $5M and finished at $12.3M. Poor requirements gathering led to constant scope changes. Midway through, it found its inventory processes didn't match SAP standard functionality, and extensive customisation followed. It had also underestimated data migration from its legacy systems. Aligning processes to standard in design would have been far cheaper.

### Timelines set by executive pressure

One global manufacturing programme went 143% over budget ($14M against $5.8M planned) and launched 11 months late. The causes: unrealistic timeline pressure from executives, an understaffed internal team, too many modules at once, and a lowest-cost partner without industry expertise.

### Part-time internal people

Programmes that assign part-time representatives instead of dedicated process owners lose days on every decision. The understaffed internal team on that global manufacturing programme was one of the reasons it ran 11 months late.

### Data found late

Data problems discovered in pre-production are the most expensive kind. Profiling should run alongside requirements, not after configuration. Three months of cleanup under live-system pressure is consistently the largest cost-of-delay item on overrun programmes.

Most overrun programmes did not have a planning failure. They had an honesty failure at the planning stage. A CFO who approves a realistic plan with proper contingency spends less than one who approves a hopeful plan with none.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Why do SAP implementations go over budget so often?</summary>

Because the estimate rests on assumptions nobody validated. Scope was documented at surface level, data quality was assumed, and internal availability was optimistic. The quote covers licences and consulting but not the cost of pulling your best people off their day jobs.

Control it by validating assumptions early, holding 15-25% contingency, and reviewing change requests for budget impact every week rather than at month-end.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How much does SAP S/4HANA cost to implement?</summary>

Mid-market S/4HANA programmes typically land between $2M and $10M; mid-sized companies on SAP GROW typically $2M-$6M. Enterprise programmes through RISE run $20M to $80M+, and global multi-region programmes $80M-$300M+.

Senior S/4HANA day rates run $1,800-$3,500 onshore US, $1,200-$2,000 in the GCC, $700-$1,400 nearshore and $300-$700 offshore India.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is cloud SAP cheaper than on-premise?</summary>

In years one and two, usually yes: no hardware, faster deployment, no large upfront licence. Over five to seven years the gap narrows, because on-premise has no recurring licence fee after purchase, only maintenance at around 20-22% of licence.

Cloud wins when you have no infrastructure to reuse, prefer opex, and want SAP to run upgrades. On-premise can win with a long system life, an existing data centre and deep customisation needs, but ECC's 2027 maintenance deadline means you are choosing an S/4HANA path either way.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What are the real hidden costs in an SAP implementation?</summary>

Five that rarely appear in the quote but always appear in the invoice: internal people's time; data cleanup that runs longer than estimated; the productivity dip around go-live; training refreshes as staff turn over; and post-go-live optimisation. Budget 15-20% of implementation cost for the optimisation work in the 6-12 months after go-live.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I estimate an SAP implementation budget?</summary>

On-premise: licence cost, multiplied by 1.5 to 2.5 for implementation, plus infrastructure, plus 15-25% contingency. Subscription: five years of subscription, plus implementation services at 1.5-2x the year-one subscription, plus 15-25% contingency.

The worked example above came to $1.63M before detailed scoping.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can you capitalise RISE with SAP implementation costs?</summary>

Partly, and it depends on your accounting framework. The subscription itself is normally expensed. Under IFRS, configuration and customisation services in a SaaS arrangement are often expensed too, unless they create a separate asset you control. Under US GAAP (ASU 2018-15), implementation costs are capitalised and amortised over the hosting term.

Agree the treatment with your auditor before you finalise the business case. It changes the year-one P&L more than most programme teams expect.

</details>
