Success in SAP implementations depends on one thing more than any other: the team. I've seen well-funded projects with experienced vendors fail because key roles were missing or split across people who had other jobs to do. I've also seen underfunded projects succeed because the right people were in the room, fully committed, with clear accountability.

A global retailer I worked with had budget, leadership support, and SAP as their chosen ERP. Their implementation team was a problem from week one. Key roles were missing. Nobody owned critical decisions. Communication ran in all directions without landing anywhere. Deadlines slipped. Costs went up. Confidence in the project collapsed.

That pattern repeats constantly. Companies focus on software selection and ignore the people running the project. The best technology doesn't save a poorly structured team.

## The eight core roles

Every SAP implementation needs these roles filled. The title doesn't matter as much as the accountability.

| Role | Primary accountability | What breaks without it |
|---|---|---|
| Executive Sponsor | Strategic decisions, funding, escalation authority | Drift, scope fights, no one breaks ties |
| Project Manager | Timeline, budget, cross-team coordination | Delays, unresolved blockers, cost overruns |
| Functional Consultants / SMEs | Business process design, module configuration | Wrong configuration, post-go-live workarounds |
| IT Team | Integration, customization, security, performance | Technical debt, broken interfaces, system instability |
| Data Migration Lead | Data quality, load sequencing, cutover accuracy | Unusable data, failed go-live, months of cleanup |
| Change Management Lead | Training, adoption, stakeholder communication | User resistance, parallel Excel workstreams |
| Implementation Partner | Architecture, integration design, delivery oversight | Overbuilding, integration failures, vendor misalignment |
| ERP Program Advisor | Independent oversight, risk identification, executive alignment | Decisions made in isolation, avoidable mistakes |

### Executive sponsor

The sponsor isn't just a name on a steering deck. They make decisions that no one else can. Budget approvals, scope changes, resource commitments that cross departmental lines, when this role is ceremonial, projects drift.

I worked with a sponsor who thought their job ended at go-live. Within six weeks of launch, adoption was flat, the backlog was growing, and no one had authority to approve the enhancements users needed. The project had delivered technically. Nobody was using it.

The most effective sponsors I've worked with stay involved through hypercare. They attend monthly steering sessions after go-live. They make decisions that look small but unblock things that have been stuck for weeks.

### Project manager

The PM runs the day-to-day. Timeline, risk log, team coordination, stakeholder updates. On a large SAP programme, this is a full-time role that requires someone who has done it before.

On one programme, the lead developer quit with eight weeks to go. The project stopped for two weeks while the PM scrambled to find a replacement and catch up. That two-week gap turned into a six-week delay by go-live.

I've also seen the opposite problem: a 30-person team with a PM who couldn't delegate. Every decision came through one person. The programme ran 12 months, then 18, then 22. Nobody could identify the critical path because everything was in one person's head.

### Functional consultants and subject matter experts

These are the people who translate business operations into SAP configuration. They need to understand both: the business deeply enough to challenge bad processes and SAP well enough to know what's possible.

On a manufacturing programme, the functional leads were pulled back to the factory floor two months into blueprinting. Configuration proceeded with assumptions about how production planning worked. The assumptions were wrong. The configuration had to be redone in testing, at significant cost, under time pressure.

SMEs who are half-committed to the project always leave gaps. Either the project gets their attention or it gets their name on a sign-off sheet. Those aren't the same thing.

### IT team

The IT team handles the technical foundation. ABAP development, Basis, security, integration architecture, performance. In S/4HANA implementations, this also means managing the clean core discipline: keeping custom code off the core and extensions routed through BTP.

What most teams underestimate is integration. Every connection between SAP and an external system needs to be designed, built, tested, and owned. I've seen interfaces scoped in week one, designed in week eight, and broken in UAT because nobody mapped the data flows carefully. The IT team needs to be in blueprinting sessions, not brought in after decisions have been made.

### Data migration lead

This role gets assigned late and given too little resource. By the time data problems surface, the programme is already under schedule pressure.

A global media company I worked with had 40% of customer records with duplicate or incomplete data. Nobody had run a proper assessment before the migration workstream started. The go-live slipped by three months while the data was cleaned under a live system. That cleanup cost more than a proper pre-migration cleanse would have.

On a financial services project, the migration lead found a critical discrepancy in the general ledger structure two weeks before go-live. It was fixable, but only because she'd been running parallel reconciliations for months. That kind of early detection doesn't happen if the role is filled by someone who also has three other workstreams.

### Change management lead

This is the most consistently underresourced role on SAP programmes. I've seen enterprise implementations assign one OCM person to a team of 50. That's not change management; it's a training function with no influence.

I watched a technically perfect implementation fail because users hated it. The system was configured correctly. The process design was sound. But the people who had to use it daily hadn't been involved in the design. They didn't understand why things had changed, and found their own ways around the new system within two weeks of go-live.

On one airline programme, the change management lead identified warehouse resistance early and spent six weeks with a specific team working through their concerns. Those users became advocates. Every other site adoption followed the same approach. Go-live was clean.

The minimum for an enterprise SAP programme is two dedicated change management people. One person cannot cover training design, stakeholder communication, resistance management, and adoption tracking simultaneously.

### ERP program advisor

An independent advisor is not the same as the implementation partner. Their job is oversight and course correction. That means checking whether the direction still makes sense and identifying risks the delivery team is too close to see. It also means bridging the gap between what executives think is happening and what's actually happening.

I've played this role for clients who had strong implementation teams but no independent voice in the room. On one manufacturing programme, the client was close to implementing modules with no clear business case. The implementation partner was scoping to the requirements they'd been given. Nobody had linked the module selection back to the company's growth strategy. That conversation had to happen at board level before design started, not after.

The advisor also provides pattern recognition across projects. When a client's steering committee had spent three sessions debating a data ownership question, I recognized the pattern. Not from that project, but from six others where the same debate ran for months. The resolution was a governance document that should have been in place before blueprinting. We had it done in a week.

## Team structure by company size

| Role | Small enterprise | Mid-market | Large enterprise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Senior director | CIO or CFO | C-suite with SteerCo |
| Project manager | 1 (full-time) | 1-2 (full-time) | Programme manager + workstream PMs |
| Functional leads | 1-2 per module | Dedicated per module | Multiple per module |
| IT team | 2-3 (shared) | 4-6 (dedicated) | 8+ specialists |

| Change management | 1 (minimum) | 2 (minimum) | 3-5 dedicated |
| Implementation partner | 5-10 consultants | 15-25 consultants | 30+ with programme director |

## Emotional intelligence matters more than most teams admit

Technical skills get projects built. Emotional intelligence determines whether people use what's been built.

A manufacturing client had a warehouse manager who smiled in every steering meeting but was quietly undermining the project with his team. The change management lead spotted the pattern from his behaviour in smaller working sessions: delayed responses, skeptical framing, off-hand comments that reached the floor. She addressed it directly, involved him in the design of his team's new workflows, and turned him into one of the project's strongest advocates. That took six weeks. Discovering the problem at go-live would have taken six months to fix.

I also watched a technically brilliant project manager lose a go-live timeline because he couldn't adapt his communication to different audiences. His steering updates were detailed and accurate. His CFO stopped reading them. His warehouse leads couldn't parse them. Nobody was aligned. The system launched with three times the support tickets expected in week one.

## Best practices

| Practice | Why it matters | What happens without it |
|---|---|---|
| Build the team before selecting software | Team ownership from the start prevents module decisions nobody can support | Bought modules the team couldn't maintain; 6 months of chaos followed |
| Dedicate 100%; no split roles | Part-time means day job takes priority when pressure arrives | Critical configuration delayed for weeks because someone was "too busy" |
| Co-locate where possible | Problems solved in minutes instead of days of back-and-forth email | Manufacturing client saved weeks by sharing a space three days a week |
| Define escalation paths early | Blockers need to move quickly; ambiguity turns a day's problem into a week's delay | Retail client used a one-page document; resolved every escalation in 48 hours |
| Document decisions and rationale | Team changes during long programmes; context disappears with people | New executives joined mid-project and spent weeks re-opening closed decisions |
| Celebrate milestones, not just go-live | SAP projects are 12-24 months; morale needs sustaining | Manufacturing client held monthly recognition events across an 18-month programme |

## Employees versus consultants

This question comes up on every programme. The answer is almost always: both.

Employees know the business. They understand the processes, the politics, and the workarounds that nobody documents. A manufacturing team's internal members identified configuration issues that the consulting team had missed entirely, issues that would have broken their month-end close.

But employees often lack SAP implementation experience. A retail client insisted on using all internal staff. Six months in, they were hopelessly behind because their team was learning SAP while implementing it. The learning curve is real.

Consultants bring pattern recognition. They've seen the same configuration decisions made well and badly across multiple clients. One consultant on a pharma programme immediately identified a data migration approach that would have corrupted their inventory balances at go-live. That intervention justified the entire consulting cost.

The risk with consultants is knowledge transfer. Companies that rely too heavily on them often struggle post-go-live. One client hemorrhaged consulting fees for two years after launch because their internal team couldn't maintain what had been built.

The model that works consistently: pair employees with consultants in shadow roles, each consultant has an internal counterpart who will own that area post-go-live. The consultant delivers. The internal person learns. Knowledge stays when the consultants leave.

## Structuring the SAP CoE for the long term

The mistake companies make after go-live is disbanding the implementation team. That's exactly when the Centre of Excellence needs to take over.

Your CoE manages the system after delivery: enhancements, upgrades, governance, training for new users, and the ongoing alignment between SAP configuration and how the business is actually operating.

Start planning it during implementation. A manufacturing client who ignored this had their key configuration experts leave within three months of go-live. Nobody knew how to maintain what had been built. The system started degrading immediately.

| CoE role | Primary responsibility |
|---|---|
| CoE Director | SAP strategy, alignment with business goals, CoE operations |
| Solution Architect | System architecture, integration design, clean core governance |
| Functional Consultants | Module optimization, process improvement, business alignment |
| Technical Consultants | ABAP, Basis, performance, security |
| Change Management and Training Lead | User adoption, training programmes, capability uplift |
| Data Governance Lead | Master data quality, data standards, governance enforcement |
| Integration Lead | Middleware, API management, cross-system data flows |
| Support Lead | Post-go-live issue resolution, continuous improvement |

A pharma client assigned module owners who had to approve any change that might affect their area. That governance structure prevented the uncoordinated changes that typically make systems unusable after two to three years.

One client invested 10% of their CoE budget in continuous learning. Three years post-go-live they were implementing capabilities their competitors hadn't reached yet. That's what a functioning CoE looks like.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Why do SAP implementation teams fail even when the plan looks solid?</summary>

Usually because the plan covers the technology and ignores the people. The most common failure patterns I've seen: key roles filled by people with other jobs, SMEs pulled back to operations mid-project, change management treated as a training function rather than a strategic workstream.

A global retailer had everything in place: budget, leadership buy-in, a credible implementation partner. Their team structure was the problem. Nobody owned critical decisions. When blockers appeared, they sat unresolved for weeks because there was no clear escalation path. The project collapsed from coordination failure, not technical failure.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What roles are non-negotiable on any SAP implementation?</summary>

Six roles that must be filled with dedicated, accountable people: Executive Sponsor, Project Manager, at least one Functional Lead per major module, an IT Lead, a Data Migration Lead, and a Change Management Lead. Remove any of these and you have a gap that will surface at the worst possible time: usually in the final weeks before go-live.

The change management role is the most commonly under-resourced. One person cannot cover stakeholder communication, training design, resistance management, and adoption tracking on an enterprise programme. Two dedicated people is the minimum. I've seen single-OCM-resource setups fail on programmes of 200 users.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How should you approach the employees versus consultants question?</summary>

The answer is almost never one or the other. Employees bring business context that consultants can't replicate quickly. Consultants bring implementation pattern recognition that employees typically don't have. Both matter.

The model that works: shadow roles, each consultant is paired with an internal counterpart who will own that area post-go-live. The consultant delivers the implementation work. The internal person learns alongside them, when the consultants leave, the knowledge stays. Companies that skip this step often spend years paying for post-go-live support that should have been handled internally.

</details>

<details>
<summary>When should you start building the SAP CoE?</summary>

During implementation, not after. The best CoE members are typically your strongest implementation team contributors. If you wait until go-live to think about it, those people will move on before you've identified them.

A manufacturing client who planned their CoE from month three of implementation had a smooth handover. A client who ignored it lost their key configuration experts within three months of go-live. Nobody could maintain what had been built. The system degraded within a year. The cost to recover exceeded the original CoE budget they'd declined to fund.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does SAP team structure look like by company size?</summary>

For small enterprises (under $100M revenue): one full-time PM, one to two functional leads per module, two to three IT staff, one data migration lead, and one change management person. Lean, but fully dedicated.

For mid-market ($100M-$1B): dedicated functional leads per module, four to six IT specialists, a data migration workstream with analysts, and at least two change management people. The implementation partner typically brings 15 to 25 consultants.

For large enterprise ($1B+): a programme manager with workstream PMs, multiple functional leads per module, eight-plus IT specialists across ABAP, Basis, security, and integration, a dedicated data migration workstream, and three to five change management people. The partner team typically runs 30-plus with their own programme director.

</details>
