## What I do

I'm Noel. Senior ERP and AI advisor. 25 years in this work, mostly on the
hard side of it. CIMA. AICPA. Masters in Accounting. The qualifications
matter less than the pattern they signal: I came at ERP through the finance
close, not through configuration.

The headline number is $700M+ in transformations delivered across
EDGE Group, Etihad Airways, ADNOC, PIF entities, DXC, and the UAE
Government. The work itself splits three ways:

1. **Programme recovery.** Late, over budget, or technically broken
   implementations. I come in, separate what's real from what's political,
   and rebuild the plan.
2. **ECC to S/4HANA and cloud ERP.** Migration design, cutover,
   Finance and Supply Chain transformation, data quality.
3. **AI on ERP data.** Governance, agentic workflows on BTP, models
   that sit on SAP, Oracle, or Microsoft foundations without
   hallucinating the ledger.

I don't sell licences. I don't resell implementation services. My
paycheck doesn't depend on Activate hours or BTP subscriptions. That
independence is the whole point.

## What I believe

These are the five opinions I'll defend in print. Real positions, not
soft takes. Each one has consequences for how I work.

### 1. Start with processes, not systems.
Most ERP failures begin in the wrong order. Buy the system, then try
to fit your business into it. The conversation should run the other
way. Map your processes first. Decide what's broken, what's worth
keeping, what needs to change. Then talk about systems. Every
greenfield-versus-brownfield debate I've watched go sideways started
without this step.

### 2. Your SI shouldn't be the one writing your business case.
You need an expert who isn't selling the implementation to write the
case for it. That's how $40M programmes become $90M without anyone
noticing. The SI's incentive is to start. Yours is to finish. Different
jobs.

### 3. AI on ERP is real now, and it's moving fast.
Joule is growing by the day. Agentic use cases on BTP are no longer
slideware. If your last look at AI-on-SAP was 12 months ago, look
again. The board-deck version is over. The shipping version is here.
Ignore it and you're on the wrong side of the next two years.

### 4. The cheapest consultant is the most expensive one.
Day rate is the smallest variable in total programme cost. A senior
advisor at $2,500/day who saves you a six-month overrun is cheaper
than a $900/day team that doesn't. Most CFOs work this out only after
the second post-mortem.

### 5. Walk if a vendor proposes a three-year roadmap before understanding your close.
The finance close is where the real complexity lives. Vendors who
skip it are selling a roadmap, not solving a problem. Anyone serious
wants to see your close cycle, your reconciliations, and your manual
workarounds before they propose anything.

## Programmes that taught me how this actually goes

Six moments from the last few years. These are the calls that
shaped how I work now.

### The 2am call from Dubai

A Supply Chain Manager I'd worked with before phoned me at 2am.
I was half asleep. His SAP system had crashed and his warehouse team
were writing orders on paper. In 2023.

The consultant before me had set it up wrong. I'm still not sure how
they managed it. Two weeks of effectively starting over. The CEO
aged five years in that fortnight. The system works now. The lesson
was about who you let near your core build, not about SAP.

### The retail chain restarting every Friday

I walked into a retail office last year. Their inventory system was
so old they had to restart it every Friday or it would crash on its
own by Monday. Not exaggerating. The owner pulled out an Excel sheet
where he manually tracked stock across 50 stores. I think I made a
face, because he started defending it before I'd said a word.

His competition was running machine-learning forecasts while he was
counting boxes. We got him sorted. He waited about three years longer
than he should have.

### The S/4HANA telecom programme

Public-sector telecom. They needed better financial reporting. Every
small decision required a committee meeting. What takes two weeks in
private sector took three months here. I complained about it to my
wife every night for a quarter.

But when we finished, they had something that worked properly. Not
a Band-Aid. A real reporting platform. Government programmes test
your patience and pay you back in durability.

### The aviation data warehouse

Same pattern. Slow start. Heavy governance. Many stakeholders. The
final architecture was cleaner than anything I'd have negotiated
through in the private sector at the same scale. There's a version
of public-sector work where the constraints produce better engineering
because nobody can shortcut their way past them.

### The Riyadh retail AI inventory project

Retail chain in Riyadh. Their inventory was a mess. We built something
that learns from sales patterns and adjusts stock automatically. Still
has quirks. Beats having a buyer guess what to reorder every week.
This is what I mean about AI on ERP: it earns its keep when the
underlying master data is clean enough to trust.

### The government chatbot that actually helped

A government agency drowning in citizen complaints. We built triage
that handles the simple cases and routes the complicated ones to
real people. The staff loved it. They could solve actual problems
instead of answering the same five questions for the hundredth time
that morning. The point of AI here wasn't to remove humans. It was
to stop wasting their time on work that didn't need a human.

## Where I stand on AI in ERP

AI on broken data is worse than no AI. Get the foundation right first.
Clean masters. Reconciled ledgers. Documented processes. Then bring
in models. Most of what's currently sold as "AI transformation" is
model-first. That's backwards.

When I bring AI into an engagement, the order is: data quality and
governance first, then targeted agentic workflows on the highest-pain
process, then broader rollout. Most clients want to skip steps one
and two. The ones who let me run the order get to step three. The
ones who don't, don't.

## Public sector versus private sector

I work across both. They're different animals, honestly.

Private sector moves fast. CEO makes a decision Monday, the project
starts the following month. Government is the opposite. I once waited
six months for security clearance to look at an existing system.

Both have their upside. Private companies can change direction
quickly. Government programmes, once moving, usually have actual
budgets behind them. Nobody's asking you to deliver a transformation
on petty cash.

The two failure modes are different too:

1. **Private sector failures are usually pace failures.** Pushed too
   fast, skipped the close discovery, broke the cutover.
2. **Public sector failures are usually governance failures.** Too
   many committees, too many sign-offs, scope frozen in concrete
   before anyone validated it against operations.

The fix is the same in both cases. Get the right senior person in
the room early. Keep them there for the duration.
